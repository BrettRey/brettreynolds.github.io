CG-0005 · CGEL Ch. 5 §3.1, p. 336

Individuation in mass uses

Supported Supported means that the tested distinction survives the stated challenge.

CGEL’s claim

The chapter distinguishes heterogeneous aggregates from homogeneous substances.

Paraphrase of the printed text; quotation marks mark short excerpts.

Finding and limits

Units used for judgment need not be count nouns. This limits the converse of the atomicity argument.

Evidence

People can compare quantities of furniture by counting individual items, although furniture has non-count syntax.

  • Research sourceSusan Rothstein (2010). Counting and the mass/count distinction. Journal of Semantics 27(3), 343–397.
    CGEL passage
    §3.1, p. 336. The chapter distinguishes heterogeneous aggregates from homogeneous substances.
    Example, contrast or countermodel
    Rothstein contrasts furniture, which denotes individual units and is “naturally atomic and nonhomogeneous” but non-count, with fence, which is count but homogeneous and not naturally atomic.
    What it establishes
    Published precedent for a non-count noun whose denotation consists of individual units, the converse limit recorded here. It agrees with CGEL’s separation of heterogeneous aggregates from homogeneous substances.
    Locator
    §3.3, p. 356.
  • Research sourceDavid Barner & Jesse Snedeker (2005). Quantity judgments and individuation: Evidence that mass nouns count. Cognition 97(1), 41–66.
    CGEL passage
    §3.1, p. 336. The chapter distinguishes heterogeneous aggregates from homogeneous substances.
    Example, contrast or countermodel
    Participants could judge the quantity of a non-count noun for discrete objects, such as furniture, by the number of objects, even when the competing set contained larger objects.
    What it establishes
    Number-based judgments can accompany non-count syntax. This bears directly on the converse of the proposed count-noun condition.
    Locator
    Experiments comparing quantity judgments for object-mass, count and substance nouns.
