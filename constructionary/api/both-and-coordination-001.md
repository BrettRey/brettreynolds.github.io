## Meaning 1

Explicitly presents the first two coordinates as jointly included.

- *We need [both a permit and an inspection].*: Constructed illustration.

## Related constructions

- A kind of: Coordination (`coordination-001`)
- Compare: Correlative *either … or* (`either-or-coordination-001`)

## Sources

- Huddleston, Rodney & Geoffrey K. Pullum (2002), The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language. Cambridge: CUP, Ch. 15 §2.3 (discusses; bears on projection)

## Projection

*Both* signals the first coordinate and *and* marks the second. *Both* alone can be an NP quantifier, a use with a different structural relation to the following material.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: *Both a and b*

*Both* before the first item and *and* before the second say outright that two things are included.

In *We need both a permit and an inspection*, *both* comes before the first item and *and* comes before the second. Together they show clearly that we need the permit and the inspection.

*Both* marks the first item, and *and* marks the second. *Both* can also appear alone before a noun phrase, but that's a different use, with a different relation to the words that follow.
