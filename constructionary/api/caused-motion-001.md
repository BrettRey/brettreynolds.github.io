# Caused-Motion Construction

- id: `caused-motion-001`
- pattern: Head:V + Od:NP[theme] + Comp:PP[locative: source/path/goal/direction]
- status: provisional analysis; evidence level: stable
- linguist edition: https://brettreynolds.ca/constructionary/linguist/caused-motion-001/
- licence: CC BY 4.0; snapshot 2026-11-19 (ef810bb)

## Meaning 1

The subject participant causes the direct-object theme to undergo the change of location described by the locative complement

The construction, not necessarily the verb's lexical valency, licenses the theme object and locative complement. In *He sneezed his tooth right across town*, *sneeze* supplies the causal event while the frame supplies the caused-motion relation. In CGEL terms, *across town* is an object-oriented locative complement with a path/goal construal, not an adjunct, a predicative complement, or a second object of the verb (CGEL pp. 257–59, 684–88; Goldberg 2006: 6, 33–35, 94, 98–100).

- *He sneezed [his tooth] [right across town].*: Attested Munsch/Goldberg example with a path/goal construal; *sneeze* does not independently have a three-argument sense
- *She sneezed [the foam] [off the cappuccino].*: Construction-licensed theme and source
- *Pat shoveled [the hay] [into the wagon].*: The verb supplies the means; the frame supplies caused motion and goal

## Constraints

- (syntactic) The NP after the verb is Od; the following PP is an object-oriented locative complement, not a second object and not a locative adjunct.
- (semantic) The external subject must be construable as the source of causal force; volition is not required, as *sneeze* and *knock* show.
- (semantic) The direct-object referent is construed as the moving theme, and the PP supplies source, path, goal, or direction. Static caused-location uses require a separate extension and are not included in this core pattern.
- (semantic) The verbal event must be construable as causally relevant means or manner; productivity is constrained by semantic similarity and statistical pre-emption.

## Projection

Recognizing the Head–Od–locative-complement frame predicts a causer subject, a moving-theme object, an object-oriented change-of-location PP, and a caused-motion reading even when the verb does not lexically license those dependents

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Making something move with a verb

A verb can be followed by a thing and a place phrase, showing that someone caused the thing to move.

In *She sneezed the foam off the cappuccino*, the sneeze makes the foam move. *Sneeze* doesn't have a sense with a moving thing and a path of its own, but the pattern supplies both.

*Pat shoveled the hay into the wagon* works the same way. The verb says how the causing happens, here shoveling, and the pattern adds the movement: someone makes something travel from a place, along a path, toward a direction or to a goal. The subject only has to be the source of the force. It doesn't have to act on purpose, as sneezing shows.

### Teacher

Helps students see that some verbs get an object and a place phrase from the pattern itself, not from the verb alone.

The pattern is a verb, a direct object noun phrase that is the moving theme, and a locative PP complement giving source, path, goal or direction. The subject causes the movement. The construction, not necessarily the verb, licenses the object and the PP: *sneeze* supplies the causing event, the frame supplies the caused motion. The PP is an object-oriented locative complement, not an adjunct, a predicative complement or a second object. The verb must be construable as the means or manner, and productivity is limited by semantic similarity.

### Child

Can a sneeze send a tooth across town?

Some sentences tell who made something move. *Pat shoveled the hay into the wagon*. Pat made the hay move, and the words *into the wagon* tell where the hay went.
