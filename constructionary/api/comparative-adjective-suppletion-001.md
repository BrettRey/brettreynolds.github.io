## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Comparing with a different word

A few adjectives make their comparison forms from a different stem, such as *good*, *better* and *best*.

*Good* has *better* and *best* as its comparison forms, and *bad* has *worse* and *worst*. These are built on different stems, not by adding something to the plain word.

*Better* still works as a comparison form even though it has no added *-er* ending. *This plan is better than the first* compares two plans, just as *a taller one* would compare two heights. *Better than* and *the best* share a pattern with plain *good*.

### Teacher

Useful for explaining why *good* and *bad* compare with different words instead of added endings.

Some adjectives realize grade through different stems: *good* has *better* and *best*, and *bad* has *worse* and *worst*. This is suppletion. *Better* behaves like a comparative even though it has no transparently added *-er* ending: it can introduce a *than*-phrase, as in *better than the first*. *Better than* and *the best* share a paradigm with plain *good* in the relevant readings.
