# Comparative -er

- id: `comparative-er-001`
- pattern: XP[X + -er] where X < {tall, deep, soon, near, few, ...} >
- status: provisional analysis; evidence level: unassessed

## Meaning 1

Denotes scalar superiority

Semantic operation comparing two points on scale denoted by base

- *taller/deeper*: Property comparison
- *sooner/nearer*: Relation comparison
- *fewer*: Quantity comparison

## Meaning 2

Denotes scalar difference

Semantic operation measuring interval between compared points

- *much taller*: Relative interval
- *two inches taller*: Measured interval

## Meaning 3

Denotes comparison basis

Semantic operation establishing reference point

- *taller than Bill*: Explicit reference
- *the taller building*: Contextual reference

## Projection

The same comparative relation can be expressed by a suffix with tall or by more with expensive. The suffix does not freely spread from one gradable adjective to another. Either realization can introduce a than-standard and accept a differential where the adjective allows it.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Comparing with -er

Adding -er to a word like tall says that one thing ranks higher than another on a scale.

Taller, deeper, sooner, nearer and fewer all compare two points on a scale. The -er form can also say by how much, as in much taller or two inches taller, and it can name what is being compared with, as in taller than Bill.

Sometimes the comparison is left to context, as in the taller building. The same idea can come out as more with another adjective, as in more expensive. Which adjectives take -er varies from word to word.

### Teacher

Useful for explaining -er comparatives, the than-phrase, and how a difference like two inches can be added.

A comparative with -er compares two points on the scale named by its base: taller, deeper, sooner, nearer and fewer. It can also express a difference (much taller, two inches taller) and the basis of comparison, whether explicit (taller than Bill) or contextual (the taller building). The same comparative relation can be expressed by a suffix with tall or by more with expensive; the suffix does not freely spread from one gradable adjective to another. Either form can introduce a than-standard and accept a differential where the adjective allows it.

### Child

Which is taller, you or your teacher?

Add -er to words like tall and deep to compare two things. Tall becomes taller. Deep becomes deeper. Then say what you compare it with, like taller than Bill.
