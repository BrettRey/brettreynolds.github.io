## Meaning 1

Uses a fronted complement to link the clause to something evoked in the discourse.

In the muffin example the fronted NP occupies the position interpreted as the object of give. A contrast or a set of alternatives can make the link available without repeating the same words. A fronted adjunct can introduce a new setting with no prior link, and left dislocation instead leaves an overt expression in the nucleus.

- *[A bran muffin I can give you.]*: The server has just said that the bagels are sold out. The muffins are an available alternative.
- *About the forms, [two of them I have already signed].*: Constructed example with an explicit anchor for the preposed object.

## Projection

In its ordinary declarative use the preposed complement needs a discourse link even when the speaker has not mentioned the exact referent. An initial setting adjunct has no corresponding requirement.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Moving a part to the front

Part of a sentence can be moved to the front to tie the sentence to something already in the conversation.

Suppose a server has just said the bagels are sold out. A bran muffin I can give you moves the thing being given to the front, and the muffin is an available alternative to the bagels. In plain order it would come after the verb give.

The moved part needs a link to the conversation. It might have been mentioned, belong to a set that was, or be a contrast with something else. A phrase that just sets the scene can open a sentence without such a link. And the moved part leaves no repeated copy behind.

### Teacher

Useful for explaining why a preposed object needs a discourse link, and how it differs from a fronted setting and from left dislocation.

In complement preposing, a complement is fronted and the clause nucleus has a gap where it would normally appear: A bran muffin I can give you, where the fronted noun phrase is interpreted as the object of give. In its ordinary declarative use, the preposed complement needs a discourse link, even when the exact referent hasn't been mentioned. The link can be repeated mention, a subset, or a contrasting alternative. A fronted adjunct can introduce a new setting with no prior link. Left dislocation differs: it leaves an overt expression in the nucleus.
