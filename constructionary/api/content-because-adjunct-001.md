## Meaning 1

Presents a circumstance as the reason for the event or state asserted.

- *Jo left [because the room was too cold].*: Constructed illustration.

## Related constructions

- A kind of: Preposition Phrase (`pp-001`)
- Compare: Speech-Act or Evidential *because* (`evidential-because-001`)

## Sources

- Huddleston, Rodney & Geoffrey K. Pullum (2002), The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language. Cambridge: CUP, Ch. 8 §§12.1–12.3 (discusses; bears on projection)

## Projection

The cold explains the leaving on the content reading. In the reasoning use of *because*, its clause can instead supply evidence for making the assertion.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: *Because* giving the reason for an event

A *because*-part can give the reason that something happened or was the case.

In *Jo left because the room was too cold*, the *because*-part says why Jo left. The cold explains the leaving. This is the content reading: the circumstance is presented as the reason for the event or state asserted.

*Because* has another use, in reasoning. There the *because*-part can give evidence for making the statement, not an explanation for an event.

### Teacher

Useful for separating *because* as a reason for an event from *because* as evidence for a claim.

In the content use, a PP headed by *because* with a finite clause as its complement modifies the predication and presents a circumstance as the reason for the event or state asserted. In *Jo left because the room was too cold*, the cold explains the leaving. In the reasoning use, the *because*-clause can instead supply evidence for making the assertion. This dictionary covers the content reading; the evidential use has its own entry.
