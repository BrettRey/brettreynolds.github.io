## Meaning 1

Supplies the propositional content associated with a noun such as claim or fact.

- *The [claim that Jo knew the code] surprised us.*: Constructed illustration.
- *The claim [that Jo rejected] surprised us.*: Constructed comparison. Integrated relative with a missing object corresponding to claim.

## Related constructions

## Projection

The embedded knew the code has no gap linked to claim. In the claim that Jo rejected, the matching string that Jo rejected instead has an object gap.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Nouns that come with a that-clause

A noun such as claim or fact can be followed by a clause that spells out its content.

In The claim that Jo knew the code surprised us, the that-clause says what the claim was: Jo knew the code. Nothing in that clause is missing or points back to claim.

Compare The claim that Jo rejected surprised us. Here Jo rejected the claim, so the clause is missing something, the thing rejected, and that something is the claim. The rumour that Jo had left gives the content of the rumour. The rumour that Jo spread works like the second claim sentence: it has a missing object that is the rumour.

### Teacher

Helps students separate a noun's content clause from a relative clause that looks the same.

A noun such as claim or fact can take a finite content clause that supplies the propositional content associated with it: the claim that Jo knew the code. The embedded clause (Jo knew the code) is complete and has no gap linked to claim. In the claim that Jo rejected, the string that Jo rejected, which opens like the content clause above, is an integrated relative clause with an object gap corresponding to claim. So a clause after a noun can be a content clause or a relative clause, and the difference is whether the clause has a gap linked to the noun.
