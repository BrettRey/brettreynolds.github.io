## Meaning 1

Presents propositional content as part of a larger construction.

*That* can be optional after a verb such as *say* or in subject extraposition. It is required in ordinary fully expressed initial clausal subjects. Whether the proposition is reported, presupposed, or questioned depends on the matrix construction.

- *[That they were lying] is now quite obvious.*: Initial clausal subject requires *that* in this example.
- *I believe [(that) they were lying].*: Constructed post-verbal complement. The bracketed *that* itself can be omitted.

## Projection

The same declarative content requires *that* in a canonical initial subject and can omit it in the corresponding extraposed subject.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: When *that* can be left out

The part of a sentence that gives what someone believes or says can be introduced by *that*, which is sometimes optional and sometimes required.

In *I believe they were lying*, the part *they were lying* gives the content of the belief, inside a larger sentence. The word *that* can introduce it: *I believe that they were lying*. After a verb like *believe*, *that* can be left out.

It isn't always optional. When that part comes first as the subject of an ordinary, fully expressed sentence, as in *That they were lying is now quite obvious*, the sentence requires *that*. Whether the content is reported, taken for granted or questioned depends on the larger sentence around it. This dictionary treats the analysis as provisional.

### Teacher

Helps explain when *that* is optional or required before a declarative content clause.

A declarative content clause is a subordinate clause, optionally introduced by *that*, that presents propositional content as part of a larger construction. *That* can be optional after a verb such as *say* or *believe*, and in subject extraposition. It is required in ordinary fully expressed initial clausal subjects: *That they were lying is now quite obvious*. Whether the proposition is reported, presupposed or questioned depends on the matrix construction. The subordinate clause preserves declarative order. Don't confuse it with a relative clause: in *Jo said that Kai read it* the content clause has its own object, while in *the book that Kai read* the relative links an object gap to *book*.
