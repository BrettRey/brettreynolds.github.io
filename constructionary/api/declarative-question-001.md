## Meaning 1

A declarative Clause used as a strongly same-polarity-biased question through question prosody

Positive declarative questions are biased toward a positive answer and negative declarative questions toward a negative answer. The expected answer therefore has the same propositional content as the question, and confirmatory responses such as *That's right*, *Exactly*, and *Quite so* are especially natural. Question is the semantic category applied to a syntactically declarative Clause, with question intonation marking the combination; the host does not change clause type. It is not an echo question, since it need not repeat or query the wording of a prior stimulus (CGEL pp. 879–83).

- *[They've finished?]*: Positive declarative question biased toward confirmation that they have finished
- *[They haven't finished?]*: Negative declarative question biased toward confirmation that they have not finished
- *[And the manager has been informed, of course?]*: Confidence marker reinforces the positive bias while remaining outside question scope
- *[There isn't any chance of her changing her mind, I take it?]*: Natural confirmation-seeking declarative question with negative polarity

## Constraints

- (syntactic) The Clause retains declarative syntax and is headed by VP; there is no clause-type-triggered subject–auxiliary inversion.
- (structural) Question intonation in speech, or corresponding question punctuation in writing, supplies the question interpretation without changing declarative clause type.
- (semantic) Question is a semantic classification of the whole form–interpretation pairing, while declarative remains the syntactic type of the headed host Clause.
- (pragmatic) The construction is strongly epistemically biased toward the same-polarity answer and is infelicitous as a neutral request between equally open positive and negative alternatives.
- (pragmatic) No prior utterance is required. If the form instead queries whether particular wording was said or heard, the independent echo-question construction is involved.

## Projection

Declarative order plus question prosody predicts strong same-polarity bias, confirmation-seeking function, and compatibility with responses such as *That's right* or *Exactly*, in contrast to a neutral closed interrogative

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Asking by tone of voice

A statement-shaped sentence said with a questioning tone can ask for confirmation of something the speaker already expects.

*They've finished?* has the word order of a statement. In speech, the questioning tone turns it into a question. The speaker expects the answer yes, and a reply like *That's right* fits naturally.

The expectation matches the sentence. A positive one, *They've finished?*, expects a positive answer, and a negative one, *They haven't finished?*, expects a negative answer. This isn't the neutral question of *Have they finished?*, which leaves the answer more open. In writing, the question mark does the work of the tone.

It's a closed question strongly biased toward the answer with the same propositional content and polarity as the declarative: positive toward positive, negative toward negative. ### Child

How can you ask a question without changing any words?

You can say a sentence in a questioning voice. *They've finished?* You think they have, and you want to check. The other person can answer, "*That's right.*"
