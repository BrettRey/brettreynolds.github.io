## Meaning 1

Denotes relative scale position

Semantic features encoding comparative, superlative and equative scale relations

- *taller/shorter than; more/less expensive than*: Bidirectional comparison
- *tallest/shortest; most/least expensive*: Scale endpoints
- *as tall as*: Scale equality

## Meaning 2

Denotes scale intervals

Semantic features encoding degree of difference between scale points

- *much taller/slightly shorter*: Relative intervals
- *two feet taller/three times less*: Measured intervals

## Meaning 3

Denotes comparison classes

Semantic features encoding reference sets for comparison

- *taller than average/most expensive of all*: Set comparison
- *as tall as expected/least expensive option*: Standard comparison

## Projection

The same dimension can have an explicit differential in *two centimetres wider* and a simple comparative in *wider*. An equality comparison can instead supply a standard with *as wide as*. The constructions test comparison structure separately from the particular scale chosen.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Comparing things on a scale

English has a few ways to say where something sits on a scale, such as *taller than*, *the tallest* and *as tall as*.

Words like *tall*, *expensive* or *short* describe a scale, and English has patterns for placing things on it. *Taller than* and *more expensive than* compare two things. *The tallest* and *the most expensive* pick out the end of the scale. *As tall as* says two things are level.

The patterns can also say how big the difference is: *much taller*, *two feet taller*. And they can compare against a group or a standard, as in *taller than average* or *most expensive of all*. This dictionary treats the analysis as provisional.

### Teacher

Helps explain comparatives, superlatives and equatives as ways of placing something on a scale.

Degree constructions denote relative scale position. The comparative (*-er* or *more*/*less*: *taller*, *more expensive*), the superlative (*-est* or *most*/*least*: *tallest*, *most expensive*) and the equative (*as tall as*) encode scale relations. Measure and differential phrases denote intervals (*much taller*, *two feet taller*), and comparison classes supply a reference set or standard (*taller than average*, *most expensive of all*). The patterns apply to gradable adjectives and adverbs. This dictionary notes that degree frames are degraded with absolute adjectives and lists *unique* and *dead* as boundary items. The analysis is provisional.

### Child

How do you say one thing is more than another?

We compare things with words like *taller*, *tallest* and *as tall as*. *Taller than* compares two things. *The tallest in the class* means no one in the class is taller. *As tall as* says they match.
