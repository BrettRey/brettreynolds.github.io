# Degree-Adjective-Indefinite (Big Mess Construction)

- status: provisional analysis; evidence level: unassessed
- linguist edition: https://brettreynolds.ca/constructionary/linguist/degree-adjective-indefinite-001/

## Meaning 1

Degree word and adjective precede the indefinite article, expressing degree or extent of the adjectival property

A degree-marked adjective phrase, including certain comparatives, precedes the indefinite article, inverting the normal order (cf. "a big mess" → "how big a mess"). Only compatible with indefinite "a(n)", not definite "the" (*"how big the mess"). The "how" variant can function as an interrogative phrase, fronting to clause-initial position. Variants differ in register. An intervening of is especially familiar in North American varieties and is more available with some degree phrases than others.

- *[How big a mess] was it?*: Interrogative; degree phrase fronted
- *He's not [that good a teacher].*: Degree word \"that\"; negative context common
- *It was [too big a problem] to ignore.*: Degree word \"too\" with infinitival complement
- *She's [so good a student] that she skipped a year.*: Degree word \"so\" with result clause
- *It's not [as big a deal] as you think.*: Comparative \"as...as\" construction
- *I've never seen [so big a crowd].*: "So" variant; more literary than other degree words
- *It's not [that big of a deal].*: Of-insertion variant; common in North American English
- *[more interesting a job] than I thought*: Published comparison. The fronted adjective phrase is comparative.

## Constraints

- (syntactic) Eligible phrases include those with how, so, too, that and as and certain comparatives. Ordinary very uses a different word order.
- (syntactic) Article must be indefinite a(n); definite the is ungrammatical
- (syntactic) "How" variant can be interrogative, fronting to clause-initial position
- (pragmatic) Acceptability and register depend on the degree phrase and the availability of the intervening of variant.

## Projection

An eligible degree phrase can precede a in too serious a problem. Ordinary very yields a very serious problem. Comparatives are also documented, and acceptability of of varies with the particular degree expression and variety.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: How big a mess, too big a problem

With words like how, too, so, that and as, the adjective can move in front of a or an.

In a big mess, the adjective sits between a and the noun. But after words such as how, so, too, that and as, the adjective moves forward and a follows it. So we say how big a mess, too big a problem, so good a student, not that good a teacher.

The word a or an has to stay: this pattern doesn't work with the. In some varieties you can also add of, as in that big of a deal, though whether of is acceptable depends on the degree word and the variety. The ordinary word very doesn't do this, and takes the usual order: a very serious problem.

### Teacher

Explains inverted order after degree words (how big a mess) and the a(n)-only restriction.

A degree word plus an adjective can precede the indefinite article, reversing the usual order: a big mess becomes how big a mess. Eligible phrases include those with how, so, too, that and as, and certain comparatives (more interesting a job). The article must be indefinite a(n); the doesn't fit, as in how big the mess. The how version can be an interrogative phrase, fronted to the start of the clause. Ordinary very uses a different word order (a very serious problem). An intervening of (that big of a deal) is especially familiar in North American varieties and is more available with some degree words than others. Register differs among the variants.
