## Meaning 1

Introduces an infinitival with a syntactically expressed subject.

- *[For them to leave now] would be awkward.*: Constructed illustration.

## Projection

*Them* is the subject of *leave*, not the object of a purpose preposition in this use. In subject position this infinitival normally requires *for* before its overt subject.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: *For*, then who, then *to*

A phrase made of *for*, a person or thing, and *to* plus a verb can say who would do the action.

In *For them to leave now would be awkward*, the words *for them to leave now* say what would be awkward, and *them* is the one who would leave. Here *for* isn't linking *them* to something as a preposition would. The person doing the leaving is *them*.

When the whole phrase is the subject of a sentence, it normally needs *for* before the person or thing. A pronoun for a person takes its *them*-type form here.

### Teacher

For advanced classes: the *for NP to-VP* pattern, where the NP is the subject of the infinitive.

The *for*-infinitival introduces an infinitival with a syntactically expressed subject: *for*, Subject NP, then a *to*-infinitival VP. A personal pronoun takes accusative form (*for them to leave*). In *For them to leave now would be awkward*, *them* is the subject of *leave*, not the object of a purpose preposition in this use.
