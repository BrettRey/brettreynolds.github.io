## Meaning 1

Specifies cardinality features

Core semantic features relating to quantity/cardinality of referents

- *cat/cats*: Singular vs non-singular reference
- *this/these*: Singular vs non-singular determination

## Meaning 2

Marks countability status

Indicates whether referents are construed as countable units

- *rice/rices*: Non-count vs count construal

## Meaning 3

Encodes the tight/loose hierarchy of count diagnostics

Tight diagnostics (*a*/*an*, low cardinals, distributives) require higher individuation precision than loose diagnostics (*many*, high round numerals, plural agreement).

- *many cattle vs *three cattle*: Quasi-count pattern
- *many folks vs ?three folks*: Boundary item

## Projection

The standard count singular three-hour span contrasts with *three hours is enough*, where a morphologically plural measure can take singular agreement. This rules out deriving all agreement directly from the number of units in the situation.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: One or more than one

Nouns like *cat* change form to show one thing or more than one, and words nearby change to match.

*Cat* and *cats* show one against more than one, and *this* and *these* do the same for the word pointing at it. The verb can follow along too.

The pattern is about more than counting. *Rice* and *rices* show that a word can be treated as something you can count or as an amount you can't, and the choice shows up in the grammar. Some words sit in between: *many cattle* is fine, while *three cattle* isn't. In *three hours is enough*, a plural measure takes a singular verb, so the grammar doesn't just tally the units.

### Teacher

Covers singular and plural marking, agreement, and the count/non-count boundary, including odd cases like *three hours is enough*.

Grammatical number covers singular and plural morphology on nouns and agreement on demonstrative determinatives, verbs and pronouns. It also reflects whether a referent is construed as countable: *rice* against *rices* shows non-count against count construal. This dictionary ranks count diagnostics: the tight ones (*a*/*an*, low cardinals, distributives) need more individuation precision than the loose ones (*many*, high round numerals, plural agreement). Quasi-count nouns show the ranking, as in *many cattle* against *three cattle*. Amount agreement is a further override: *three hours is enough* has a plural measure with singular agreement, so agreement doesn't follow directly from the number of units. This dictionary marks this analysis as provisional.

### Child

How can you tell one cat from more than one cat?
