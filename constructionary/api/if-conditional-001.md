# Open and Remote If-Conditionals

- id: `if-conditional-001`
- pattern: Clause with an adjunct PP[if + finite declarative clause]
- status: provisional analysis; evidence level: unassessed
- linguist edition: https://brettreynolds.ca/constructionary/linguist/if-conditional-001/
- licence: CC BY 4.0; snapshot 2026-11-19 (5c11b28)

## Meaning 1

Sets out a condition and presents a consequence in relation to it.

The open variant treats the condition as an open possibility. The remote variant characteristically combines a preterite or irrealis form in the condition with a remote modal in the consequence. That preterite can be compatible with an explicitly future time. Present or past remotes often suggest that the condition fails, while a future remote can describe an unsettled possibility.

- *[If he takes the later plane tonight he won’t have to rush.]*: Open variant with a future time.
- *[If he took the later plane tonight he wouldn’t have to rush.]*: Remote counterpart. Tonight keeps the condition in the future.

## Projection

A preterite form in a remote if-clause can describe the same future time as the corresponding present in an open conditional. Remoteness can be marked without asserting that the condition is false.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: If this, then that

An if-phrase sets out a condition, and the rest of the sentence says what follows from it.

In If he takes the later plane tonight he won't have to rush, the if-part sets a condition and the rest says what follows. The condition is treated as an open possibility.

A second version changes the wording: If he took the later plane tonight he wouldn't have to rush. Both are about tonight, so the changed wording doesn't point to the past. The second marks the possibility as more remote, but that doesn't mean the condition is false.
