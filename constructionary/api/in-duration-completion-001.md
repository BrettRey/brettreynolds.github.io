# Completion in a Time Span

## Meaning 1

Measures the time to a bounded occurrence or endpoint.

- *Jo sealed the cartons [in fifteen minutes].*: Constructed illustration.
- *Lee will start [in fifteen minutes].*: Constructed comparison. Temporal location reading of the same form.

## Projection

With a completed-event preterite the interval normally measures time to completion. With a prospective reading *in fifteen minutes* can instead locate an event fifteen minutes from now.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Finishing in a stretch of time

A phrase like *in fifteen minutes* can say how long something took, but the same words can also say when something will start.

In *Jo sealed the cartons in fifteen minutes*, the phrase *in fifteen minutes* measures the time up to the point where the job was done. It tells you how long the sealing took.

The same words can do something different. In *Lee will start in fifteen minutes*, they say when the starting will happen, fifteen minutes from now. So one form carries two readings, and the rest of the sentence helps you tell which one you've got.

### Teacher

Helps with *in* plus a time measure, which can mean time taken or time until a future event.

The pattern is a clause with an adjunct of the form *in* plus a noun phrase measuring time. With a completed event in the preterite, the interval normally measures the time to completion, as in *Jo sealed the cartons in fifteen minutes*. With a prospective reading, the same form can instead locate an event fifteen minutes from now, as in *Lee will start in fifteen minutes*. The first measures the time to a bounded occurrence or endpoint. The second gives a temporal location. This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional.
