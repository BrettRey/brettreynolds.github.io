# Intensifying To Death

- id: `intensifying-to-death-001`
- pattern: VP[Head:V + (Od:NP) + to death]
- status: provisional analysis; evidence level: unassessed

## Meaning 1

Intensifies an action or the experience it causes.

In the dog scared me to death, to death conventionally expresses extreme fright. With a killing verb or an appropriate context, the same phrase instead identifies a literal result. A literal outcome and an intensified experience can thus differ without a change in surface order.

- *The alarm [scared me to death], but everyone was fine.*: Constructed context selects the degree interpretation.
- *The fox [bit the hare to death].*: Constructed comparison with a fatal outcome.

## Related constructions

- Compare: Object-Oriented Resultative Construction (`object-resultative-001`): The literal construction presents death as an achieved result.
- Compare: Degree (`degree-001`): The figurative use locates the experience high on an intensity scale.

## Sources

- Perek, Florent & Amanda Patten, English Constructicon. University of Birmingham, records 62–64 (discusses; bears on projection): Distinguishes intensity, stimulating emotion and a literal killing outcome.

## Projection

A later report of survival is compatible with the emotional intensity reading. The same continuation contradicts the literal outcome reading.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Scared to death, but fine

The words to death after a verb can mean extreme feeling, as in scared me to death, or a literal fatal outcome, depending on the verb and the context.

In The alarm scared me to death, but everyone was fine, to death expresses extreme fright. Nobody died. The continuation about everyone being fine fits that reading.

With a killing verb, the same words identify a literal result, as in The fox bit the hare to death. The word order is the same in both. A later report of survival fits the extreme-feeling reading and contradicts the literal one.

### Teacher

Distinguishes figurative to death as an intensifier from literal to death as a fatal result.

The pattern is a verb, optionally an object, and to death. It intensifies an action or the experience it causes. In The dog scared me to death, to death conventionally expresses extreme fright. With a killing verb or an appropriate context, the same phrase identifies a literal result, as in The fox bit the hare to death. The two readings can differ without any change in surface order. A later report of survival is compatible with the emotional intensity reading and contradicts the literal outcome reading, which makes a useful classroom test. This dictionary relates the literal use to object resultatives and the figurative use to degree, and marks itself provisional.
