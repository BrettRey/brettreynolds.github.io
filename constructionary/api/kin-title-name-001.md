# Kin Title with a Personal Name

- id: `kin-title-name-001`
- pattern: NP[Nom[pre-head kin term + personal name]], with no determiner
- status: provisional analysis; evidence level: unassessed
- linguist edition: https://brettreynolds.ca/constructionary/linguist/kin-title-name-001/
- licence: CC BY 4.0; snapshot 2026-11-19 (fa37529)

## Meaning 1

Designates a relative by combining a kin title with that person's name.

Schneider and Homans report that an uncle may be addressed or referred to by uncle plus first name, by first name alone or by uncle alone, and similarly an aunt (1955: 1195). Among their informants the combination was the most frequently employed term for aunts and uncles (p. 1199), and they write that the American system “classes all uncles together and then subdivides members of the class” (p. 1201). CGEL calls pre-head modifiers of personal names such as Queen, Sister, Captain and Dr appellations, expressing the status of the individual, and notes that it is arguable whether they form part of the name (pp. 518–519). Its list of appellations doesn't include kin terms. Treating the kin term here as a modifier of the same kind extends that analysis and isn't itself grounded in CGEL.

- *Well, we are actually leaving for [Grandma Sharon] and [Grandpa Dale] in three days.*: Attested, mother to child. Coordinated NPs as object in a PP; the transcript's pause mark is omitted.
- *I like the ones that [Grandmama Linda] made though.*: Attested, mother. Subject of a relative clause.
- *How is [Grandma Baum]?*: Attested. The speaker is herself a grandmother (participant Kate); a surname follows the kin title.
- *That reminds me of [grampa Charlie]'s story, Julia.*: Attested, mother to child. The NP is subject-determiner in a genitive.
- *Thanks, [Aunt Sarah].*: Constructed vocative.

## Constraints

- (syntactic) The kin term takes no determiner of its own. With a genitive determiner the structure differs: in it's your brother Ben's birthday (CHILDES Peters, 010621a.cha, line 20), brother is head, your is its determiner and Ben is an integrated appositive. CGEL draws the same contrast between architect Norman Foster, where architect is an omissible embellishment, and the architect Norman Foster, where architect is head and the name an appositive dependent (p. 520).
- (semantic) The kin term assigns the bearer to a kin class, and the personal name selects one member of it (Schneider and Homans 1955: 1201).
- (pragmatic) The same relative can be designated in other ways. Schneider and Homans's informants used first name alone as the principal term for an uncle or aunt where there was strong positive or negative affect (1955: 1200). One informant called his father's brother Uncle Bill when speaking with his father, Bill when speaking with the uncle directly and my uncle when telling a friend about him (p. 1196).

## Related constructions

- Overlaps with: Strong Proper Name Construction (`strong-proper-name-001`): In referential argument use the NP is a strong proper name (leaving for Grandma Sharon); in address it isn't (Thanks, Aunt Sarah). Test: hold the referent fixed and drop the personal name. Bare Grandpa has a simple head and is itself the name, while in Grandpa Dale the kin term modifies a proper noun.
- Overlaps with: Title with a Personal Name (`title-personal-name-001`): Both place a title before a personal name with no determiner, the pattern CGEL calls appellation plus name (pp. 518–519). That entry's titles identify an office, qualification or style (Sir James, Dr Patel); a kin title identifies a kin class. Test: drop the personal name. A kin title can remain as a name for the same person: Schneider and Homans's informants addressed or referred to an uncle by uncle plus first name or by uncle alone (1955: 1195), and bare Aunty occurs as an argument (Do the duck for Aunty?, CHILDES Peters, 010410c.cha, line 605). Sir can't: dropping the name from Sir James opened the ceremony leaves ∗Sir opened the ceremony (constructed). Some occupational appellations pass the test, as in CGEL's Have you seen Nurse? (p. 518), so it separates kin titles from Sir, not from every office title.
- Implements: Proper Name (`proper-name-001`): The NP is used as a relative's name; proper name stays the semantic classification.
- Compare: Integrated Appositive Name (`restrictive-appositive-name-001`): Compare it's your brother Ben's birthday, where brother is head and Ben an integrated appositive, with grampa Charlie's story, where the kin term modifies the name and no determiner occurs.
- Interacts with: Vocative Construction (`vocative-001`): The same NP serves in address (Thanks, Aunt Sarah, constructed).
- Interacts with: Genitive 's (`genitive-s-001`): The NP can be subject-determiner in a genitive (grampa Charlie's story).

## Projection

Knowing that a kin title plus a personal name is a family's designation for a relative predicts that it occurs with no determiner in argument functions, as a genitive subject-determiner and in address, and that the same person can also be designated by the first name alone, the kin term alone or a relational NP such as my uncle, depending on audience and relationship.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Family titles before a name

A family word such as Grandma or Aunt placed before a name works as the name of one relative.

A relative can be called by a family word plus a name: Grandma Sharon, Aunt Sarah, Grandpa Dale. The two parts do different jobs. The family word says what kind of relative it is, and the name picks out which one.

There's no the or my in front. You can say we're leaving for Grandma Sharon, or call out Thanks, Aunt Sarah. The same person can be named in other ways too, depending on who is listening: one study found a man calling his father's brother Uncle Bill, Bill or my uncle, depending on whom he was speaking to.

### Teacher

Explains kin titles used as part of a name (Grandma Sharon) and how they differ from titles like Dr or Sir.

In Grandma Sharon, the head of the NP is the proper noun Sharon, and the kin term is a pre-head modifier with no determiner of its own. The kin term gives a kin class, and the personal name picks out one member of it. The NP can be a referential argument, the subject-determiner in a genitive (grampa Charlie's story), or a vocative (Thanks, Aunt Sarah). This dictionary treats the kin term as a modifier of the same kind as CGEL's appellations (Queen, Sister, Captain, Dr), but notes that CGEL's list doesn't include kin terms, so this extends CGEL's analysis. With a genitive determiner the structure differs: in your brother Ben's birthday, brother is head and Ben an appositive.
