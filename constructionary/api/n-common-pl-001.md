### Curious reader: Plural words for kinds of things

A plural common noun such as *dogs* or *cities* typically names more than one of something and goes with plural verbs.

*Dogs* and *cities* are plural common nouns. They go with plural verbs, and with numbers greater than one: *Two cats are asleep*. They typically stand for more than one of something.

Some plural words work differently. *Scissors* takes a plural verb too, but it names a single tool made of two blades. To count the tools one by one, people ordinarily say *pairs of scissors*. This dictionary notes that special and irregular plurals can depart from the typical meaning.

A plural common noun is plural-marked, selects plural agreement and plural-selecting determinatives, and typically denotes more than one instance. *Cats* licenses plural verbal agreement and cardinals greater than one. Lexicalized and non-canonical plurals diverge from that typical meaning. A plural-only form such as *scissors* also takes plural agreement, but counting individual instruments ordinarily uses *pairs of scissors*. The pattern interfaces with verb-number agreement, and this dictionary lists the quasi-count noun as a related construction.

How do you count *scissors*?
