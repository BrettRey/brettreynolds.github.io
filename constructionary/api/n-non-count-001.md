### Curious reader: Words for stuff you don't count

Non-count nouns such as water and rice name something thought of as stuff, not as separate items.

Water and rice are non-count nouns. They name something thought of as undifferentiated stuff. They reject cardinal numbers, they take much and less, and they default to singular agreement: much coffee.

The same word can sometimes shift. Two coffees works because the context supplies portions or varieties, such as cups or kinds. So the word alone doesn't decide how it's used, and this dictionary lists beer, chicken, paper, furniture, cattle and police as words near the boundary. The analysis is provisional.

### Teacher

Teaches the count and non-count distinction, with flexible cases such as two coffees as context-dependent shifts.

A non-count noun has non-count morphosyntax and, by default, a substance or aggregate construal. Diagnostics: it rejects cardinals, takes much and less, and defaults to singular agreement. In much coffee, coffee has non-count distribution; Two coffees permits a count reading because the context supplies portions or varieties. The individual lexical form alone doesn't decide the syntactic use, and speakers vary on how flexible particular words are. Near the boundary are beer, chicken, paper, furniture, cattle and police. ### Child

Why do we say much rice but count apples?

Some words name stuff, like water and rice. We don't count the stuff itself. We say much water. But two coffees can mean two cups of coffee.
