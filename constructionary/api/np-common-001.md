## Meaning 1

NP whose nominal head is a common noun; compatible with definite or indefinite, and unique or non-unique, reference

- *dog*: General reference to the kind
- *the books*: Specific reference to a set of books

## Constraints

- (syntactic) The nominal head must be a common noun

## Projection

A common noun such as river heads either the river or a river. A particular identifiable referent need not therefore be named through a proper noun.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Phrases built on everyday nouns

A common noun phrase is built around an ordinary noun like river or book, not a name.

Ordinary nouns such as dog, river and book head phrases like the books or a river. A phrase like this can point to something definite or something indefinite, and to one particular thing or to something less specific.

So a particular, identifiable thing doesn't have to be named with a name. The river can point to one particular river, and the books to a set of books.

### Teacher

Separates the kind of noun heading a phrase from whether the phrase is definite or identifies one referent.

A common noun phrase has a common noun as its nominal head. It's compatible with definite or indefinite reference and with unique or non-unique reference. The noun class and the referential status are different matters: a noun such as river heads either the river or a river, so a particular identifiable referent need not be named with a proper noun. Dogs and the dogs share the same head category; the first has no overt determiner and the second has one.
