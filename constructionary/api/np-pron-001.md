# Pronominal Noun Phrase

- id: `np-pron-001`
- pattern: NP[Pron]
- status: provisional analysis; evidence level: unassessed
- linguist edition: https://brettreynolds.ca/constructionary/linguist/np-pron-001/

## Meaning 1

Anaphoric reference - refers back to a previously mentioned entity (antecedent)

The interpretation can recover an earlier linguistic antecedent in the same sentence or the surrounding discourse.

- *John arrived. [He] looked tired.*: '*He*' refers back to '*John*'.

## Meaning 2

Cataphoric reference - refers forward to an entity mentioned later in the discourse

A following antecedent is possible in suitable structural and discourse settings.

- *Before [he] entered the room, John switched off his phone.*: '*He*' refers forward to '*John*' in this reading.

## Meaning 3

Exophoric reference - refers to an entity in the extralinguistic context (situation, shared knowledge)

Salience in the situation can identify the referent without any antecedent expression.

- *[He]'s late again.*: Said about someone not present, referring to a shared understanding of who '*he*' is.

## Meaning 4

Bound variable reference - refers to an entity whose referent varies depending on a quantifier

The value can vary with the quantified antecedent.

- *Every student thinks [they] are smart.*: '*they*' refers to each student, and its referent varies with each student.

## Meaning 5

Dummy pronoun - has no referential meaning, serves a grammatical function

Dummy *it* and *there* occupy grammatical positions without identifying entities.

- *[It] is raining.*: '*It*' does not refer to anything; it's a grammatical placeholder.
- *[There] is a problem.*: '*There*' does not refer to a place; it introduces the existence of something.

## Constraints

- (syntactic) Must consist of a pronoun functioning as the head of the NP

## Projection

*They* and *them* can pick up a prior referent but change realization with subject versus object function. Dummy *it* can fill a subject slot with no antecedent, so antecedence is neither necessary for pronominal NP status nor sufficient to pick its case.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Words that stand in for a noun phrase

A pronoun like *he*, *they* or *it* can take the place of a whole noun phrase and still work as the subject or object.

In *John arrived. He looked tired*, the word *he* points back to John. In *Before he entered the room, John switched off his phone*, *he* can point forward to John. Said about someone who isn't there, *He's late again* points to a person the speakers both know about.

Some uses point to a different person for each case: in *Every student thinks they are smart*, *they* follows each student. And in *It is raining*, the word *it* doesn't point to anything. It just fills the spot a subject normally fills.

### Teacher

Covers the different ways a pronoun NP finds its meaning, from earlier and later mentions to the situation and dummy uses.

A pronominal NP consists of a pronoun as head. The dictionary distinguishes anaphoric uses (*He looked tired*, pointing back to John), cataphoric uses (*Before he entered the room, John switched off his phone*), exophoric uses (*He's late again*, with the referent supplied by the situation), bound variable uses (*Every student thinks they are smart*) and dummy uses (*It is raining*; *There is a problem*), where the pronoun doesn't identify an entity. Having an antecedent is neither necessary for pronominal NP status nor sufficient to pick its case: *they* and *them* can pick up a prior referent but change form with subject versus object function. In this grammar, *everybody*, *something* and *nobody* are compound determinatives, not pronouns. The dictionary marks this account as provisional.
