# Paired-NP Introduction

- id: `paired-np-introduction-001`
- pattern: Vocative:NP + pause + NP[name or definite description]
- status: provisional analysis; evidence level: unassessed
- linguist edition: https://brettreynolds.ca/constructionary/linguist/paired-np-introduction-001/
- licence: CC BY 4.0; snapshot 2026-11-19 (5c11b28)

## Meaning 1

Introduces the referent of the second NP to the addressee picked out by the first.

A pause separates the two NPs. Gaze or gesture often helps identify the participant being introduced. The introduced referent can be presented by name or by description without a preceding clause to complete. In the shelter example, Alex can learn that Teacup is the kitten’s name in the course of being introduced. The construction alone gives no evidence about when the name was established or whether the hearer stores a separate noun lexeme.

- *[Alex, Teacup.]*: Constructed. A volunteer addresses Alex and indicates the kitten called Teacup.
- *[Elise, Casey.]*: Published example of introducing Casey to Elise.
- *[Alex, the new vet.]*: Constructed. A definite description identifies the person being introduced.

## Related constructions

- Contains: Vocative Construction (`vocative-001`): The first NP has vocative function.
- Compare: Strong Proper Name Construction (`strong-proper-name-001`): The introduced-referent slot can be filled by a strong name NP.
- A kind of: Clause-Analogue Construction (`clause-analogue-001`): The two phrases conventionally perform an introduction without a VP at the construction’s level.
- Compare: Proper Name in a Descriptive NP (`proper-name-secondary-use-001`)

## Projection

In an introduction context, the first NP sets the addressee and the second identifies the introduced referent. The second slot admits descriptions as well as names, without requiring an overt introducing verb or a linguistic antecedent.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Introducing someone with two names

Saying someone's name, pausing, then naming or describing someone else can introduce that second person or thing to the first.

A volunteer at an animal shelter says Alex, Teacup. Alex is the person being spoken to, and Teacup is the kitten being pointed out. A pause separates the two phrases, and pointing or looking helps show who's being introduced.

There's no verb, and none is needed. The second phrase can be a name, as in Elise, Casey, or a description, as in Alex, the new vet. This dictionary describes the first phrase as addressing someone and the second as identifying who is being introduced.

### Teacher

Shows how two noun phrases with a pause can serve as a complete introduction.

The pattern is a vocative NP, a pause, then an NP that is a name or a definite description. The first NP picks out the addressee, and the second identifies the referent being introduced to that addressee. A pause separates the two, and gaze or gesture often helps identify the person or animal introduced. The introduced referent can be presented by name or by description, without a preceding clause to complete. This dictionary treats the pair as a clause analogue: the two phrases conventionally perform an introduction without a VP at the construction's level. This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional.

### Child

How can you introduce a friend with just two names?

Say the name of the person you're talking to. Pause. Then say the name of the person you're introducing. Pointing or looking helps.
