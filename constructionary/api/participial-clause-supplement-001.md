# Subjectless Participial Supplement

## Meaning 1

Adds a circumstance whose understood subject is normally recovered from the anchor.

- *[Hearing footsteps], Jo switched off the light.*: Constructed illustration.
- *Lee shut the door, [startled by the noise].*: Constructed comparison. The past-participial supplement takes Lee as its understood subject.

## Projection

Jo is the ordinary understood hearer. A separate overt subject would instead support an absolute supplement, while some fixed discourse adjuncts use contextual subjects.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Extra -ing and -ed phrases beside a sentence

A phrase starting with an -ing or -ed verb can add a circumstance to a sentence, with its doer understood from the main clause.

In Hearing footsteps, Jo switched off the light, the phrase hearing footsteps adds a circumstance but doesn't say who heard. We understand that it was Jo. In Lee shut the door, startled by the noise, the -ed phrase is understood to be about Lee.

The doer is normally taken from the main part of the sentence. If the added phrase has its own subject, it's a different pattern, which this dictionary calls an absolute supplement. Some fixed phrases get their understood subject from the situation instead.

### Teacher

Explains -ing and -ed phrases added to a main clause, and how their understood subject is recovered.

A subjectless participial supplement is a nonfinite phrase (V-ing or V-en plus dependents) anchored to a main clause. It adds a circumstance, and its understood subject is normally recovered from the anchor: Jo is the hearer in the first example, and Lee is the startled one in the second. A separate overt subject would support an absolute supplement instead. Some fixed discourse adjuncts use contextual subjects. This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional.
