# Past Tense *-ed*

- id: `past-tense-ed-001`
- pattern: Verb stem + -ed/-d/-t (including allomorphs /d/, /t/, /ɪd/)
- status: provisional analysis; evidence level: unassessed
- linguist edition: https://brettreynolds.ca/constructionary/linguist/past-tense-ed-001/

## Meaning 1

Marks temporal/modal/social distance

Core meaning potentially involving displacement from a reference point (temporal, modal, or interpersonal) - though this unification remains debatable

- *She walked home*: Temporal displacement
- *If I owned a car*: Modal displacement
- *I wanted to ask you*: Social displacement

## Meaning 2

Temporal anteriority

Marks situation as occurring before reference time (but see cases like sports commentary where this breaks down)

- *They finished yesterday*: Clear anterior time
- *Jones passed to Smith!*: Immediate past challenging pure anteriority

## Meaning 3

Non-actual/hypothetical marking

Indicates displacement from actual/current reality

- *If she studied more*: Counterfactual condition
- *I wish I lived there*: Contrary-to-fact desire

## Meaning 4

Social distancing marker

Creates interpersonal distance, often for politeness

- *Did you want coffee?*: Polite offer
- *I hoped we could talk*: Tentative approach

## Projection

Regular *-ed* can mark a preterite or past participle. In *Jo checked the key* it marks finite preterite, while in *Jo has checked the key* it realizes the participle selected by *have*. Remote and backshifted preterites need not locate the event before speech time.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: The *-ed* ending on verbs

Adding *-ed* to a verb can place something in the past, but it can also mark something imagined or add politeness.

The *-ed* ending (or *-d* or *-t*, with more than one pronunciation) places something in time, as in *She walked home* and *They finished yesterday*. But it does more. In *If I owned a car*, it marks something that isn't actual. In *I wanted to ask you*, it adds social distance, which can make a request or offer more polite. This dictionary also gives *Did you want coffee?* as a polite offer, with the past form *did*.

This dictionary lists these as separate meanings and notes that whether one idea, displacement from a reference point, unites them remains debatable. Past time doesn't fit every use: *Jones passed to Smith!* in a sports commentary describes an immediate past.

### Teacher

Explains the *-ed* ending as more than past time, covering hypothetical and politeness uses, and how its preterite use differs from its participle use.

Regular *-ed* (with the allomorphs *-d*, *-t*, /d/, /t/ and /ɪd/) marks the preterite. This dictionary lists four meanings: displacement from a reference point (temporal, modal or interpersonal), temporal anteriority (*They finished yesterday*), non-actual or hypothetical marking (*If she studied more*; *I wish I lived there*), and social distancing (*Did you want coffee?*; *I hoped we could talk*). The first, unifying meaning remains debatable. Temporal anteriority has exceptions such as sports commentary (*Jones passed to Smith!*). Remote and backshifted preterites need not locate the event before speech time. Regular *-ed* also marks the past participle, as in *Jo has checked the key*. This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional.

### Child

What happens to a verb when you add *ed*?

Adding *ed* can make a verb tell about the past, as in *She walked home*. It can also talk about something that isn't real, as in *If I owned a car*. It can also make something sound more polite, as in *I wanted to ask you*.
