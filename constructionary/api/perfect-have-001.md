## Meaning 1

Places a prior situation relative to a temporal anchor

The present perfect has a present anchor and often suggests current relevance. In many varieties it resists an explicitly finished past-time setting. The preterite perfect instead uses a past anchor.

- *She has finished the report*: Prior completion relevant now
- *They had already left*: Prior event relative to a past reference time

## Meaning 2

Marks experiential or repeatable prior occurrence

- *I have visited Japan*: Experiential perfect
- *We have met before*: Prior occurrence with relevance

## Meaning 3

Marks a continuing situation spanning up to the reference time (universal/continuative perfect)

- *I have lived there for three years*: Residence continues up to now
- *She has worked here since 2020*: Ongoing state with inception in the past

## Projection

Perfect have selects a past participle. With a past anchor, had left precedes that past time. A present anchor is only one possibility, even when no time expression is overt.

### Curious reader: Have plus a past participle

Have followed by a verb's past-participle form relates a situation to a point in time, such as now. The situation can be earlier than that point, or continue up to it.

In She has finished the report, the finishing came before now, and it matters now. In They had already left, the leaving came before some time in the past.

The same build also covers I have visited Japan and We have met before, which report something that happened earlier, and I have lived there for three years, where the living continues up to now. This dictionary notes that the present version often suggests current relevance, and that in many varieties it resists an explicitly finished past-time setting.

### Teacher

Core pattern for the perfect, with its present, past, experiential and continuing uses.

Perfect have selects a past participle and relates a situation to a temporal anchor. With a present anchor (has finished) the situation is prior to now or, in the continuing use, runs up to now; with a past anchor (had left) it precedes that past time. A present anchor is only one possibility, even when no time expression is overt. This dictionary distinguishes three uses: a prior situation relative to the anchor (has finished the report, had already left), experiential or repeatable prior occurrence (have visited Japan), and a continuing situation spanning up to the reference time (have lived there for three years, has worked here since 2020). It also separates perfect anteriority from primary tense and from the contextual interpretation of current relevance. This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional, with medium confidence.

### Child

When someone says I have finished, what happened before now?

Have or has plus a word like finished can tell us something already happened. She has finished the report. They had already left means the leaving came before some time in the past.
