## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Words that mean more than one

Plural forms mark more than one thing, or a group of things taken together, and they trigger matching forms elsewhere in a sentence.

Plural forms appear in nouns, pronouns and some other small words, not just nouns: *cats*, *they* and *these* all point to more than one. Some plurals name a group, as in *Kennedys*, or types rather than a mass, as in *wines*. Others mark recurring instances, as in *headaches*.

The plural form controls ordinary plural agreement, as in *The scissors are blunt*. Yet the description can be about one instrument. So the plural form tells us how the grammar treats the word, not strictly how many objects there are.

### Teacher

Overview of plural forms across nouns, pronouns and determinatives, and what they signal.

Plural morphology (*-s*, *-es*, irregular, zero) applies to common and proper nouns, pronouns and determinatives. It signals non-singular construal and triggers agreement. This dictionary lists four uses: non-singular entities (*cats*, *they*, *these*), countable aggregates (*Kennedys*, *many*), countable taxonomic categories, that is, types rather than non-count (*wines*), and recurring instances (*headaches*). Form and number of referents can come apart: *the scissors are blunt* has plural agreement but can describe one instrument, because grammatical number is separate from the number of objects described. This dictionary marks this as provisional, with medium confidence.

### Child

Why do we say *the scissors are*, when you hold only one pair?

Plural words like *cats* and *they* mean more than one. Some words, like *scissors*, look plural even when you mean one tool. We say *The scissors are blunt*.
