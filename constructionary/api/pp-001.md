## Meaning 1

Phrase headed by a preposition, with or without a complement

The head may take an NP object (in the garden), a predicative NP or AdjP (as a liability, as reasonable), an AdvP (until recently), a clause (because it rained), a PP (from under the bed), or no complement (outside). A single complementless preposition constitutes a complete PP (CGEL Ch. 2 §7, p. 58).

- *[in the garden]*: P with NP object
- *[as a liability]*: P with predicative NP complement
- *[until recently]*: P with AdvP complement
- *[because it rained]*: P with clause complement
- *[from under the bed]*: P with PP complement; under the bed expresses the source location
- *[outside]*: Complete PP consisting of one complementless preposition

## Constraints

- (syntactic) The phrase is headed by a preposition; locative members accept such modifiers as right and straight.

## Projection

A preposition can head a phrase even without an NP after it, as in We waited outside. Some heads take a different category, as with because it rained, and a PP can itself be selected, as with from under the stairs.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Phrases built around words like in and until

A preposition phrase is a group of words whose main word is a preposition, such as in, as, until or because.

In in the garden, the preposition in is the main word, and the garden follows it. The same kind of phrase can be built with other kinds of follower: as a liability, until recently, because it rained, and from under the bed.

A preposition can also stand alone as a complete phrase. Outside is a whole phrase on its own, as in We waited outside. What makes each of these a preposition phrase is its main word, not the kind of word that comes after it.

### Teacher

Defines the PP and the range of complements a preposition can take, including none.

A PP is a phrase headed by a preposition, with or without a complement. The head may take an NP object (in the garden), a predicative NP or AdjP (as a liability, as reasonable), an AdvP (until recently), a clause (because it rained), a PP (from under the bed), or no complement (outside). A single complementless preposition constitutes a complete PP. PPs are classified by their heads, not by an NP being present. Locative members accept modifiers such as right and straight. A PP can function as a complement, modifier, adjunct or predicative complement in a larger construction.
