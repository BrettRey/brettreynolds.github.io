# Subject Extraposition

- id: `subject-extraposition-001`
- pattern: Clause[Subject:dummy it + VP[... + extraposed content clause or infinitival]]
- status: provisional analysis; evidence level: unassessed
- linguist edition: https://brettreynolds.ca/constructionary/linguist/subject-extraposition-001/
- licence: CC BY 4.0; snapshot 2026-11-19 (5c11b28)

## Meaning 1

Puts the situation under discussion after the predicate, leaving dummy it in subject position.

A that-clause, an interrogative clause, or an infinitival may occur in the later position. The basic initial-clause version is often less natural when the information is presented as new. A referential NP does not ordinarily alternate in this way. In a right dislocation like It’s fascinating, this book, it refers to the book.

- *[It worries me that he hasn’t phoned.]*: Compare the basic That he hasn’t phoned worries me.
- *[It is unclear why she told him.]*: Interrogative in the later position.
- *[It would be pointless to resist.]*: Infinitival in the later position.

## Projection

A clause can appear after the predicate with dummy it. The same predicate need not allow an ordinary NP in that position. Extraposition is especially useful with newly introduced information.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Saving the clause for the end

A sentence can start with it and put a clause after the verb to say what the situation is.

Instead of saying That he hasn't phoned worries me, we can say It worries me that he hasn't phoned. The clause that tells what the worry is about moves to the end, and the small word it fills the subject spot as a stand-in with nothing of its own to refer to.

The later part can be a that-clause, a question clause or a to-phrase. This dictionary points out that the version with the clause up front is often less natural when the information is new.

This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional.
