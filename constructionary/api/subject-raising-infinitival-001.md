## Meaning 1

Combines a verbal assessment or aspectual predicate with a subject selected through its complement.

- *[There seems to be a leak].*: Constructed illustration.
- *[Jo seems to have checked the lock].*: Constructed comparison. The corresponding passive, *The lock seems to have been checked by Jo*, preserves the subordinate event being assessed.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Sentences where the subject belongs to the next verb

With verbs like *seem*, the subject of the sentence really belongs to the *to*-phrase that follows.

In *There seems to be a leak*, the word *there* can't be doing anything of its own. It is the subject because the *to*-phrase, *to be a leak*, selects it. The verb *seems* gives an assessment of what follows.

That's why *Jo seems to have checked the lock* and *The lock seems to have been checked by Jo* describe the same thing that seems to hold. The subordinate event is what's being assessed.

This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional and bases it on constructed illustrations.

### Teacher

Useful for contrasting seem-type verbs with promise-type verbs and for showing why dummy there works with them.

The pattern combines a verbal assessment or aspectual predicate with a subject selected through its complement. Two tests show it. Dummy *there* is acceptable (*There seems to be a leak*), and active and passive descriptions of the subordinate event preserve what seems to hold (*Jo seems to have checked the lock*; *The lock seems to have been checked by Jo*). Subject control, as in *Jo promised to return*, differs on both counts. This dictionary treats the analysis as provisional and bases it on constructed illustrations.
