# Tag Question

- id: `tag-question-001`
- pattern: Anchor:Clause[declarative | exclamative] + Tag:Clause[Head:VP[Head:V[auxiliary]]; Subject:NP[personal pronoun]; surface Auxiliary–Subject (+ not)] => Meaning:Question
- status: provisional analysis; evidence level: stable
- linguist edition: https://brettreynolds.ca/constructionary/linguist/tag-question-001/
- licence: CC BY 4.0; snapshot 2026-11-19 (ef810bb)

## Meaning 1

- *[He is ill], [isn't he]?*: Positive declarative Anchor Clause plus negative Tag Clause; reversed polarity
- *[Everybody has read it], [haven't they]?*: Tag auxiliary agrees with plural tag Subject *they*, not directly with singular anchor Subject *everybody*
- *[What a mess I've made of things], [haven't I]?*: Exclamative Anchor Clause with the normally falling, acknowledgement-oriented reversed tag

## Meaning 2

- *[He is ill], [is he]?*: Positive declarative anchor and positive tag; constant polarity
- *[So you have forgotten your homework again], [have you]?*: Constant-polarity tag with reproachful or disapproving force

## Constraints

- (structural) The construction combines an Anchor Clause with a supplementary Tag Clause; it does not turn their combination into a single Clause. Each Clause independently has the strict structure (Subject +) Head:VP.
- (syntactic) The Anchor is a declarative or exclamative Clause. The Tag is a short closed-interrogative Clause with a personal-pronoun Subject and a VP headed by an auxiliary; subject–auxiliary inversion determines surface order.
- (syntactic) If the relevant anchor Predicator is auxiliary-headed, the tag normally selects the same auxiliary lexeme; otherwise it selects *do*. The tag auxiliary normally has the anchor's tense.
- (syntactic) The personal-pronoun tag Subject repeats a pronominal anchor Subject or is anaphorically selected for a non-pronominal one. Person–number agreement is controlled by the tag Subject, as *Everybody has read it, haven't they?* demonstrates.
- (morphological) A negative tag permits synthetic auxiliary negation in ordinary style or analytic final *not* in more formal style.
- (semantic) The tag has polarity opposite to the anchor in the frequent reversed-polarity variant and the same polarity in the constant-polarity variant; semantically negative anchors such as *Few of them liked it* select a positive reversed tag.
- (pragmatic) Prosody is constructionally significant: a reversed rising tag normally requests verification, a reversed falling tag seeks acknowledgement, and a constant tag characteristically has a slight rise with attributional or emotive effects.

## Projection

Recognizing Tag Question membership predicts an anaphoric personal-pronoun Subject, same-auxiliary selection or *do*-support, preservation of relevant tense, agreement with the tag Subject, a systematic reversed-or-constant polarity relation, and prosody-sensitive verification, acknowledgement, or emotive force

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Questions tacked onto the end of a statement

A short question such as *isn't he?* can follow a statement to ask the listener to confirm it or react to it.

In *He is ill, isn't he?*, a short question follows the statement. It's built from an auxiliary verb and a pronoun that refers back to the person in the statement: *isn't he*. In the frequent version the question has the opposite polarity from the statement, positive then negative. A rising voice asks for confirmation, and a falling one invites agreement or acknowledgement.

A tag can also repeat the statement's polarity: *He is ill, is he?* This version can express surprise, disapproval or sarcasm, not doubt. The pronoun decides the verb's form, as in *Everybody has read it, haven't they?*

This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional.

### Teacher

Core material for tag questions: how the tag is formed and how polarity and intonation change its force.

A tag question pairs an anchor clause (a declarative or exclamative) with a short closed-interrogative tag clause. The tag has a personal-pronoun subject and an auxiliary-headed VP, with subject-auxiliary inversion. If the anchor's predicator is headed by an auxiliary, the tag normally selects the same auxiliary; otherwise it uses *do*. The tag normally keeps the anchor's tense, and the verb agrees with the tag's pronoun. In the frequent reversed-polarity variant, a rising tag asks for verification and a falling tag seeks acknowledgement. A constant-polarity tag characteristically has a slight rise and can convey surprise, disapproval, reproach or sarcasm. Prosody is part of the construction. This dictionary treats the analysis as provisional, though confidence in the pattern is marked high.

### Child

How do you ask a friend to agree with something you just said?

A tag question is a tiny question stuck on the end of a sentence. It uses a short helper word like *is*, *has* or *does*, and a pronoun like *he* or *she*. Your voice rising or falling changes what you mean.
