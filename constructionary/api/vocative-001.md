## Meaning 1

Addresses the hearer directly, identifying them as the intended recipient

Forms its own intonation phrase, typically with fall-rise or level contour; summons use may have high sustained "calling" contour. Not syntactically integrated as a clause argument. NP is either bare or determined by 1st/2nd person forms only (genitive my/our/your, or determinative you).

- *[Mom], can you help me?*: Bare kinship term, clause-initial
- *I think, [Brett], you're wrong.*: Bare proper name, clause-medial
- *[Moooom]!*: Summons with calling contour (high sustained pitch)
- *[Ladies and gentlemen], welcome.*: Bare coordinated NPs, formulaic
- *[My friend], let me explain.*: 1st-person genitive determiner
- *[Our Father], who art in heaven...*: 1st-person plural genitive
- *[Your majesty], we request an audience.*: 2nd-person genitive, honorific
- *[You idiot], watch out!*: 2nd-person personal determinative

## Constraints

- (syntactic) NP is bare or determined only by 1st/2nd person forms (my, our, your, you); 3rd-person and non-deictic determiners excluded
- (pragmatic) Requires addressee to be present (real or imagined)
- (structural) Forms separate intonation phrase with fall-rise, level, or calling contour
- (position) Can occur clause-initially, clause-medially, or clause-finally

## Projection

An overt imperative subject and a vocative can co-occur, as in Lee, you take the first shift. Moving Lee to the end can preserve the address reading while you continues to supply the subject. The name in the vocative is therefore not the only potential subject expression.

## Reader editions (draft, AI-assisted, unreviewed; the linguist entry is the reference)

### Curious reader: Calling someone by name

A name or title said directly to the person you're speaking to, set apart from the rest of the sentence.

In Mom, can you help me?, the word Mom isn't part of what is being asked. It addresses the hearer directly and picks them out as the person meant. This is a vocative.

It sits in its own stretch of speech, with its own tone, such as a fall-rise, a level tone, or a high held calling tone when summoning someone. It can come at the start, the middle or the end: I think, Brett, you're wrong. The noun phrase is either bare, as in Mom or Ladies and gentlemen, or begins with my, our, your or you, as in My friend, let me explain. A drawn-out, high Moooom! is the calling version.

This dictionary marks this account as provisional.

### Teacher

Helps explain direct address, its separate intonation and the determiners it allows.

A vocative addresses the hearer directly. It forms its own intonation phrase, with a fall-rise or level contour, or a high sustained calling contour for a summons. It is not selected by the verb and is not syntactically integrated as a clause argument. The noun phrase is either bare or determined only by first- and second-person forms (the genitives my, our, your, or the determinative you). It can occur clause-initially, clause-medially or clause-finally, and it needs an addressee, real or imagined. In an imperative, an overt subject and a vocative can co-occur, as in Lee, you take the first shift. This dictionary marks its analysis provisional.
