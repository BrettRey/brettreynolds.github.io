English Constructionary

How English puts words together, one pattern at a time.

A construction is a pattern that pairs a form with a meaning. Some are tiny, like adding -s to make a plural. Others are whole sentence shapes, like She baked him a cake, where the pattern itself adds the idea of giving. This edition explains each pattern in everyday language, with examples you can try on your own speech.

    A

    • A bench to sit on sketchA to plus a verb after a noun can describe the thing by what could be done with it, as in a bench to sit on.
    • A car of my ownA phrase like a car of my own presents something as set aside for your ownership or use.
    • A change with a cause, and without one sketchSome verbs can say who or what caused a change, or can simply say that the thing changed.
    • A circumstance with its own subjectA phrase added to a sentence can have a subject of its own and describe the circumstances of the main action.
    • A command followed by a warning sketchA command joined by or to a result tells you what may happen if you ignore it.
    • A command that works like an if sketchA command followed by and can work like an if-sentence, saying what will happen if you do the thing.
    • A few and a little sketchA few and a little present a small quantity as a positive amount.
    • A genitive that acts like a subjectSometimes the word with 's before a noun tells you who did the action the noun names, not who owns something.
    • A kind of thing plus its labelA word naming a kind of thing, followed by a number or letter code, can pick out one particular thing.
    • A name added right after a description sketchA name can follow a descriptive noun with no pause, so the two together pick out one particular thing.
    • A noun in front of another nounA noun can come before another noun to say what kind, what for or where from.
    • A noun of a noun, used to judgeIn a phrase like a nitwit of a husband, the first noun is a judgment about the person or thing named by the second.
    • A noun with its describing wordsThe part of a noun phrase made of a noun and its modifiers, not counting the word like the in front.
    • A question word reaching into a later clauseA question word at the front of a sentence can ask about something that belongs to a verb in a later clause.
    • A relative clause that reaches into a later clauseA phrase that describes a noun can link back to the noun from inside a later clause.
    • A second description added to a name sketchA second noun phrase beside the first adds another way of identifying the same person or thing.
    • A shaken Giuliani, a smiling JohnsonPutting a or an and a describing word in front of a person's name can present that person in a particular state on a particular occasion.
    • A single noun phrase that calls for attentionA lone noun phrase, said with marked stress, can direct someone's attention to a person or thing.
    • A ten, three tensA number word can work like an ordinary noun, naming an instance of a number such as a score.
    • A verb and an object that name the same eventSome verbs can be followed by a noun naming the same event, which then lets you describe how it happened.
    • A what phrase that acts as a noun phraseA phrase like what she wrote names the thing that was written, much as a noun phrase would.
    • A which clause that adds extra information sketchA which clause after a name can add a separate piece of information about it.
    • A word that means at no timeThe adverb never says that something did not happen at any time.
    • Active-looking words with a passive meaningAfter verbs like need, an -ing word can have a passive meaning even though it looks active.
    • Activity nouns ending in -ing sketchSome words in -ing name an activity or its result and behave like nouns, taking words like careful and of.
    • Adding -s to make a pluralAdding -s or -es to a common noun makes it plural, and the same ending can also count types, repeated events or members of a family.
    • Adding force to yes and noInformal speakers put an emphatic word in front of yeah, yes or no to give the answer extra feeling.
    • Adjectives ending in -ableIn its central pattern, adding -able to a verb gives an adjective that says something can have that action done to it.
    • Adjectives ending in -ful sketchAdding -ful to a noun gives an adjective saying something has or displays what the noun names.
    • Adjectives in front of me, you and usA pronoun such as you or me can take an adjective in front of it to pick out a stage of a person's life or the state they were in.
    • Admitting a drawback with althoughAn although phrase acknowledges something that might have counted against the main claim.
    • Advice with had better sketchHad better recommends a course of action and often hints at a bad result if it isn't followed.
    • Aiming an action at somethingAn at-phrase can show that an action was aimed at something, without saying it connected.
    • All as a restriction, as in an all girls' schoolPutting all before a class word can say that only members of that class fill a role connected to the noun.
    • All before this, that or the sketchAll can stand before a determiner such as those or the, applying to the whole group named.
    • All of before a numberPutting all of before a stated amount draws attention to that amount, as if it were noteworthy.
    • Amounts and containers followed by ofA unit or container word followed by of says how much of a substance or how many things were involved.
    • Another three days sketchAnother before a number or measure adds an amount of that size to what has gone before.
    • Answering yes or no with a short reply sketchA short reply to a yes/no question can say yes or no, then a pronoun, then a helper verb, and leave the rest out.
    • Are to, for rules and arrangements sketchA form of be followed by to and a verb can express a requirement, an official arrangement or a planned event.
    • Asking by tone of voiceA statement-shaped sentence said with a questioning tone can ask for confirmation of something the speaker already expects.
    • Asking for and giving a sizeA noun phrase like that size can sit before or after another noun to say which value of some property we mean.
    • Asking or denying "long" after a verbPlain long after a verb works in a question, a negative or an if-clause to describe a potentially long time.
    • Asking whether something has ever happenedA have plus past-participle sentence can place earlier happenings within a stretch of time that matters now.
    • Asking whose it isThe word whose can stand alone as a question about the owner of something.
    • At school, with no the sketchSome everyday nouns, such as school, can appear with no article when they refer to an activity or status.

    B

    • Because giving the reason for an event sketchA because-part can give the reason that something happened or was the case.
    • Being a philosopher in every wayEvery inch a philosopher says that someone fully fits a role or type, in the respects that matter for it.
    • Being plus -ing for plans sketchA be plus -ing verb with a future time can describe something already planned.
    • Being plus -ing for things in progressA form of be plus a verb ending in -ing presents something as ongoing or unfolding at a particular time.
    • Being sent somethingA passive sentence can make the person who receives something the subject while the thing received stays after the verb.
    • Being the same as something else sketchThe same followed by as compares two things and says they match.
    • Big numbers said with andSome numbers are built by joining a large amount and a small addition with the word and.
    • Big numbers said without andSome numbers are built by putting a small number straight after a large one, with no and between them.
    • Both a and b sketchBoth before the first item and and before the second say outright that two things are included.

    C

    • Calling someone by nameA name or title said directly to the person you're speaking to, set apart from the rest of the sentence.
    • Came running, came roaringCome followed by an -ing word can show how someone approached or what sound came with the movement.
    • Carrying a preposition to the front sketchIn a describing clause, the preposition can move to the front together with the word which or whom.
    • Choosing the better description with more than sketchMore ... than can compare two descriptions of the same thing and say which one fits better.
    • Clauses that state a requirementAfter words like requested, a that-clause can use the plain form of the verb to say what is required or recommended.
    • Commands, requests and wishesAn imperative is a sentence built around a plain-form verb, like Be generous, and it can express more than an order.
    • Comparing things on a scaleEnglish has a few ways to say where something sits on a scale, such as taller than, the tallest and as tall as.
    • Comparing with -erAdding -er to a word like tall says that one thing ranks higher than another on a scale.
    • Comparing with a different word sketchA few adjectives make their comparison forms from a different stem, such as good, better and best.
    • Comparing with as ... as sketchAs ... as sets one thing's degree against another's without saying the first has more.
    • Comparisons that leave a word out sketchIn a than-clause, part of the clause can be missing but still understood.
    • Conditions without ifPutting had, were or should before the subject can set up a condition without using if.
    • Counting and arranging in groupsA plural number word after in or by can tell how people or things are grouped.
    • Counting servings or kindsA noun for a substance, like coffee, can be counted either as servings or as varieties.
    • Crying yourself to sleep sketchA verb can take a reflexive word and a phrase naming the state that results, even if the verb wouldn't normally take that word.

    D

    • Dates written month firstA month name followed by a number picks out a day, as in June 14 or November 18.
    • Describing a noun from a distance sketchThe words that describe a noun can sit later in the sentence instead of right beside it.
    • Describing a state with a word like locked sketchA word that looks like part of a passive verb can instead describe how something is.
    • Describing the person or thing after the verbIn some sentences the phrase after the object describes it, and isn't a second object.
    • Describing the place instead of the crowd sketchA busy scene can be described through the things doing the activity or through the place where it happens.
    • Describing words in front of a nounAn adjective phrase placed before a noun says something about it, as in old men.
    • Do so standing in for an action sketchDo so can replace a phrase that has already named an action, so the speaker doesn't have to repeat it.
    • Doing something just in case sketchA phrase beginning with in case names a possibility that you're guarding against, so the main action is a precaution.
    • Doing something to reach a goalA to plus a verb after a clause can say what the action was for, as in borrowing a van to move the chairs.
    • Doing something until it reaches a state sketchA describing word after the object can show the point on a scale that the action brought something to.
    • Doing something until the object ends up a certain wayIn He painted the fence blue, the last word says what state the fence ends up in.
    • Doing something with a tool sketchA phrase with with and a thing can name what was used to do something.
    • Doubting that something deserves its nameYou call that a sandwich? can say the thing doesn't deserve the label, rather than ask what to call it.

    E

    • Each of the group sketchEach followed by of and a known group says something about every member of that group.
    • Each other and one anotherEach other and one another say that the members of a group do the same thing to one another.
    • Each, placed away from its group sketchThe word each can sit after a group of people or things and say the action applies to every member separately.
    • Easy to follow, hard to find fault withIn some sentences the adjective judges the opening noun by what we do to it, as in impossible to follow.
    • Eleven and twelveEleven and twelve sit right after ten, but they aren't built with -teen.
    • Ending a sentence with to sketchAfter words like wanted, the word to can stand without a verb after it when the listener can work out what verb is meant.
    • Enough to do something sketchEnough after a describing word says that a quality reaches the level needed for a stated result.
    • Event nouns followed by ofA noun made from a verb can be followed by of and a phrase naming someone or something involved in the event.
    • Exclaiming that something was quite a specimenSaid with heavy stress, some in an exclamation presents something as remarkable for its kind.
    • Expecting something about someoneIn They expected there to be trouble, what is expected is a whole situation, not the word after expected.
    • Extra -ing and -ed phrases beside a sentenceA phrase starting with an -ing or -ed verb can add a circumstance to a sentence, with its doer understood from the main clause.

    F

    • Fainter and fainter sketchRepeating a comparative word with and says that something keeps changing by degrees.
    • Family titles before a nameA family word such as Grandma or Aunt placed before a name works as the name of one relative.
    • Fifty miles an hourPutting a or an before a unit, as in $20 a yard, turns an amount into a rate.
    • Finding yourself a room sketchA sentence can show someone securing something for themselves by using a reflexive word, like herself, right after the verb.
    • Finishing in a stretch of timeA phrase like in fifteen minutes can say how long something took, but the same words can also say when something will start.
    • Flipping the helper verb after a restricting openingAfter an opening phrase such as only after the meeting, the helper verb comes before the subject.
    • For, then who, then to sketchA phrase made of for, a person or thing, and to plus a verb can say who would do the action.
    • Four days ago sketchA length of time followed by ago tells you how far back something happened.
    • Fraction words as nounsIn a fraction like one fifth, the word fifth names the size of the pieces and behaves like a noun.
    • Friends with someoneA plural noun like friends plus with can describe a two-way relationship, even when the sentence is about one person.
    • From one role to another, with no the or aTwo nouns with no the or a, joined by from and to, mark the two ends of a path, a handing-over or a change.

    G

    • Giving examples with such as sketchSuch as introduces some examples of the kind of thing you've just named.
    • Giving permission on one condition sketchProvided that adds a condition under which a promise or permission holds.
    • Giving your reason for saying something sketchA because phrase can explain why the speaker says something, not why the thing happened.
    • God knows whatA phrase like God knows what, placed inside a larger phrase, marks something as unknown or left unspecified, often with feeling.
    • Going to as a way of looking ahead sketchBe going to followed by a verb can present something as coming up, because of a plan or because of what is happening now.
    • Got promoted sketchUsing got with a past-participle word, as in Jo got promoted, presents someone as undergoing something that happened to them.
    • Groups of words built around a key wordA phrase is a group of words organized around one main word, which decides what else can come with it and where it can go.

    H

    • Had plus a verb for what came earlier sketchHad with a past-participle verb puts an event before another moment in the past.
    • Have plus a past participleHave followed by a verb's past-participle form relates a situation to a point in time, such as now. The situation can be earlier than that point, or continue up to it.
    • Having done something up to nowSome have-sentences say that a situation has been going on through a stretch of time that ends at the moment being talked about.
    • Having someone do something sketchIn a sentence like I'll have Jo check the figures, the speaker arranges for someone else to do the checking.
    • Having something done sketchHave plus a noun phrase and a past-participle word presents an outcome as arranged by, or happening to, the subject.
    • Hearing or seeing something happenA verb of perceiving, a person or thing, and a plain verb form together present what was perceived as an event.
    • Helper verbs in a fixed order sketchWhen several helper verbs come before a main verb, each shapes the next, and they keep a set order.
    • Her every moveA genitive before every picks out all the instances tied to a person or thing, as in her every move.
    • Here comes the busStarting a sentence with a stressed here or there, then the verb, points out something arriving, present or leaving.
    • How about a short break? sketchHow about followed by a thing or an activity puts it forward as an option for the listener to consider.
    • How big a mess, too big a problemWith words like how, too, so, that and as, the adjective can move in front of a or an.
    • How come sketchHow come asks for an explanation of something that seems to be the case.
    • How much something changedAfter a verb of rising or falling, by plus an amount says how big the change was, while to says where it ended up.
    • How odd! sketchHow followed by an adjective can stand alone as an exclamation, showing a strong reaction to how far something goes.
    • Hundred, thousand and million as base amountsHundred, thousand and million are base amounts that other numbers can scale.
    • Hundreds of, thousands of, millions ofA plural word for a large size, followed by of, gives a rough, large amount without saying exactly how many.

    I

    • I don't know who sketchA question can be reduced to its question word when the rest can be recovered from what came before.
    • I hope not sketchAfter a word like hope, not can stand in for a whole statement, the negative of something just raised in the conversation.
    • I think so sketchAfter a word like think, so can stand in for a whole statement mentioned in the conversation.
    • I wish I were taller sketchIn some wishing and imagining sentences, a form of be, were, appears with I or with he, she or it.
    • I've had it up to hereHave had it up to here says someone has reached the limit of what they can put up with, and with names the cause.
    • If only sketchIf only, standing alone, expresses a wish about something that isn't the case or might not be.
    • If this, then thatAn if-phrase sets out a condition, and the rest of the sentence says what follows from it.
    • Introducing someone with two namesSaying someone's name, pausing, then naming or describing someone else can introduce that second person or thing to the first.

    J

    • Jobs and roles without "the" or "a"Words like treasurer or captain can describe the role someone holds without a word like the or a in front.
    • Joining pairs of things after one verb sketchOne verb can serve two pairs of things joined by and, even though each pair isn't an ordinary phrase.
    • Joining things with and, or and butWords like and, or and but can link two or more equal parts, and none of the parts is in charge.
    • Judging a person's action with "kind of you"A sentence like It was kind of you to call judges a person by what they did or considered doing.
    • Judging the past with must have sketchA modal verb of judging, followed by have and a past participle, assesses an earlier situation from a later point of view.

    K

    • Words that describe thingsAdjectives are words like tall, wooden or happy that say what something is like, and they don't all behave alike.
    • Words that mean more than onePlural forms mark more than one thing, or a group of things taken together, and they trigger matching forms elsewhere in a sentence.
    • Words that open a noun phraseA noun phrase can start with a small word or phrase that marks it as a complete phrase and signals whether we mean a particular thing or not.
    • Words that stand in for a noun phraseA pronoun like he, they or it can take the place of a whole noun phrase and still work as the subject or object.
    • Worked our hardestA verb followed by a genitive and a superlative, as in worked our hardest, says the effort reached its highest point.

    Y

    • Years as namesA number like 1994 can name a particular year, working like a name rather than a count.
    • You're always doing that sketchBe plus always plus an -ing verb describes a habit as a series of repeated occurrences, often with the speaker's opinion in it.
