Linguist

Tag Question

tag-question-001 · semantic · provisional

Anchor:Clause[declarative | exclamative] + Tag:Clause[Head:VP[Head:V[auxiliary]]; Subject:NP[personal pronoun]; surface Auxiliary–Subject (+ not)] => Meaning:Question

Meaning

Meaning 1

Implements Question, meaning 1: A reversed-polarity tag presents a biased polar answer set whose salient positive member confirms the anchor proposition. A rising tag asks for verification or expresses doubt; a falling tag seeks acknowledgement and may be rhetorical. Exclamative anchors normally take this falling pattern because the truth of their proposition is presented as not at issue.

  • He is ill, isn't he?Positive declarative Anchor Clause plus negative Tag Clause; reversed polarityHuddleston, Rodney & Geoffrey K. Pullum (2002), The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language. Cambridge: CUP, Ch. 10 §5.1, p. 892, [3ia]
  • Everybody has read it, haven't they?Tag auxiliary agrees with plural tag Subject they, not directly with singular anchor Subject everybodyHuddleston, Rodney & Geoffrey K. Pullum (2002), The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language. Cambridge: CUP, Ch. 10 §5.1, p. 893, [7iv] discussion
  • What a mess I've made of things, haven't I?Exclamative Anchor Clause with the normally falling, acknowledgement-oriented reversed tagHuddleston, Rodney & Geoffrey K. Pullum (2002), The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language. Cambridge: CUP, Ch. 10 §5.2, p. 895

Meaning 2

Implements Question, meaning 1: A constant-polarity tag has the same polarity as its typically positive declarative anchor. With its characteristic slight rise, it commonly attributes the anchor content to the addressee and conveys acknowledgement, surprise, disapproval, reproach, or sarcasm rather than speaker doubt.

  • He is ill, is he?Positive declarative anchor and positive tag; constant polarityHuddleston, Rodney & Geoffrey K. Pullum (2002), The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language. Cambridge: CUP, Ch. 10 §5.1, p. 892, [3iia]
  • So you have forgotten your homework again, have you?Constant-polarity tag with reproachful or disapproving forceHuddleston, Rodney & Geoffrey K. Pullum (2002), The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language. Cambridge: CUP, Ch. 10 §5.2, p. 895, [12i]

Constraints

  • structural The construction combines an Anchor Clause with a supplementary Tag Clause; it does not turn their combination into a single Clause. Each Clause independently has the strict structure (Subject +) Head:VP.
  • syntactic The Anchor is a declarative or exclamative Clause. The Tag is a short closed-interrogative Clause with a personal-pronoun Subject and a VP headed by an auxiliary; subject–auxiliary inversion determines surface order.
  • syntactic If the relevant anchor Predicator is auxiliary-headed, the tag normally selects the same auxiliary lexeme; otherwise it selects do. The tag auxiliary normally has the anchor's tense.
  • syntactic The personal-pronoun tag Subject repeats a pronominal anchor Subject or is anaphorically selected for a non-pronominal one. Person–number agreement is controlled by the tag Subject, as Everybody has read it, haven't they? demonstrates.
  • morphological A negative tag permits synthetic auxiliary negation in ordinary style or analytic final not in more formal style.
  • semantic The tag has polarity opposite to the anchor in the frequent reversed-polarity variant and the same polarity in the constant-polarity variant; semantically negative anchors such as Few of them liked it select a positive reversed tag.
  • pragmatic Prosody is constructionally significant: a reversed rising tag normally requests verification, a reversed falling tag seeks acknowledgement, and a constant tag characteristically has a slight rise with attributional or emotive effects.

Dimensions

Form
A declarative or exclamative Anchor Clause followed by a supplementary short closed-interrogative Tag Clause; the tag has a personal-pronoun Subject and an auxiliary-headed VP, with surface subject–auxiliary inversion
Meaning
A normally biased polar question anchored to the proposition of the first Clause; polarity relation and prosody distinguish reversed- and constant-polarity interpretations
Function
Seeks verification, acknowledgement, agreement, or uptake of the anchor, with exclamative anchors normally supporting acknowledgement or rhetorical force rather than doubt
Distribution
Independent utterances in which a declarative or exclamative Clause licenses a following supplementary Tag Clause

Kind and projectibility

What recognizing it lets you predict

Recognizing Tag Question membership predicts an anaphoric personal-pronoun Subject, same-auxiliary selection or do-support, preservation of relevant tense, agreement with the tag Subject, a systematic reversed-or-constant polarity relation, and prosody-sensitive verification, acknowledgement, or emotive force

Evidence for the projection

The predictions jointly distinguish direct tags from arbitrary interrogative supplements: anchor Subject and Predicator determine pronoun, auxiliary, tense, and agreement, while polarity relation and contour predict the characteristic interpretations

Confidence: high.

  • Huddleston, Rodney & Geoffrey K. Pullum (2002), The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language. Cambridge: CUP, Ch. 10 §§5.1–5.2, pp. 892–95. cited · bears on projection Provides the direct tag-formation procedure and the polarity-by-prosody interpretation contrasts
  • Huddleston, Rodney & Geoffrey K. Pullum (2002), The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language. Cambridge: CUP, Ch. 10 §5, pp. 891–95. attestation · bears on stability The pronoun, auxiliary, tense, agreement, polarity, and prosody cluster recurs across declarative and exclamative anchors

Stable: the profile recurs. Provisional.

Core properties

  • Two-Clause anchor-plus-tag realization (declarative or exclamative Anchor Clause; supplementary closed-interrogative Tag Clause)
  • Auxiliary–pronoun tag form (personal-pronoun Subject; auxiliary-headed VP; surface subject–auxiliary inversion; do-support where required)
  • Anchor-dependent feature selection (anaphoric pronoun; same auxiliary lexeme or do; same tense; agreement with tag Subject)
  • Polarity–prosody interpretation (reversed versus constant polarity; rising verification; falling acknowledgement)

Peripheral properties

  • Emotive constant-polarity use (surprise; reproach; sarcasm)

Boundary cases

  • CGEL also attests tags with closed-interrogative and imperative anchors; those lie outside this declarative-or-exclamative-anchor constructional node.
  • Lovely day, isn't it? has a non-clausal anchor under the strict VP-headed Clause ontology and therefore is not an instance of this Clause-anchored node.
  • Interrogative parentheticals such as don't you think? are larger interrogative supplements, not auxiliary-plus-pronoun tags.
