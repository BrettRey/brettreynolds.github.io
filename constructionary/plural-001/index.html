Words that mean more than one

Plural forms mark more than one thing, or a group of things taken together, and they trigger matching forms elsewhere in a sentence.

Plural forms appear in nouns, pronouns and some other small words, not just nouns: cats, they and these all point to more than one. Some plurals name a group, as in Kennedys, or types rather than a mass, as in wines. Others mark recurring instances, as in headaches.

The plural form controls ordinary plural agreement, as in The scissors are blunt. Yet the description can be about one instrument. So the plural form tells us how the grammar treats the word, not strictly how many objects there are.

Examples

  • cats
  • they
  • these
  • Kennedys
  • wines
  • The scissors are blunt.
