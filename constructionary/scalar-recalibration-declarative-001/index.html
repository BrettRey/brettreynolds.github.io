Topping someone's claim with your own

You think you're tired? can set up a comparison in which the speaker's own case goes further on the same scale.

You think you're tired? I've been up for 36 hours. The first part looks like a remark about what you believe, but it can shift the scale. The speaker can be saying they're beyond tired, in a way that makes the listener's tiredness look smaller.

Whether the sentence really works this way depends on what follows. A follow-up that gives the speaker's evidence, such as the 36 hours, supplies the comparison. In a literal conversation about what someone believes, the same sentence need not do this. This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional.

Examples

  • You think you're tired? I've been up for 36 hours.
  • You think that's expensive. Wait till you see the repair bill.
  • You think you know stress. Try raising triplets.

Try it

Think of a time someone said they were really tired, cold or busy. How would you top that with You think ...?
