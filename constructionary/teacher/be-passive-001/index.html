Verbal be-Passive

Explains the be-passive as a way of realigning a verb's arguments so an active complement becomes the subject.

How to explain it

In the verbal be-passive, an internal complement of the active verb appears as the subject, and the participial VP lacks that complement. A by-phrase can realize the participant that would be the active subject, and the short passive usually leaves that participant unexpressed. The externalized complement can be a direct object, an indirect object, or the object of a preposition, as in the paper was referred to. The by-phrase can supply an experiencer when the active subject is an experiencer. The participial VP may have complements and verbal modifications. Only the construction with auxiliary be is covered here.

Terms

Long passive has a by-phrase, and short passive has none. Such clauses are sometimes said to be in the passive voice; this dictionary calls the version with be the verbal be-passive.

Examples

  • My surfboard was stolen by Pat.
  • My surfboard was stolen.
  • Her paper is referred to by everyone.

Contrasts to teach

  • Pat stole my surfboard.The active version.
  • My surfboard was stolen by Pat.Long passive; the active subject is the complement of by.
  • My surfboard was stolen.Short passive.

Watch for

  • A learner might think the by-phrase is required, when a short passive can leave that participant out.
  • A learner might expect only a direct object to become the subject, when a prepositional object can too (Her paper is referred to).

In class

Students turn active sentences into long passives and then short passives. They say which participant becomes the subject and where the original subject went.
