Cardinal Noun

Helps explain why a number word sometimes behaves like a noun, with its own plural and determiner, and sometimes doesn't.

How to explain it

A cardinal noun is a number word used as a noun. Proper-noun uses head bare noun phrases: Ten is even (the number itself) and Room 101 (a numerical label). Common-noun uses take determiners and plural forms: a ten, two tens, hundreds of people. They denote number entities, quantities, labels or numerical tokens, and they are not determiners. Contrast ten cards, where ten is a determinative and the noun is cards.

Terms

Cardinal number or numeral: the traditional names. This dictionary separates the cardinal determinative (ten cards) from the cardinal noun (Ten is even, two tens).

Examples

  • Ten is even.
  • a ten
  • Room 101
  • hundreds of people

Contrasts to teach

  • I have ten cards.Determinative use. Ten determines the noun cards.
  • I have two tens.Common-noun use. Tens is a plural noun with its own determiner.

Watch for

  • A learner might read Room 101 as one hundred and one rooms.
  • A learner might think ten in Ten is even is counting something, when it names the number.
  • A learner might expect ten cards to take a determiner before ten and a plural ending, as two tens does.

In class

Give students cards with ten cards, two tens, Ten is even and Room 101. They sort them into "number before a noun" and "number used as a noun", then say how they decided.
