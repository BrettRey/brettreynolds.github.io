Causative Have with a Bare Infinitival

Helps separate causative have from perfect have and from the affected-object pattern (had the roof replaced).

Early sketch. The linguist entry has one example so far and its evidence isn’t written up yet. Use this page as a starting point, not a settled account.

How to explain it

In causative have, the subject presents an event as arranged, instructed or brought about, and the noun phrase after have (Jo) is the one who carries it out, followed by a bare infinitival (the infinitive without to). I'll have Jo check the figures: Jo undertakes the checking. Compare perfect have (Jo has checked the figures), where have is an auxiliary, and affected have (have the figures checked), where the object is what is affected and no one is named as the doer.

Terms

Bare infinitival: an infinitival with no to; in this pattern it is check the figures. Perfect have is the auxiliary in has checked. Affected have is the pattern in had the roof replaced.

Examples

  • I'll have Jo check the figures.

Contrasts to teach

  • I'll have Jo check the figures.Causative have. Jo is the one who checks.
  • Jo has checked the figures.Perfect have. Has is an auxiliary.
  • I'll have the figures checked.Affected have. The figures are what is affected.

Watch for

  • A learner might read have in have Jo check as perfect have.
  • A learner might take Jo to be the thing affected, when Jo is the understood checker.
  • A learner might expect to before check, though the pattern has a bare infinitival.

In class

Write sentences on strips using have Jo check, have the figures checked and has checked. Students sort them by whether someone is arranged to act, something is affected, or it is the perfect have in has checked.
