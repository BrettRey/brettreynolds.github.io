Suppletive Adjectival Comparison

Useful for explaining why good and bad compare with different words instead of added endings.

Early sketch. The linguist entry has one example so far and its evidence isn’t written up yet. Use this page as a starting point, not a settled account.

How to explain it

Some adjectives realize grade through different stems: good has better and best, and bad has worse and worst. This is suppletion. Better behaves like a comparative even though it has no transparently added -er ending: it can introduce a than-phrase, as in better than the first. Better than and the best share a paradigm with plain good in the relevant readings.

Terms

Suppletion: forming grade forms from a different stem. Comparative and superlative are the traditional names for the two grades; this dictionary says grade.

Examples

  • This plan is better than the first.

Contrasts to teach

  • This plan is better than the first.Suppletive comparative of good.
  • This plan is longer than the first.Regular comparative with the -er suffix.

Watch for

  • A learner might look for the -er ending in better and not see it as a comparison form.
  • A learner might treat better and good as unrelated words, when they belong to one paradigm.

In class

Students write the three forms (plain, comparison, best) for good and bad and for a regular adjective like long. They mark which forms are built by adding an ending and which come from a different stem.
