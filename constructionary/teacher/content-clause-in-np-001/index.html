Noun with a Declarative Content Complement

Helps students separate a noun's content clause from a relative clause that looks the same.

How to explain it

A noun such as claim or fact can take a finite content clause that supplies the propositional content associated with it: the claim that Jo knew the code. The embedded clause (Jo knew the code) is complete and has no gap linked to claim. In the claim that Jo rejected, the string that Jo rejected, which opens like the content clause above, is an integrated relative clause with an object gap corresponding to claim. So a clause after a noun can be a content clause or a relative clause, and the difference is whether the clause has a gap linked to the noun.

Terms

Content clause: a clause giving propositional content. Integrated relative: a relative clause closely tied to its noun.

Examples

  • The claim that Jo knew the code surprised us.
  • The claim that Jo rejected surprised us.

Contrasts to teach

  • The rumour that Jo had left surprised us.Content clause. It gives the content of the rumour; nothing is missing.
  • The rumour that Jo spread surprised us.Integrated relative. The clause has an object gap linked to rumour.

Watch for

  • A learner might treat the claim that Jo rejected as a content clause, when Jo rejected the claim and the clause has a gap.
  • A learner might assume that-clauses after a noun all work alike, when the gap test separates two types.

In class

Give students noun phrases like the rumour that Jo had left and the rumour that Jo spread. They test each by asking whether the clause has a gap linked to the noun (what was spread?), then label content clause or relative clause.
