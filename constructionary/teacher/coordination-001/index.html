Coordination

Helps explain coordinations as headless groups of equal parts joined by and, or, but or nor.

How to explain it

A coordination is a non-headed constituent made up of two or more coordinates, optionally linked by a coordinator (and, or, but, nor). It isn't a phrase, because phrases are headed. No coordinate is the head, so the group's distribution follows from the coordinates jointly. Each coordinate could typically stand alone in the coordination's position. Coordinates can differ in category where their functions are alike, as in two thousand and twenty-seven. The meaning is the joint contribution of the coordinates: conjunction, disjunction, contrast or addition.

Terms

Traditional grammars call the linking words coordinating conjunctions. This grammar calls them coordinators and treats the group as a coordination rather than a phrase.

Examples

  • bread and butter
  • slowly but surely
  • two thousand and twenty-seven

Contrasts to teach

  • bread and butterCoordination of two nouns; no head.
  • fresh breadA phrase, with a head noun and a dependent.

Watch for

  • A learner might look for a head word in bread and butter, as in a noun phrase, when neither noun is the head.
  • A learner might think the coordinates must be the same category, when a similar function is enough.

In class

Students write short coordinations with and, or and but (nouns, adjectives, adverbs). For each, they test whether either part could stand alone in the same sentence, and note the meaning the coordinator adds.
