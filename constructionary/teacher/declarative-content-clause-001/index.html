Declarative Content Clause

Helps explain when that is optional or required before a declarative content clause.

How to explain it

A declarative content clause is a subordinate clause, optionally introduced by that, that presents propositional content as part of a larger construction. That can be optional after a verb such as say or believe, and in subject extraposition. It is required in ordinary fully expressed initial clausal subjects: That they were lying is now quite obvious. Whether the proposition is reported, presupposed or questioned depends on the matrix construction. The subordinate clause preserves declarative order. Don't confuse it with a relative clause: in Jo said that Kai read it the content clause has its own object, while in the book that Kai read the relative links an object gap to book.

Terms

Traditional grammars may call these that-clauses or noun clauses. This dictionary calls them declarative content clauses.

Examples

  • That they were lying is now quite obvious.
  • I believe (that) they were lying.

Contrasts to teach

  • I believe that they were lying.That introduces the content clause after a verb.
  • Jo said that Kai read it.Content clause with its own object.
  • the book that Kai readRelative clause linked to book through an object gap.

Watch for

  • A learner might assume that can be dropped in every position, when it is required in an ordinary initial clausal subject.
  • A learner might treat that as the same in Jo said that Kai read it and the book that Kai read, when one introduces a content clause and the other a relative.

In class

Give students matrix verbs (say, believe, know) and a short statement. They build sentences with and without that, then try the statement as the subject at the start, noting where that must stay.
