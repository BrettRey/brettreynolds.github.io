Teacher

Degree

Helps explain comparatives, superlatives and equatives as ways of placing something on a scale.

How to explain it

Degree constructions denote relative scale position. The comparative (-er or more/less: taller, more expensive), the superlative (-est or most/least: tallest, most expensive) and the equative (as tall as) encode scale relations. Measure and differential phrases denote intervals (much taller, two feet taller), and comparison classes supply a reference set or standard (taller than average, most expensive of all). The patterns apply to gradable adjectives and adverbs. This dictionary notes that degree frames are degraded with absolute adjectives and lists unique and dead as boundary items. The analysis is provisional.

Terms

This dictionary's term for the broader scale meaning is degree.

Examples

  • as tall as
  • taller than average
  • most expensive of all
  • much taller
  • two feet taller

Contrasts to teach

  • widerA simple comparative.
  • two centimetres widerThe same scale with an explicit differential.
  • as wide asAn equality comparison that supplies a standard.

Watch for

  • A learner might expect degree frames to work with any adjective, when absolute adjectives such as unique and dead are boundary items.
  • A learner might treat much taller and two feet taller as the same kind of difference, when this dictionary treats much taller as a relative interval and two feet taller as a measured interval.

In class

Students measure or rank classroom objects, then write sentences with a comparative, a superlative, an equative and a measure phrase. They swap and check that each sentence matches the facts.
