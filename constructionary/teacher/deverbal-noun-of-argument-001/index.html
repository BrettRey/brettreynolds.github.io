Deverbal Noun Of-Argument

Helps explain how an of-phrase after a deverbal noun carries a participant of the underlying verb.

How to explain it

In an NP with a deverbal noun head, the of-PP is a complement that expresses a semantic argument of the underlying verb, such as the entity that arrived or the entity destroyed. The noun and the construction select the role. With a transitive source, a genitive can express the agent and the of-phrase the affected participant (the enemy's destruction of the city). The of-complement can help identify the event, and an indefinite determiner remains possible (a repainting of the hall). This dictionary notes that the genitive form alternates with the of-form (the failure of WM and WM's failure), with the genitive preferred for agents and the of-PP for non-agentive arguments.

Terms

Traditional grammars may call such nouns nominalizations or verbal nouns.

Examples

  • the arrival of the guests
  • the destruction of the city
  • the enemy's destruction of the city
  • a repainting of the hall

Contrasts to teach

  • the failure of Washington MutualThe of-phrase names the entity that failed.
  • Washington Mutual's failureThe genitive alternative for the same participant.

Watch for

  • A learner might read the of-phrase in the destruction of the city as the one doing the destroying, when it is the thing destroyed.
  • A learner might think a noun with an of-argument must be definite, though a repainting of the hall has an indefinite determiner.

In class

Give students event nouns (arrival, destruction, death, failure). They add an of-phrase to each and say who or what it names and what role that participant has in the event.
