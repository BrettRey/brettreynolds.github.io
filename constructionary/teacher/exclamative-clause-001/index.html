Exclamative Clause

Helps students recognize what and how exclamatives as verb-headed clauses, and tell them from questions and from phrase-only exclamations.

How to explain it

An exclamative clause is a clause headed by a VP (normally tensed) and marked by an exclamative phrase based on what or how. It presents a proposition as holding to a remarkable degree or in a remarkable way, and characteristically makes an exclamatory statement. The exclamative phrase is normally initial and the Subject precedes the Predicator, though subject postposing and subject-auxiliary inversion are possible. It occurs in main clauses and as a subordinate content clause. Clause type isn't the same as force: exclamation is a wider pattern of use. Phrase-only What a disaster! isn't a headed clause in this dictionary; see the clause-analogue entry. What and how can also mark open interrogatives, and role, order, meaning and context separate the two.

Terms

Exclamative clause is CGEL's term. Sentences like these are sometimes called exclamatory sentences.

Examples

  • What a fine player she is!
  • How generous you are!
  • I realised what a bargain it was.
  • How happy would he be if he could see her once more!

Watch for

  • A learner might treat any sentence beginning with what or how as an exclamative, when the same words can start an open interrogative.
  • A learner might take Subject-Predicator order as required, though the how-happy example shows a licensed inversion.
  • A learner might count What a disaster! as a verb-headed clause, when this dictionary treats it as phrase-only.

In class

Students get strips with what/how sentences, some exclamatives and some questions (What did she play? alongside What a fine player she is!). They sort them and mark where the verb and subject fall.
