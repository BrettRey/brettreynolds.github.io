Finite Do Support

Helps explain when English adds finite do, in questions and negatives, and when an existing auxiliary does the job.

How to explain it

Finite do support supplies a finite auxiliary when the construction (inversion or clause negation) requires one and the positive clause has a lexical verb. The tense is expressed on do and the verb is plain: Did you check the hinge? If a finite modal is available, it occupies the auxiliary position directly: Can you check the hinge? Likewise Has Jo left? inverts the auxiliary already present, and Jo hasn't left uses the existing auxiliary, whereas Did Jo leave? and Jo didn't leave insert do. Imperative do also occurs before be. This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional, with medium confidence.

Terms

Also called do-support. The finite auxiliary that inverts or hosts negation is sometimes called the operator.

Examples

  • Did you check the hinge?
  • Can you check the hinge?

Contrasts to teach

  • Did you check the hinge?No other auxiliary, so do is supplied and check is plain.
  • Can you check the hinge?The modal already occupies the finite auxiliary position.
  • Has Jo left?The existing auxiliary inverts, so no do.

Watch for

  • A learner might put the tense on both did and check.
  • A learner might add did when a modal or another auxiliary is already present.

In class

Students get statements with plain verbs (Jo left, You checked) and with auxiliaries (Jo has left, You can check). They turn each into a question and a negative, and sort the results by whether do was added.
