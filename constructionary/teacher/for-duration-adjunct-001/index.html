Duration with For

Helps explain for-phrases that measure how long, and how they differ from in-phrases that measure time to completion.

Early sketch. The linguist entry has one example so far and its evidence isn’t written up yet. Use this page as a starting point, not a settled account.

How to explain it

A for-PP with a measure NP of time is an adjunct that measures how long a state, process or maintained result holds: Jo stayed outside for fifteen minutes. Wrote for an hour measures time spent writing, while wrote the letter in an hour measures elapsed time to completion. With a normally punctual predicate, the adjunct can favour a repeated-event or resulting-state construal where context supports it. This dictionary treats it as a specialization of the PP and marks it provisional, with medium confidence.

Terms

For-phrases like this are sometimes called duration adverbials.

Examples

  • Jo stayed outside for fifteen minutes.

Contrasts to teach

  • Jo wrote for an hour.Measures time spent writing.
  • Jo wrote the letter in an hour.Measures time elapsed to completion.

Watch for

  • A learner might mix up for an hour (time spent) and in an hour (time to finish).
  • A learner might read for fifteen minutes with a quick action as one long event, when context can favour a repeated-event reading.

In class

Give students a list of activities, some open-ended (reading, waiting) and some with an endpoint (writing a letter, cleaning a room). They add for or in plus a time and explain what each phrase measures.
