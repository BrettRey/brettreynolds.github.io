Anaphoric Adjective in a Fused-Head NP

Helps explain an adjective standing in for a noun recovered from context, and how it differs from the wealthy.

Early sketch. The linguist entry has one example so far and its evidence isn’t written up yet. Use this page as a starting point, not a settled account.

How to explain it

In an anaphoric fused-head NP, a determiner plus an adjective phrase distinguishes a referent while the descriptive noun is recoverable from context: I took the dry cloth and left the damp. The inferred kind comes from cloth, and agreement follows the recovered nominal. The human-class reading of the wealthy instead needs no such antecedent. This dictionary treats it as a specialization of the fused-head construction, related to the adjective NP and to pro-nominal one, and marks it provisional, with medium confidence.

Terms

This is sometimes described as an adjective used as a noun. Fused head is this grammar's term for an NP whose head function is carried by another constituent, here the adjective.

Examples

  • I took the dry cloth and left the damp.
  • She chose the red scarf and I chose the blue.

Contrasts to teach

  • I took the dry cloth and left the damp.The noun is recovered from cloth.
  • I took the dry cloth and left the damp one.Here one overtly stands in for cloth.

Watch for

  • A learner might treat the damp as a noun with no link to the earlier cloth.
  • A learner might expect the wealthy to need an earlier noun, when its human-class reading doesn't.

In class

Students get sentences with two contrasting things (the dry cloth and the damp cloth). They rewrite them without repeating the noun and underline what supplies the missing noun.
