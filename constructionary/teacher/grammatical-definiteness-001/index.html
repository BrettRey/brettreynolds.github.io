Grammatical Definiteness

Frames definiteness as identifiability, reference scope and discourse status, with known mismatches.

How to explain it

Grammatical definiteness is a semantic feature implemented through nominal determination. This dictionary distinguishes identifiability (the cat against a cat), reference scope (the students against some students; the winner against a winner) and discourse status (given against new). Form is typically realized by the, this, that, dependent genitive pronouns, genitive NPs or bare proper nouns. Familiarity, unique reference and structural diagnostics need separate predictions: shared knowledge can make the only exit identifiable at first mention, and the in take the train can mark a lexicalized activity without distinguishing a specific train. This dictionary marks this analysis as provisional.

Terms

This dictionary describes the forms as typically deital determiners (a function, filled by the, this, that, dependent genitive pronouns or genitive noun phrases) or bare proper nouns. The and a are determinatives (often called articles), and their function in the NP is determiner.

Examples

  • the/a cat
  • the students/some students
  • the/a winner

Contrasts to teach

  • the/a bookGiven versus new.
  • John/some personA name is inherently identifiable; some person is not.

Watch for

  • A learner might think the requires that the listener has seen the thing before, when shared knowledge or a lexicalized activity can also license it.
  • A learner might take the train in take the train as pointing to one specific train.

In class

Give students short texts with the and a. They underline each, say whether the referent is new, given or identifiable from shared knowledge, and discuss any that don't fit.
