Have Had It Up to Here

An idiom for reaching the limit of tolerance, with an optional with-phrase naming the cause.

How to explain it

The idiom is perfect have plus had it up to here, optionally followed by a with-phrase: We have had it up to here with typical politicians. It presents the speaker or a reported experiencer as having reached a limit of tolerance. Here locates a figurative threshold, which a gesture can give a physical locus. The with-phrase identifies the source of frustration. A distance phrase does not freely substitute for here with the same idiomatic reading, and a literal liquid-level description lacks the experiencer and tolerance interpretation. This dictionary says the core expression has a largely fixed lexical form.

Terms

Idiom is the usual word. This dictionary calls the with-phrase's referent the stimulus.

Examples

  • We have had it up to here with typical politicians.
  • I've had it up to here.

Watch for

  • A learner might read it as a literal measurement of where something reaches.
  • A learner might replace here with a distance phrase and expect the same idiomatic reading.
  • A learner might leave out the perfect have, though this dictionary gives that as the canonical form.

In class

Give students a list of annoyances (a noisy neighbour, a late bus). They write I've had it up to here with ... for each, then compare a literal sentence about water height to the idiom.
