Heavy Complement Postposing

For advanced classes, a word-order pattern where a long complement follows a short dependent of the same verb.

Early sketch. The linguist entry has one example so far and its evidence isn’t written up yet. Use this page as a starting point, not a settled account.

How to explain it

In heavy complement postposing, a verb has a short dependent (to the chair) followed by a relatively heavy NP complement (a folder containing all the amended exhibits). The heavy NP is placed after the lighter dependent. Substituting a short pronoun gives strongly degraded handed to the chair it. Relative length and information status affect the postposed order. This dictionary relates the pattern to transitive verbs with a recipient to-phrase and marks the analysis as provisional.

Terms

Heavy NP shift is the textbook name. This dictionary calls the pattern heavy complement postposing.

Examples

  • She handed to the chair a folder containing all the amended exhibits.

Watch for

  • A learner might treat the postposed order as free for any complement, when this dictionary says relative length and information status affect it.
  • A learner might place a short pronoun last, as in handed to the chair it, which this dictionary calls strongly degraded.

In class

Give students a sentence with a short object and a long object after a verb. They try both orders aloud, then replace the long phrase with a pronoun and notice which order resists.
