Imperative Clause

Defines the imperative by its grammar, with optional subject and special do-support, and separates it from directive force.

How to explain it

An imperative clause has a plain-form VP head and an optional second-person subject; the subject is normally understood as second person, but overt you and quantified subjects are licensed (You be careful; Everybody stand up). Supportive do is required for verbal negation and emphatic polarity, even with be (Don't be late). An overt subject precedes a positive VP but normally follows don't in a negative imperative. Imperatives are normally main clauses, and English lacks a general subordinate imperative construction. Directive force is characteristic, not necessary: Sleep well is a wish, while interrogative, declarative, non-finite and non-headed expressions can convey directives without becoming imperatives. This dictionary marks its analysis as provisional.

Terms

Imperative is the clause type; directive is the use. Plain form is the CGEL name for what traditional grammars call the base form.

Examples

  • Be generous.
  • You be careful.
  • Everybody stand up.
  • Sleep well.
  • Don't be late.

Contrasts to teach

  • Be generous.Subjectless; the subject is understood as second person.
  • You be careful.Overt second-person subject.
  • Sleep well.An imperative clause used as a wish.

Watch for

  • A learner might think every imperative gives an order, when Sleep well is a wish.
  • A learner might use isn't or aren't to negate be in an imperative, when this dictionary requires do-support in Don't be late.
  • A learner might treat any directive as an imperative clause, though other forms can convey directives.

In class

Give students a mixed set of directives, wishes and requests. They mark which are imperatives, using this page's examples as models, and which are other forms that convey a directive.
