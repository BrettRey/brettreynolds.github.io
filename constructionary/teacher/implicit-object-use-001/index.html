Teacher

Indefinite Object Omission

Helps explain why some verbs are fine without an object, while a near-synonym may need one.

Early sketch. The linguist entry has one example so far and its evidence isn’t written up yet. Use this page as a starting point, not a settled account.

How to explain it

A verb that can take an object is used without one, leaving a usual participant nonspecific while presenting the activity or event itself: in Have you eaten yet? the food is left open. The implied food need not have been mentioned. This dictionary says leaving the relevant object unexpressed is natural for eat, even when plausible near-synonyms such as consume strongly favour a stated object. Availability depends on the verb and its use. This dictionary relates the pattern to simple intransitive and monotransitive VPs and marks the analysis as provisional.

Terms

Sometimes called object omission or the intransitive use of a transitive verb.

Examples

  • Have you eaten yet?

Contrasts to teach

  • Jo ate lunch.Object stated.
  • Jo ate.Object left unspecified.

Watch for

  • A learner might assume any near-synonym of eat can drop its object, when consume strongly favours a stated one.
  • A learner might look for a specific food in Have you eaten yet?, when the food is left unspecified.

In class

Give students pairs of verbs with similar meanings (eat and consume). They test each with and without an object, record which works, and discuss what is left unspecified.
