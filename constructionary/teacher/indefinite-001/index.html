Indefinite Construction

Explains that indefinite noun phrases don't mark the referent as identifiable, and that the same form covers several uses.

How to explain it

This dictionary describes indefinite reference as referring without presenting the referent as already uniquely identifiable. The speaker may know the identity, and the construction doesn't require the hearer to resolve it from prior information. It lists four uses: reference without presenting the referent as already identifiable (a dog, some students), a new discourse entity (there is a solution, first mention), an arbitrary instance where the choice is left open (take a card), and type membership (is a teacher, is some kind of expert). Choice and permitted quantity are separate. The article alone doesn't rule out specific reference or supply an any-member permission. A student phoned can refer to a particular student known to the speaker, while A student can enter allows a general reading. This analysis is provisional.

Terms

Traditionally, a and an are called indefinite articles. In CGEL terms they're determinatives, and the noun phrase can include bare plural or non-count forms. The specific and non-specific readings are the textbook names for the contrast between a particular referent and a general one.

Examples

  • a dog
  • there is a solution
  • take a card
  • is a teacher
  • some students

Contrasts to teach

  • A student phoned.Can refer to a particular student known to the speaker.
  • A student can enter.Allows a general reading.

Watch for

  • A learner might assume that a means the speaker doesn't know which one is meant.
  • A learner might read take a card as allowing any number of cards.
  • A learner might read is a teacher as introducing a new person rather than naming a category the person belongs to.

In class

Give students short sentences with a, some and bare plurals. They label each as introducing something new, leaving a choice open, or naming a category, and note any sentence that could fit more than one.
