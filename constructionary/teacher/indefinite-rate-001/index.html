Rate with an Indefinite Denominator

Covers rates like fifty miles an hour, where a or an plus a unit expresses a ratio.

How to explain it

The pattern pairs a quantity or frequency expression with a noun phrase made of a or an and a singular unit noun. It expresses a ratio: a numerator over a unit denominator. Fifty miles an hour gives a speed even if the motion lasts only a minute, whereas fifty miles every hour can assert successive hourly totals. The same form supports price, salary and frequency. The second noun phrase is interpreted as a unit denominator, and the construction describes a rate without asserting one complete occurrence of that unit. A per-phrase can often express the same ratio and is more flexible with stacked or complex denominators.

Terms

Sometimes called a distributive use of a/an. The a/an here is the indefinite article in traditional terms, a determinative in CGEL terms.

Examples

  • He was going at 50 miles an hour.
  • It costs $20 a yard.
  • The nurse checked it twice a day.

Contrasts to teach

  • He was going at 50 miles an hour.A rate, whatever the length of the trip.
  • He drove 50 miles every hour.Can assert successive hourly totals.

Watch for

  • A learner might read an hour in fifty miles an hour as one particular hour.
  • A learner might assume the trip must last a whole hour for fifty miles an hour to be true.

In class

Students collect rates from signs, menus and timetables (price a kilo, trains an hour). They say each aloud with a or an, then restate it with per.
