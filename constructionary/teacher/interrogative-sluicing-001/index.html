Interrogative Sluicing

Explains reduced questions that keep only the question phrase, as in I don't know who.

Early sketch. The linguist entry has one example so far and its evidence isn’t written up yet. Use this page as a starting point, not a settled account.

How to explain it

The pattern is an interrogative phrase with the rest of the clause unexpressed. It recovers a question with only the questioned part overt. In Someone moved the keys, but I don't know who, the mover role is recovered from the preceding clause. A more explicit interrogative phrase such as which person can likewise remain overt. This dictionary relates the pattern to open interrogative clauses and cites CGEL and Goldberg and Perek (2019). It is provisional.

Terms

Sluicing is the standard name in the linguistics literature. Some textbooks call this a reduced or elliptical wh-question.

Examples

  • Someone moved the keys, but I don’t know who.
  • Mia left early, but I don't know why.

Contrasts to teach

  • Someone moved the keys, but I don't know who.Only the question word is overt.
  • Someone moved the keys, but I don't know who moved the keys.The full question, spelled out.

Watch for

  • A learner might think I don't know who is incomplete.
  • A learner might not see that the earlier clause supplies the missing part of the question.

In class

Students write sentences that begin with someone or something, then add but I don't know followed by a question word. They write out the full question each one stands for.
