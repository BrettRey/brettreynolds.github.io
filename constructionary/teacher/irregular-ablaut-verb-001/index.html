Vowel Alternation in Irregular Verb Inflection

Covers verbs whose past and participle forms are marked by a stem vowel change, as in sing, sang, sung.

Early sketch. The linguist entry has one example so far and its evidence isn’t written up yet. Use this page as a starting point, not a settled account.

How to explain it

This dictionary describes selected verb stems with present, preterite and participial vowel alternants. The changes are lexicalized and distinguish verbal inflectional forms. For sing, sang and sung correspond to different slots. The perfect selects the past participle, here sung. Other verbs can share one form across those slots, so the exact mapping is lexical, and each verb has to be learned. This dictionary relates the pattern to regular -ed past tense and to the perfect with have. It is provisional.

Terms

Traditional grammars sometimes call these strong verbs, a label that may be broader than this pattern, and ablaut is a textbook word for stem-vowel change of this kind. This dictionary calls the pattern vowel alternation in irregular verb inflection.

Examples

  • Jo sang yesterday and has sung twice today.

Contrasts to teach

  • Jo sang yesterday.The preterite form.
  • Jo has sung twice today.The past participle after has.

Watch for

  • A learner might mix up which of sang and sung fills which slot.
  • A learner might expect every verb to add -ed for its past.

In class

Students list verbs with a vowel change and write each in a pair of sentences, one with the past form and one with has and the participle. They note any verb that uses one form for both.
