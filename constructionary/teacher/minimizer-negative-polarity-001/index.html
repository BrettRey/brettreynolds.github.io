Minimizer under Non-Affirmative Polarity

Explains why a minimal unit under negation reads as "not even a little".

Early sketch. The linguist entry has one example so far and its evidence isn’t written up yet. Use this page as a starting point, not a settled account.

How to explain it

A minimizer is a minimal quantity expression that, in a negative or otherwise licensed context, emphasizes the absence of any relevant amount. In They didn't budge an inch, the negation denies even the smallest unit, so the whole sentence is emphatic. Outside such a context, an inch can simply denote a literal distance. Teach it as a pattern tied to a context, not as a property of the noun alone. This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional and the evidence as medium confidence; its one example is a constructed illustration.

Terms

Minimizer is the term used here. Negative polarity item is a textbook name for expressions that need a licensing context; this dictionary links the pattern to polarity-sensitive ever.

Examples

  • They didn't budge an inch.

Contrasts to teach

  • They didn't budge an inch.Emphatic denial of any movement.
  • The window opened an inch.A literal distance, with no emphatic reading.

Watch for

  • A learner might read budge an inch as a measurement of how far something moved.
  • A learner might expect the emphatic reading of an inch in a plain positive sentence, when this dictionary ties it to negative or otherwise licensed contexts.

In class

Write They didn't budge an inch on the board. Ask what the speaker is claiming about movement. Then show The window opened an inch and have students explain how the same words mean something different in each sentence.
