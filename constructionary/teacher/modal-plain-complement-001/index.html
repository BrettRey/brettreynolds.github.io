Central Modal with a Plain-Form Complement

Helps explain why verbs after modals stay in the plain form and why modals have no -s form.

Early sketch. The linguist entry has one example so far and its evidence isn’t written up yet. Use this page as a starting point, not a settled account.

How to explain it

A central modal combines with a plain-form verb phrase. Might selects plain have in Jo might have missed the train, and the modal has no third-person singular suffix. This dictionary adds that ordinary central modals cannot occupy the complement slot of perfect have: the modal comes first, and have follows it. The pattern is a specialization of the general verb phrase. Teachers may want to pair it with the perfect, which this dictionary lists as related.

Terms

Plain form is sometimes called the base form or bare infinitive. Central modal is the CGEL name; modal auxiliary is sometimes used in textbooks.

Examples

  • Jo might have missed the train.
  • Kim must have left early.
  • They will call tomorrow.

Contrasts to teach

  • Jo might have missed the train.Modal first, then plain have.
  • Jo has missed the train.Perfect have with a third-person singular form, and no modal.

Watch for

  • A learner might add -s to a modal after he or she, since other verbs take it.
  • A learner might use have or has before the modal rather than after it.
  • A learner might use a verb form with -ing or -ed after might instead of the plain form.

In class

Give students subjects (I, she, they) and modals (might, must, will). They build sentences with a plain verb, then with have and a past participle, and compare the endings on each verb.
