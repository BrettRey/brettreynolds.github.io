Common Noun

Teaches the basic noun category, and how it differs from the proper noun.

How to explain it

A common noun denotes a class or category of entities. It is the head of a nominal, supplies descriptive content for reference, takes number and case inflection, and supports the count and non-count distinction. Dog and city are this dictionary's examples. The pattern provides a nominal description usable with ordinary determination when the count and number profile permits it. Identification alone doesn't make a noun proper: the tallest building can identify a unique referent while keeping a common noun as its head. Compare with the proper noun, a parallel noun category.

Terms

Common noun is the traditional term and the one used here. Countable and uncountable nouns are sometimes called count and non-count nouns; this dictionary prefers non-count.

Examples

  • dog
  • city

Contrasts to teach

  • the tallest buildingIdentifies one building, but the head word building is still a common noun.

Watch for

  • A learner might treat a noun as proper whenever a phrase points to one individual, such as the tallest building.
  • A learner might assume every common noun can follow a cardinal number, though this dictionary says the count and number profile has to permit it.

In class

Students list five common nouns from the classroom. For each, they say whether it can follow a, much or a cardinal number, then compare lists with a partner.
