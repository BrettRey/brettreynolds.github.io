Non-count Noun

Teaches the count and non-count distinction, with flexible cases such as two coffees as context-dependent shifts.

How to explain it

A non-count noun has non-count morphosyntax and, by default, a substance or aggregate construal. Diagnostics: it rejects cardinals, takes much and less, and defaults to singular agreement. In much coffee, coffee has non-count distribution; Two coffees permits a count reading because the context supplies portions or varieties. The individual lexical form alone doesn't decide the syntactic use, and speakers vary on how flexible particular words are. Near the boundary are beer, chicken, paper, furniture, cattle and police. This dictionary treats the category as a provisional profile.

Terms

Mass noun and uncountable noun are traditional names for non-count nouns. This dictionary uses non-count.

Examples

  • water
  • rice
  • much coffee

Contrasts to teach

  • much coffeeNon-count distribution with much.
  • Two coffees, please.A count reading; context supplies cups or kinds.

Watch for

  • A learner might assume a noun that names a substance can't ever appear with a number, though context can supply portions or varieties.
  • A learner might take chicken, paper or beer to belong to one category only, when this dictionary lists them near the boundary.

In class

Students sort a list of food and drink words (water, rice, coffee, beer, paper). They test each with much, then with a number. Discuss which words shift when the context supplies cups, portions or kinds.
