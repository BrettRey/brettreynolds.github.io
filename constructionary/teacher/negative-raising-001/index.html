Inferred Embedded Negation under a Matrix Negative

For advanced classes, a main-clause negative that is interpreted as negating the content clause.

How to explain it

With a matrix negation, an opinion or expectation predicate and a content clause, the interpretation often shifts to a negative in the subordinate content. I don't think the shop is open is naturally read as approximating I think the shop isn't open. The two differ if the speaker lacks an opinion either way, as the comparison with I don't believe the gate is open shows. The availability of the reading depends on the matrix predicate and context. This dictionary treats the effect as an interpretive inference, not a change in the grammar of the clause.

Terms

Negative raising or neg-raising is the traditional name for this effect.

Examples

  • I don't think the shop is open.
  • I don't believe the gate is open, but I have no view on whether it is closed.

Contrasts to teach

  • I don't think the shop is open.Naturally read as I think the shop isn't open.
  • I don't believe the gate is open, but I have no view on whether it is closed.The continuation cancels the ordinary interpretation.

Watch for

  • A learner might read I don't think as meaning I think not, though this dictionary notes the two differ when the speaker has no opinion.
  • A learner might assume any verb allows the negative reading, when this dictionary says it depends on the predicate and context.

In class

Students read pairs such as I don't think ... and I think ... isn't ... aloud and judge whether the meanings are the same. Then they add a continuation that says the speaker has no view, and discuss the difference.
