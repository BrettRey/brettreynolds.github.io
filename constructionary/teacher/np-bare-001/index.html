Bare NP Construction

Helps explain when a noun phrase can appear without a determiner and why the reading depends on the construction.

How to explain it

A bare NP consists of a nominal with no overt determiner. The dictionary lists uses for non-count substances (Water is essential), bare plurals (Dogs are loyal; Those are bears; Dogs barked all night), proper names, activities, times, places and abstract concepts. A singular count noun can be bare only in restricted roles or naming uses (Lee stayed president, Jo became chair), whereas bare plurals and non-count nouns can normally be arguments. Not having a determiner doesn't fix the interpretation: kind, classification, or an episodic set all depend on the surrounding construction. The dictionary marks this account as provisional.

Terms

Sometimes called a determinerless NP or a bare noun phrase. Bare plural is the usual name for dogs with no determiner.

Examples

  • Water is essential.
  • Dogs are loyal.
  • Dogs barked all night.
  • Jo became chair.
  • Monday works.

Contrasts to teach

  • Dogs barked all night.Bare plural, with a set of dogs supplied by context.
  • Those dogs barked all night.Same nominal with an overt demonstrative, selecting a contextually identifiable set.

Watch for

  • A learner might think a missing determiner means the sentence is incomplete.
  • A learner might use the same bare noun for the incumbent of a role as an ordinary argument, though the dictionary notes that such a noun needs a determiner there.
  • A learner might read Dogs are loyal and Dogs barked all night as having the same kind of meaning.

In class

Give students a set of sentences with bare noun phrases (water, dogs, Monday, president). They sort them into kind, set, time, role and substance, and discuss how they decided.
