Reciprocal Anaphor

Explains each other and one another as reciprocal anaphors and contrasts them with reflexives.

How to explain it

A reciprocal anaphor relates members of an antecedent group to other members of that group. In Kim and Pat defended each other, the ordinary reading is that Kim defended Pat and Pat defended Kim. Each other and one another can occur as genitive determiners, as in each other's parents, whereas reflexives lack corresponding genitives. For larger groups, weaker readings occur, such as relations only between neighbours in a line, so full pairwise reciprocity is too strong in some cases. A reflexive links a pronoun to its own referent, while a reciprocal relates group members. The dictionary marks this account as provisional.

Terms

Reciprocal pronoun is the usual name for each other and one another. This grammar groups them as a reciprocal anaphor.

Examples

  • Kim and Pat defended each other.
  • They blamed each other's parents.

Contrasts to teach

  • Kim and Pat defended each other.Reciprocal; each defends the other.
  • Sue defended herself.Reflexive; the object is the same person as the subject.

Watch for

  • A learner might use each other when the object is the same person as the subject.
  • A learner might think a reciprocal means every member relates to every other member, when larger groups can have weaker readings.

In class

Students stand in pairs and say what they did to each other (high-fived, thanked). Then groups of five say what happened and decide whether each person really did it to every other person.
