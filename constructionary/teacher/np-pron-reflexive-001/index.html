Direct-Object Reflexive Anaphor

Explains the basic direct-object reflexive and its contrast with an ordinary object pronoun.

How to explain it

In the simple pattern, a reflexive pronoun serves as direct object with a local subject antecedent: Sue defended herself. The personal pronoun in reflexive form has the same referent as the subject of the verb. In the ordinary transitive reading, Sue defended her is about Sue defending someone else. The reflexive form tracks the antecedent's person and number, so changing it to the plain accusative usually removes the local co-reference reading. The pattern here doesn't cover emphatic reflexives or those in picture noun phrases, which can have different licensing. The dictionary marks this account as provisional.

Terms

Reflexive pronoun is the traditional name. Her in Sue defended her is an ordinary personal pronoun in accusative form.

Examples

  • Sue defended herself.
  • The players congratulated themselves.

Contrasts to teach

  • Sue defended herself.The object is Sue, the subject.
  • Sue defended her.The ordinary reading is about someone else.

Watch for

  • A learner might use her for the subject's own referent, as in Sue defended her, meaning Sue.
  • A learner might choose a reflexive that doesn't match its antecedent in person and number.

In class

Give students a set of sentences with a blank after a transitive verb. They fill in either a reflexive or a plain pronoun and say who each one refers to.
