Fused-head Numerative

Shows number words standing alone for a whole noun phrase, and separates that use from bare numbers that name numbers.

How to explain it

A numerative can fill a whole NP by itself, with the noun recovered from context. This dictionary calls this a fused determiner-head: one word has both the determiner function and the head function. A partitive of-complement may follow. Stand-alone shape doesn't settle the analysis: in Lee bought thirty books and I bought forty, forty fills the object NP and recovers books, but in Forty is even the bare form can name the number. The fragment Two, answering How many thousand?, substitutes for the factor in two thousand and isn't a fused head. This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional.

Terms

Fused determiner-head is the term used here. Traditional grammar might describe these as numerals used without a noun.

Examples

  • She bought twenty books, but I bought twenty-two.
  • How many thousand? Two.
  • I bought twenty-two of the books.

Contrasts to teach

  • Lee bought thirty books and I bought forty.Fused head, noun recovered from context.
  • Forty is even.The bare form can name the number instead.

Watch for

  • A learner might treat the fragment answer Two, given to a question about thousands, as a fused head, when it replaces only the factor.
  • A learner might read Forty in Forty is even as standing for forty things, when it can name the number.

In class

Write short pairs of sentences where the second leaves out the counted noun. Students underline the number word and say what noun the reader recovers.
