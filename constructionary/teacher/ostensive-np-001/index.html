Ostensive NP

Shows how a single noun phrase can serve as an attention-directing utterance, with two packagings that differ in determination.

How to explain it

An ostensive NP is an extra-clausal NP that directs attention to its referent. It isn't an argument of any predicator, and it resists embedding, tags and question formation. It has two packagings: vocative (addressee-directed, securing the channel) and directive-ostensive (environment-directed, securing joint attention). Directive-ostensives keep ordinary determination (a snake, the bus), while vocative function licenses restricted bare common-noun NPs (Mum, driver, officer, buddy). Taxi! is ambiguous between the two, and reminders such as Your keys! sit between alerting and addressing. This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional.

Terms

Vocative is the traditional name for the addressee-directed use. Directive-ostensive is this dictionary's term for the environment-directed use.

Examples

  • Mom! (calling)
  • A snake!
  • Taxi!

Contrasts to teach

  • The bus!Directive-ostensive, with ordinary determination.
  • Mum!Vocative, a bare common noun allowed in calling.
  • There's a fire!A clausal alert, which has the function but not this form.

Watch for

  • A learner might think a vocative such as Mum needs a determiner like a or the.
  • A learner might take Taxi! as having one fixed use, when it is ambiguous between the two.
  • A learner might treat a lone noun phrase as an interjection, when it keeps its referential behaviour.

In class

Students list short shouts from a playground or street scene. They decide for each whether it calls a person (vocative) or points something out (directive-ostensive), and mark the ambiguous ones.
