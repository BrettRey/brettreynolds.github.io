Subjectless Participial Supplement

Explains -ing and -ed phrases added to a main clause, and how their understood subject is recovered.

How to explain it

A subjectless participial supplement is a nonfinite phrase (V-ing or V-en plus dependents) anchored to a main clause. It adds a circumstance, and its understood subject is normally recovered from the anchor: Jo is the hearer in the first example, and Lee is the startled one in the second. A separate overt subject would support an absolute supplement instead. Some fixed discourse adjuncts use contextual subjects. This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional.

Terms

Participial phrase is the traditional name. This dictionary calls the pattern a supplement because it's added to the clause and not required by it.

Examples

  • Hearing footsteps, Jo switched off the light.
  • Lee shut the door, startled by the noise.

Contrasts to teach

  • Hearing footsteps, Jo switched off the light.The understood hearer is Jo, recovered from the main clause.
  • Jo having left, Lee switched off the light.A separate overt subject supports an absolute supplement instead.

Watch for

  • A learner might look for the understood subject inside the phrase, when it is recovered from the main clause.
  • A learner might expect the subject of startled by the noise to be the door, when this dictionary takes it to be Lee.

In class

Students underline the participial phrase in each of several sentences and circle who or what it is understood to be about. They then say how they know.
