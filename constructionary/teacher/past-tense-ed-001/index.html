Past Tense -ed

Explains the -ed ending as more than past time, covering hypothetical and politeness uses, and how its preterite use differs from its participle use.

How to explain it

Regular -ed (with the allomorphs -d, -t, /d/, /t/ and /ɪd/) marks the preterite. This dictionary lists four meanings: displacement from a reference point (temporal, modal or interpersonal), temporal anteriority (They finished yesterday), non-actual or hypothetical marking (If she studied more; I wish I lived there), and social distancing (Did you want coffee?; I hoped we could talk). The first, unifying meaning remains debatable. Temporal anteriority has exceptions such as sports commentary (Jones passed to Smith!). Remote and backshifted preterites need not locate the event before speech time. Regular -ed also marks the past participle, as in Jo has checked the key. This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional.

Terms

Past tense is the traditional name; CGEL says preterite. The -ed form after have is the past participle.

Examples

  • She walked home.
  • They finished yesterday.
  • If she studied more
  • I hoped we could talk.

Contrasts to teach

  • Jo checked the key.Finite preterite.
  • Jo has checked the key.Participle selected by have.
  • I wish I lived there.Contrary-to-fact desire, not past time.

Watch for

  • A learner might assume every -ed form locates the event in the past, when this dictionary lists hypothetical and politeness uses.
  • A learner might read Did you want coffee? only as asking about a past wanting, when it can be a polite offer.
  • A learner might treat the -ed in has checked as a preterite, when it is the participle selected by have.

In class

Students sort -ed sentences into past time, imagined situations and polite requests or offers, using examples such as They finished yesterday, If she studied more and I wanted to ask you. They then explain what each use has in common with the others, if anything.
