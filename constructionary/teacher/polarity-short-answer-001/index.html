Finite Auxiliary in a Polar Short Answer

Shows how a reply to a yes/no question can be built from yes or no, a subject pronoun and a finite auxiliary.

Early sketch. The linguist entry has one example so far and its evidence isn’t written up yet. Use this page as a starting point, not a settled account.

How to explain it

A polar short answer resolves an antecedent polar question with an overt indication of polarity. The pattern is an answer particle, a subject pronoun and a finite auxiliary, optionally negated. Did presents a positive answer and helps identify the elided predicate. Responses to negative questions can vary in the use of the answer particle across speakers and contexts. This dictionary marks this as provisional, with medium confidence, and relates it to auxiliary VP ellipsis.

Terms

Textbooks sometimes call these short answers or elliptical answers. This dictionary's answer particle is the word yes or no.

Examples

  • “Did Jo lock up?” “Yes, she did.”
  • “Can Sam swim?” “Yes, he can.”

Watch for

  • A learner might treat the reply to a negative question as having a single fixed form, when this dictionary notes variation in the answer particle.

In class

Pairs ask each other five yes/no questions with different auxiliaries (did, can, is, has, will) and reply with a full short answer, then say what part of the verb phrase was left out.
