Preposition Phrase

Defines the PP and the range of complements a preposition can take, including none.

How to explain it

A PP is a phrase headed by a preposition, with or without a complement. The head may take an NP object (in the garden), a predicative NP or AdjP (as a liability, as reasonable), an AdvP (until recently), a clause (because it rained), a PP (from under the bed), or no complement (outside). A single complementless preposition constitutes a complete PP. PPs are classified by their heads, not by an NP being present. Locative members accept modifiers such as right and straight. A PP can function as a complement, modifier, adjunct or predicative complement in a larger construction.

Terms

Traditional grammars sometimes treat words like outside, until and because as adverbs or conjunctions when no NP follows. Here each is a preposition heading a PP.

Examples

  • in the garden
  • as a liability
  • until recently
  • because it rained
  • from under the bed
  • outside

Contrasts to teach

  • We waited in the garden.Preposition with an NP object.
  • We waited outside.A complete PP with no complement.

Watch for

  • A learner might think a preposition needs an NP after it, when outside can be a complete phrase.
  • A learner might analyze because it rained as a clause only, when this dictionary takes because as the head of a PP with a clause complement.

In class

Give students a list of PPs (in the garden, until recently, outside). They underline the preposition, then say what, if anything, follows it.
