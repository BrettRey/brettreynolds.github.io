Preposed Verbal Predicate

Covers sentences where the verb part comes first and the auxiliary is left behind, as in run she did.

Early sketch. The linguist entry has one example so far and its evidence isn’t written up yet. Use this page as a starting point, not a settled account.

How to explain it

The pattern is a clause consisting of a preposed VP, the subject and a stranded licensing auxiliary, as in run she did. It repeats or validates a salient predicate by putting the verbal material first. The auxiliary remains in the nucleus, and the preposed VP is plain after do. Preposing the lexical predicate does not require inverting the subject and auxiliary. This dictionary relates it to complement preposing, auxiliary VP ellipsis and finite do-support. It is provisional, with medium confidence.

Terms

Textbooks sometimes describe this as VP fronting. This dictionary calls it a preposed verbal predicate.

Examples

  • They said Jo would run, and run she did.

Watch for

  • A learner might expect the subject and auxiliary to swap places, when this dictionary says preposing the lexical predicate does not require inversion.
  • A learner might use an inflected verb in the fronted part, when it is plain after do.

In class

Give students a prediction (They said Jo would run). They continue each with a confirming clause that fronts the verb, then compare the order with a plain version.
