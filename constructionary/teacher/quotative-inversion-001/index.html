Quotative Inversion

Explains why a reporting verb can come before its subject after direct speech.

Early sketch. The linguist entry has one example so far and its evidence isn’t written up yet. Use this page as a starting point, not a settled account.

How to explain it

In quotative inversion the reporting verb comes before its subject after or within direct speech: “I can explain,” said the clerk. Full NP subjects readily follow the verb. Plain personal pronouns usually precede it in contemporary neutral prose, though literary inverted variants occur. This dictionary marks the analysis provisional and notes the link with the subject-dependent inversion family.

Terms

Sometimes called 'speech-verb inversion'; this dictionary calls it quotative inversion.

Examples

  • “I can explain,” said the clerk.

Contrasts to teach

  • “I can explain,” the clerk said.The same report with the subject before the verb.

Watch for

  • A learner might think said the clerk is a question because the verb comes first.
  • A learner might use the inverted order with a plain pronoun in everyday writing.

In class

Students take dialogue from a story and rewrite each quotation tag with the verb first and then with the subject first, comparing how each reads.
