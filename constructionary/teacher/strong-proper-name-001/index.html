Strong Proper Name Construction

Helps explain why names appear without a determiner, including a family word like Mom when it is used as a name.

How to explain it

The construction is a singular NP with no determiner, naming semantics, and referential use in an argument function (subject, object in a VP, object in a PP). Its head may be a proper noun (Kim, Ukraine) or a common noun in naming use (Mom, Grandma). Naming semantics means a conventional name, not mere identifiability: the doctor can identify someone without being a name. Names with the article (the Thames) are weak proper names and outside the construction. So are bare vocatives, whose bareness comes from the vocative function (Waiter!). A small set of emotive adjectives can embellish a strong name without a determiner. This dictionary marks the analysis as provisional and departs from CGEL's usage: it treats proper name as a semantic classification and the NP as the unit with a head.

Terms

CGEL: strong proper names (no determiner) and weak ones (with the, as in the Thames). Proper name is a semantic category; proper nouns are the word class specialized for heading such NPs. Kin terms used as names remain common nouns on CGEL's analysis.

Examples

  • Kim called.
  • She lives in New York.
  • You wanna roll the ball to Mommy?
  • Poor Mom needs a rest.

Contrasts to teach

  • Mom called.Naming use; no determiner.
  • My mom called.Relational use; the same noun takes a determiner.
  • The poor dog needs a rest.An ordinary singular count noun takes the same adjective with a determiner.
  • They walked along the Thames.A weak proper name, with the article; outside this construction.

Watch for

  • A learner might assume Mom must take a determiner like any other singular count noun.
  • A learner might treat a bare vocative such as Waiter! as an instance, when its bareness comes from the vocative function.
  • A learner might treat the Thames as the same kind of name as Kim.

In class

Students list names from their lives (people, places, family words). They test each in a subject slot and in a relational version with my, then sort into names that stand alone and nouns that need a determiner.
