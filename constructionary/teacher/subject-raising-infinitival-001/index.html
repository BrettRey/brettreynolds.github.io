Subject Raising with a Catenative Verb

Useful for contrasting seem-type verbs with promise-type verbs and for showing why dummy there works with them.

How to explain it

The pattern combines a verbal assessment or aspectual predicate with a subject selected through its complement. Two tests show it. Dummy there is acceptable (There seems to be a leak), and active and passive descriptions of the subordinate event preserve what seems to hold (Jo seems to have checked the lock; The lock seems to have been checked by Jo). Subject control, as in Jo promised to return, differs on both counts. This dictionary treats the analysis as provisional and bases it on constructed illustrations.

Terms

Subject raising is this dictionary's name for it. Sometimes called subject-to-subject raising. The contrast is with subject control.

Examples

  • There seems to be a leak.
  • Jo seems to have checked the lock.

Contrasts to teach

  • Jo seems to have checked the lock.Active; the subordinate event is what seems to hold.
  • The lock seems to have been checked by Jo.The passive preserves what seems to hold.
  • Jo promised to return.Control; Jo has a role in the promising.

Watch for

  • A learner might think the subject of seems is doing the seeming.
  • A learner might treat seems to and promised to as the same pattern, when only one allows dummy there.

In class

Students try the dummy there and the passive tests on sets of verbs followed by to-phrases. They sort the verbs into those that pass both tests and those that do not.
