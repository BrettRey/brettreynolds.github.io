What With Circumstantial Adjunct

For advanced classes, what with as a way to present circumstances as the reason for a situation.

How to explain it

The pattern is an adjunct beginning with what with, followed by a noun phrase, a non-finite or verbless predication, or a coordination. It presents one or more circumstances as explaining the main situation. A plain with phrase can depict an accompanying circumstance with no explanatory force, as in She waited with her arms folded. Adding what invites a reason reading. A simple noun phrase such as the weather can fill the same slot. This dictionary marks its analysis provisional.

Terms

This dictionary treats what with as an idiom introducing a reason adjunct, and relates it to absolute supplements, which can also express circumstances with their own predication.

Examples

  • What with the trains being late and the roads blocked, we stayed put.
  • What with the weather, we never left the house.

Contrasts to teach

  • What with the weather, we never left the house.Reason reading.
  • She waited with her arms folded.Accompanying circumstance, no explanation.

Watch for

  • A learner might read what with as a question about what, when it introduces a reason.
  • A learner might take a plain with phrase to explain the main situation, when it can depict an accompanying circumstance.

In class

Students write sentences with with in two uses, one a depiction and one with what added as a reason, and say which is which.
