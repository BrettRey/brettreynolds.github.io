    The same mental process undeniably recurs in every language; from language to language, powers, means and results are as alike and as unlike one another as human bodily and mental dispositions for language permit diversity within the limits set by nature. When attempting to describe individual languages, we must now begin with the general type of this process and return to it, since otherwise we would entirely lack the necessary point of comparison. The generality of this type consists in everything that can be said of it being conditioned solely by the pure concept of language, while all other circumstances are disregarded: circumstances that arise from the remaining human dispositions and the conditions acting upon them, and that individualize the general type in reality.

    What has so far been developed in this sense under the name of general grammar falls far short of exhausting the concept, and stops at only a small part of it. Unfortunately, we do not yet know whether this discipline had already made progress among the Indian grammarians; the Greek grammarians, however, had brought certain aspects of it to a perfection that left little to add. It is actually concerned only with the logical analysis of discourse and the examination of the word forms needed to connect thoughts within it: thus, in the first instance, with a product of language, and consequently with only a part of language.1 Here and there, the concept of general grammar has indeed been given a wider scope; but nowhere, so far as I know, has anyone attempted what is required here: an analysis of the procedure of language in its combined operation as a whole. Since general grammar is also chiefly concerned with examining grammatical forms individually as the components of discourse, it falls largely within the following section, according to the quite different division of linguistic analysis attempted here. It can therefore in no way relieve us of the task of establishing anew, through the development of our own ideas, the general principles needed to compare particulars, however much better it would be, when describing a class of particular languages, to refer to a general theoretical system of language already constructed and tested elsewhere.

    The mental process of language is taken here in its widest scope, considering language not only in relation to discourse and its stock of word elements, but also in relation to its influence on the capacity for thought and feeling.     For it has already been demonstrated elsewhere, and may surely be accepted as undisputed, that language does not merely designate thought formed independently of it, but is itself the formative organ of thought. Intellectual activity, wholly mental, wholly inward, and passing, in a sense, without a trace, becomes external and perceptible to the senses through sound in discourse, and receives a lasting body through writing. What is produced in this way is everything spoken and recorded; language, however, is the totality of the sounds that the particular intellectual activity to which it belongs can produce by this means, together with their possible combinations and transformations according to laws that arise, in turn, from the nature of intellectual activity and its corresponding system of sounds. Intellectual activity and language are therefore one and inseparable; we cannot even regard the former simply as what produces and the latter as what is produced. For although what is spoken on any occasion is certainly a product of the mind, it belongs to a language already in existence, and is therefore determined not only by the mind's activity but also by the sounds and laws of that language; and, immediately passing back into language as a whole, it in turn acts back upon the mind, determining it. Intellectual activity is bound by the necessity of entering into a connection with sound; otherwise thinking cannot attain distinctness, nor can a representation become a concept. It produces sound by a free decision and shapes it through its power, for sound becomes articulated sound through being permeated by intellectual activity; and (if it were possible to conceive a beginning of all language) it establishes a domain of such sounds that independently acts back upon the activity, determining and limiting it.2

    Denn es ist schon sonst dargethan, und kann wohl, als unbestritten, angenommen werden, dass die Sprache nicht bloss die Bezeichnung des, unabhängig von ihr geformten Gedanken, sondern selbst das bildende Organ des Gedanken ist. Die intellectuelle Thätigkeit, durchaus geistig, durchaus innerlich, und gewissermassen spurlos vorübergehend, wird durch den Ton in der Rede äusserlich und wahrnehmbar für die Sinne, und erhält durch die Schrift einen bleibenden Körper. Die intellectuelle Thätigkeit und die Sprache sind daher Eins und unzertrennlich von einander; man kann nicht einmal schlechthin die erstere als das Erzeugende, die andre als das Erzeugte ansehen.     What thinking requires in language is not, strictly speaking, what is actually audible to the ear; or, to put it differently, if articulated sound is divided into articulation and noise, thinking requires only the former, not the latter.3 Articulation rests on the mind's power over the organs of speech, compelling them to treat sound in a way that corresponds to the form of the mind's own operation. What brings this form and articulation together, as though in a connecting medium, is that both divide their domains into elementary parts whose combination invariably produces wholes that carry within themselves the striving to become parts of new wholes. Besides this power, however, the mind also contains an urge to make such use of the organs of speech, an urge communicated to those organs themselves; and the production of language rests on this power and this urge even independently of noise audible to the ear. The example of deaf-mutes teaches us that language is indeed wholly inward and remains possible even without the production or perception of sound.4 Every avenue of access to them through the ear is closed, yet they learn to understand what is spoken from the movements of the speaker's organs of speech and from writing; they themselves speak when the position and movement of their organs of speech are guided. This can happen only through the faculty of articulation that they too possess: through the connection between their thinking and their organs of speech, they learn to guess, in another person, one member of this connection—the person's thinking—from the other, the movement of that person's organs of speech. The sound we hear reveals itself to them through the position and movement of the organs; they perceive its articulation without its noise. There may also be something sensory in them corresponding to this noise, perhaps something their imagination supplies in place of what is missing, something of which we have no conception; but in any event, a remarkable division of articulated sound takes place in them. They really do understand language, since they learn to read and write alphabetically and even to speak themselves, rather than merely understanding thought through signs or pictures. They learn this not merely because they possess reason like other human beings, but specifically because they also possess a capacity for language: an accord between their thinking and their organs of speech, and an urge to make the two act together, both essentially grounded in their human nature, though it is mutilated in one respect.

    Articulation—whose concept I take here only in terms of its effect, as the shaping of sound that makes it a bearer of thoughts—has nevertheless been described above only in its logical, or at least only in its intellectual, nature. In its essential nature, however, it depends precisely on sound as noise, or rather on the vibration of the air through which the organs of speech act at a distance. It can therefore never be applied directly to any sense other than hearing; however one attempted this, one would always produce only signs of sounds, not directly signs of words divisible into elementary parts.5 This is because precisely the vibration of the air produced by organs determined by thought, and audible to another person's ear, is inseparably connected with human nature and necessary to its development.

    Thinking is a mental act, but through its need for language it becomes an impulse towards a bodily act. It is a progressive unfolding, a purely inward movement in which nothing lasting, steady or at rest can be assumed, and at the same time a longing to pass from darkness into light, from limitation into infinity. In the human being, whose twofold nature is fused into one, this striving also turns outwards. Through the mediation of the organs of speech, it finds in air—the finest and most easily moved of all the elements, whose apparent incorporeality corresponds to the mind even at the sensory level—a material wonderfully suited to it: a material in which, given the upright human posture, discourse flows freely and calmly from lips to ear, which carries to us the light of the heavenly bodies, and which extends into infinity without visible bounds.6

    As mental striving thus breaks its way through the lips, its product returns to one's own ear; the subjective inner act is received again as an external object. Language thereby shares and advances the innermost nature of thinking: the continual passage of subject and object into one another. For no kind of representation can be regarded as the mere contemplation of an object already present. The activity of the senses must unite synthetically with the mind's inner act; the representation breaks free from this union, becomes an object confronting the subjective power, and, perceived anew as such, returns into that power. Thus, quite apart from communication between human beings, speaking is a necessary condition of the individual's thinking in secluded solitude. In its actual manifestation, however, language develops only socially. For a human being understands himself only by having tried and tested the intelligibility of his words on others.

    When unarticulated sound takes the place of language, as it always does in animals and sometimes in human beings, it is either wrung out by distress, as with unpleasant sensations, or prompted by an intention, as when it entices, warns or calls for help; or else it flows, without distress or intention, from the joyful feeling of existence, from delight in the ringing out of sounds.     The same impelling causes pass into language: distress, intention and delight in discourse. But in language the last also, and chiefly, concerns thought, and this adds a fourth cause: the need to communicate and develop ideas and feelings through conversation. It is surely among the most erroneous claims to attribute the emergence of languages principally to the need for mutual assistance. Human beings in the state of nature are not so needy, and unarticulated sounds would have sufficed for that purpose, as we see in animals. Language is wholly human, even in its beginnings. Even the languages of the so-called savages8, and precisely those languages, possess an abundance and variety of expressions that everywhere exceed need; and there is probably no wandering family in any wilderness that does not have its songs, for the human being, considered as an animal species, is essentially a singing creature, only one that joins ideas to its tones. The far more essential cause of the emergence of language is delight in speaking, and hence the degree to which a people is taciturn or talkative has such an important influence on the formation of languages.

    Even in their noblest strivings, human beings must always be considered in their whole nature, one side of which they share with animality; hence in language too we must not overlook the mere sounding through which, as it were, animal sound passes into articulated sound. The first thing to observe here is the relation of this sounding to the designation of ideas. It may be compared with colouring in painting. Languages are sometimes richer in it, sometimes poorer, sometimes freer of resounding noise, sometimes more overloaded with it. It also happens, however, that languages, either generally or at certain stages of development, mix into actual language a greater or lesser amount of ringing out devoid of ideas, using syllables and even words without any definite effect on the sense, almost solely to fill out the sound. A certain feeling may admittedly attach to all expressions of this kind, but that is also possible with unarticulated sound. Through the cultivation of language, these mere filler words either disappear or, in the more favourable case, become, through more artful elaboration, signs of fine nuances of ideas or of their connections.

    Nations also continually change their languages purely through themselves, without branching off or mixture, in the continuation of their shared life; and after longer periods these changes become noticeable. The extent, speed and nature of these transformations depend on the liveliness of linguistic intercourse and on the depth with which human beings grasp the content of language. For we shall see below that there are different degrees of this. Attending to these transformations is a principal task of linguistic research. But even where language is reshaped more violently through branching off and mixture, national individuality exerts a predominant influence according to the way in which what is foreign touches it and the way it works upon that foreign material with its inherent power. It scarcely needs recalling, however, that to understand this national influence clearly we must return to the individual's distinctive treatment of language, which appears in that influence only as an aggregate.20 Yet even within a nation, an individual sometimes departs from the course established by everyone and brings a new way of treating language into currency, through an insight that suddenly lights up within him, through a decisive invention, or through his distinctive manner of using language. For although limits are set to the intellectual life of nations, just as to that of humanity, a certain latitude is nevertheless reserved for the individual here too. Wherever freedom moves within the bounds of finitude, there is admittedly a series of determining influences foreign to it at the moment of its operation; yet, like a flash of lightning from the cloudless aether, it can also suddenly step outside them and become self-determining.

    This consideration is especially important for linguistic research because it offers a warning against treating merely mechanical explanations as sufficient and against placing too much trust in analytical procedure. Research must indeed trace the uniform course human beings follow in transforming and forming language, but only while taking account of the independent power that can depart from that course or shorten it, and whose mere breath gives the materials of language an entirely different nature. We must indeed analyse and derive one thing from another, but only with a feeling that there are also true syntheses, in which idea and sound meet by a path that cannot be investigated further. Just as the stock of words and system of rules we call language are always fertilized by the living mind in thinking, speaking and recording, investigation too must always keep the living mind and dead language before it together, and follow their interaction. The individuality of nations can take such manifold paths that any attempt even to group it into classes would be futile. But for the principal influences upon language, only two points ever matter: whether national individuality inclines towards intellectual life at all, and from what direction; and whether it inclines specifically towards intellectual life through language. For just as in everyday life we encounter different degrees and kinds of need for language, feeling for language and treatment of language among human beings, so it is with nations; and what appears in the continual further development of languages also occurred when they arose from one another, since this emergence itself (43) could only have been a gradual becoming different. Much more could be said about this diversity in the national appropriation of language, were it not my purpose in this chapter to touch on particulars only by way of indication, so as to show the structure and interlocking of the whole.

    Terminology guide

    Local reading aids for this section. They do not impose fixed English equivalents across the whole work.

    General type

    Typus, Sprachtypus

    The shared pattern of the mental process described in16–20, abstracting from circumstances that give particular languages their character. It is not a classification such as “inflecting” or “agglutinating”.

    Mind, soul and inner life

    Geist, Seele, Gemüth

    This unit uses “mind”, “soul” and “inner life” to keep these recurring German terms recoverable. They overlap in reference; the wording does not impose a system of three separate faculties. The earlier English147 used “mind” for some instances of Seele and remains preserved.

    Representation and concept

    Vorstellung, Begriff, Wesenheit

    A representation is what the mind presents to itself; a concept is its articulated determination in the argument. “Reality” translates Wesenheit where a thought acquires standing as an object for a subject, not necessarily material substance.

    Sound and articulation

    Ton, Laut, Geräusch, Articulation

    Articulation is the shaping of sound into thought-bearing elements and combinations. Ton and Laut overlap and can both require “sound”; “speech sound” marks Laut where useful. Geräusch is “noise” in the specific contrast of21–22, without the implication of unwanted sound. “Alphabet” in29 and33 ranges over speech sounds rather than merely written letters.

    Power and capacity

    Kraft, Vermögen, Sprachkraft, Sprachvermögen, Gewalt, Macht

    “Power of language” keeps the recurring Sprachkraft visible; “capacity for language” renders Sprachvermögen. These are contextual contrasts, not names for two independently established mechanisms. Empfindung is “feeling” in the thinking/feeling contrast and “sensation” where bodily or animal responsiveness is at issue. Gewalt and Macht can also require “power”; their German identity remains available in the source. In51 the crucial contrast is who acts upon whom, made explicit in the English, rather than three independently established kinds of mechanism. Gefühl also requires “feeling”; the German words overlap here rather than naming universally separate domains.

    Individuality and similarity in kind

    Individualität, Gleichartigkeit

    Individuality is a distinctive way of being, said of languages, nations and persons at different levels; similarity in kind is what makes them comparable. The bearer and comparison matter. Sound’s privileged role in29 is qualified by49–50, as the local note explains.

    View and representation of the world

    Weltansicht, Weltvorstellung

    Weltansicht in39 is “view of the world”: a standpoint and manner of apprehending things, not merely an explicit set of opinions. Weltvorstellung in49 is “representation of the world”; the English retains the change of German term. This does not by itself make every member of a nation hold identical beliefs;50 explicitly returns to individual differences.

    Descent, stock and contact

    Abstammung, Stamm, Verkehr, Ursprache

    Descent may concern people or languages; the argument distinguishes these. “Stock” retains the historical grouping metaphor, including regional groups such as the Florentines in29, without making it a modern biological taxon. “Primordial language” belongs to the explicitly qualified origin discussion in45–47. In43 “mother language” is a supposed parent language; “mother tongues” in49 are people’s native languages.

    Speech, discourse, matter and form

    Rede, Redeverbindung, Materie, Form, Stoff

    Rede concerns connected speech or discourse, rather than a stock of words. In55 the distinction is between words as matter and their combination into discourse as form, explicitly not simply sounds versus rules. “Material” renders Stoff where the inherited language is contrasted with its living production. “Dead” material means the inherited stock considered apart from its living use; it need not be an extinct language.

    Intellectual life

    Intellectualitaet

    The repeated Intellectualitaet in52 and54 is “intellectual life”: the sphere towards which national activity is directed, with a role for language. It does not mean measured intelligence or modern national test scores. Geistig and intellectuell can also overlap as “mental” or “intellectual”; the English does not posit two exclusive faculties from that lexical difference.

    Nation and people

    Nation, Volk

    These name the human communities in the argument, not automatically modern sovereign states. Humboldt places dialects, social groups and individual ways of speaking within them, and describes their historical formation through separation and cohesion.

    Thing and object

    Gegenstand, Object

    In31 the word is contrasted with things and then becomes an object for a thinking subject;39 distinguishes an external object’s impression from the word’s own effect. “Object” can translate either German noun where the context supports it. Gegenstand does not invariably mean a physical thing:31 also calls I and he different Gegenstände.
