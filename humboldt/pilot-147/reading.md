**147** Here, however, one circumstance must not be overlooked. It does indeed lessen the disadvantage arising from the lack of genuine grammatical forms in languages, and could be regarded as a decisive objection to what has just been said. Even if a grammatical form has no designation in the language, it is nevertheless present, as a guiding law of understanding, in those who speak the language. At the same time, it is also indicated in the language in some way, however that may be. It may therefore even seem doubtful whether it should be attributed to the language or denied to it, since, after all, by language we understand not merely the dead sounds but also the speakers’ apprehension of them. Yet it remains no less necessary to distinguish this apprehension precisely from the dead sounds, and here to speak only of what actually lies in the sound perceptible to the senses. By grammatical form, therefore, I here understand an indication of the grammatical relation in the audible part of language itself that is so fixed and pure that the grammatical relation indicated is definitely recognized in it, and that the manner of indication cannot be taken as a designation of a concept at all but immediately, through itself, makes manifest the distinction between the designation of concepts and the designation of relations. Given the order deliberately chosen (134.), I can explain the manner of indication only later. Here I do not even exclude indication through word order, provided that it is absolutely fixed, immovable, and refers to a fixed point that is not itself recognizable merely by its position. If I now assume a case in which such an indication of a grammatical form as that just described is entirely absent from a language, or is effected not formally but through a concept with substantive content[^substantive-content], the grammatical form[^form-or-indication] is nevertheless brought into the language, and thus in turn also acts back from the language upon the mind. Thus, for example, in Chinese there is no designation of the verb as a grammatical form.[^chinese-grammatical-form] But the language possesses words which, although grammatically they could also be nouns, are always taken only as verbs in the language. The Chinese speaker therefore never sees anything other than a verb in such a word, and for him the language consequently has verbs, provided one pays no attention to whether their verbal nature lies in a grammatical designation or in habitual linguistic usage. The same is found where a word in Chinese can be used equally well as a noun or as a verb, but the context of meaning or position indicates that the latter use is intended. Yet position in Chinese is not an indication of grammatical form in the sense just established, if only because it lacks a fixed centre outside itself. It also happens in languages that words originally possessing substantive content pass into actual relational words. In Chinese, ì[^historical-chinese-transcription] is a verb and means to use, to employ. But it also serves as a preposition, and Mr Abel-Rémusat[^remusat-citation][^editor-remusat-page][^citation-number-context] very rightly observes that the nation, frequently using the word in this latter meaning, gradually forgets its original verbal sense entirely. In this instance, admittedly, such forgetting is more difficult, since the word is still current as a verb even today. But very often the situation is such that the original substantive meaning has disappeared entirely and can scarcely still be discerned etymologically; in that case, such a grammatical word functions solely as a relational concept. This, however, can only have happened little by little, through a gradual transition. Consequently, it is no longer always possible to tell from the language alone what is taken as truly grammatical, as a genuine and pure designation of relation; for that, one would have to know the nation’s manner of apprehension, and to know it at different epochs.

## Terminology guide

**Mind and soul** (*Geist*, *Seele*). The inherited English uses *soul* for *Seele* in 13 and *mind* for it in 147; that English variation does not establish a German lexical contrast. *Geist* and *Seele* are not assigned an invariant division into separate faculties here. The main text is preserved in this annotation pass.

**Apprehension and knowledge** (*Anschauung*, *Auffassung*, *Erkenntniss*). The distinctions are local: 14 contrasts apprehended living individuality with knowledge obtained by analysis; 147 contrasts speakers’ apprehension with what is audibly marked. No uniform English substitution settles their philosophical relations.

**Substantive and relational** (*Sachbegriff*, *Sachwörter*, *Sachbedeutung*, *Verhältnisswörter*, *Verhältnissbegriff*). The contrast in 147 is between what a word means in its own right and a grammatical relation it expresses. The meaning ‘to use, to employ’ is an example of content meaning; in a later stage of a word’s history, such an earlier meaning may be lost while a relational use remains. *Substantive* does not mean ‘noun’, and *relational word* is not imposed as a universal modern word-class label.

**Noun and verb** (*Nomen*, *Nomina*, *Verbum*). *Noun* is the working English label for the Latin *Nomen* in 147; its precise historical extension remains unsettled. The examples describe a word’s use as a noun or a verb, without establishing a modern category-membership test.

## Notes

[^substantive-content]: *Substantive* means the content meaning of a word, as distinct from the grammatical relation it marks. For example, ‘to use, to employ’ is the content meaning of the Chinese word also used as a preposition. An *original substantive meaning* is an earlier content meaning that may later be lost; *substantive* does not mean ‘noun’. (Trans.)

[^form-or-indication]: *The grammatical form* supplies the referent of German *sie*. ‘The indication’ is also grammatically possible; the surrounding discussion favours the form as the chosen reading. (Trans.)

[^chinese-grammatical-form]: *Grammatical form* has the restricted sense defined above. The argument distinguishes a grammatical relation from its formal designation; it explicitly allows Chinese verbs to be recognized through habitual use in the following sentences. (Trans.)

[^historical-chinese-transcription]: The form *ì* reproduces the historical transcription in the print. No Chinese character or modern pinyin equivalent is supplied here. (Trans.)

[^remusat-citation]: *Lettre sur le génie de la langue Chinoise à Monsieur Abel Remusat* [*Letter on the character of the Chinese language to Monsieur Abel Remusat*], p. 22. 98. (Humboldt)

[^editor-remusat-page]: [On Humboldt’s preceding citation:] Cf. above, p. 268. (1906 Ed.)

[^citation-number-context]: The unexplained terminal *98.* is retained from Humboldt’s note. The editor’s page 268 belongs to the 1906 German volume, rather than this English reading. (Trans.)
