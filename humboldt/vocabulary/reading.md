## β. Vocabulary.[^first-publication]

**72** A word is a sound that designates a concept. Every word therefore has a unity of sound and a unity of concept. Where sounds are combined, accent produces their unity. The simple word is the true individual in language. It contains the entire operation of the understanding in language. It is itself the form in which the mind takes what it has transformed into its own subjectivity and sets it outside itself again as an object (§39). Analysing the word in turn yields the syllable and the letter. These are only elements of language and are no longer language. All words, including compound words, are the constituents and material of speech, and the vocabulary of a language therefore contains the entire material of a nation’s thought. The word shares the whole nature of language described above. It has the same objectivity and subjectivity (§40), exercises the same power over human beings (§42) and is equally subject to their freedom (§51). It shapes objects into the distinctive ideal world that language as a whole puts in place of the actual world (§39). The distinctive way in which language apprehends things consists chiefly in this, that individual subjectivity, remaining constant, takes and treats all the constituents of language in the same way and attaches itself to each of them. This way of apprehending things arises more from the ideal than from the material part of languages, more from the mode of presentation they provide than from the sounds used in them. Words that pass from generation to generation and from nation to nation, with little change in sound and designating definite objects, can therefore bear this distinctive character only faintly on their face. It emerges only in their use and in the way they supplement, limit, and determine one another. Where this use is known only from scanty samples, as with the languages of uncultivated nations, mere knowledge of the words, which for that very reason is also incomplete, is insufficient to get to the bottom of what is distinctive about the language in its most important respect.

**73** The number of possible words in a language depends on the number of possible syllables in it (§62.a) and on its particular rules and habits of word formation (§62.b). This number can readily be determined only where it is relatively small and the language actually makes use of all or most of the possibilities.[^chinese-tones] This can occur almost exclusively in languages with monosyllabic words.

**74** When these languages are grouped together as a class of their own, however, one must be clear about the basis on which this is done. This classification cannot be called purely factual. For a large proportion of languages, however polysyllabic they may be, can be traced back by analysis to monosyllabic roots, and if one goes back in thought to the origin of languages, the most natural thing is to assign just one simple sound to a simple concept. This should not, however, be taken to mean that every stem word must necessarily be monosyllabic. Our investigation of language can never go back to the beginning. In it a stem word is a relative concept, defined by its relation to other words. Any of our languages may have begun with many polysyllabic words alongside monosyllabic ones. Conversely, even monosyllabic languages do not exhibit this property in its pure form. When two words in them come together to form a compound concept, it is solely the accent that either binds them together or still keeps them apart by bringing each out separately. It would probably be all but impossible to decide whether this separation also keeps the concepts further apart in the understanding, or whether their connection leads one to regard the peculiarity of pronunciation as merely that, without taking it to mark a division between words. There is good reason, however, to maintain that keeping the sounds separate where the close connection makes the concepts one interferes with the effect on the mind of dividing speech into proper and appropriate units, each forming a whole word. In any individual case it may make no difference whether the division of sounds keeps pace with the division of thought. Taken together, however, the cases are unmistakably important, and the systematic breaking up of sounds into separate syllables must adversely affect the flow of thought. Yet these subtler effects of language on the mind have probably never been investigated in China itself. In nature, other circumstances that arise alongside an imbalance usually compensate for it, so that on the whole the result is the same as where the imbalance does not occur.

**75** The very concept of monosyllabic languages must therefore be understood with some qualification, and polysyllabic languages may originally have been monosyllabic. Even as regards sound, the division into words on which this classification rests depends on circumstances that, as we have just seen, are harder to judge because there is no presupposition of an exact knowledge of speech in its living delivery. Nevertheless, the monosyllabic structure of a small number of languages remains a highly remarkable phenomenon, deserving the closest attention precisely because of its rarity. It is altogether mistaken to regard these languages as children’s languages, as it were, that have remained at a stage of original imperfection. The causes of monosyllabism must surely be sought in quite a different way and at a much deeper level. Since this property is foreign to the American languages, however, my aim here has simply been to assign it its proper place in the structure of language in general.

**76** The same applies, in polysyllabic languages, to the uniformity in the structure of stem words found, for example, in the Semitic languages.

**77** Where word formation is not bound to so fixed a form, the number of possible words remains indeterminate, and which of them are actually called into being is determined by a host of converging circumstances that can rarely be accounted for. One must always assume, however, that each word arose through another that existed before it. For all changes in language occur gradually (§43), and what is repeated in speech has invariably been heard before, in whole or in part. The investigation of word sounds therefore always finds itself in the middle of a chain whose beginning remains inaccessible to it. It scarcely needs pointing out, however, that the languages still in existence need not therefore all be parts of the same chain, since there could have been separate lines of development of words that came into contact only after a long time, or never did so at all (§46). Yet the sameness of the organs of speech and of their laws makes the same limited range of word sounds possible everywhere. Mere identity or affinity of sound cannot by itself decide the historical or physiological distinction (§47). The affinity can be conceived in two ways, through derivation, when the simpler sound becomes more or less composite by the addition of something at the end, at the beginning, or within it, and through echo, when the simple sound recurs, still simple but altered, or when a kindred idea is designated by a kindred sound. At most, words imitating natural sounds might be excepted from this emergence of each word out of another. They usually pass from generation to generation and from nation to nation like other words, though they can also arise suddenly, in which case they always conform to the sound analogies of the rest of the language.

**78** Here, where the question is simply how word formation proceeds in a given language, all that is required is to analyse composite word sounds into progressively simpler ones as far as possible, to work out the analogy governing their composition, and then to compare the simplest words thus found with one another in respect of their sound relationships. This reveals both the laws and habits of word formation among nations and the extent, completeness, and antiquity of their word structure. The laws and habits include changes to words by variation along the vowel series, the insertion and alteration of consonants, the extension of words by sounds added at the beginning or end, and the repetition and transposition of syllables. All this very often reveals how coarse or refined a nation’s sense of language is.

**79** The more directly a language is taken from one already fully formed, the less far it leads back up to simple sounds. These are scarcely encountered at all in the vocabularies of several such languages. It is only where major tribes have handed down their language from generation to generation for centuries, largely free from admixture from outside, and where its origin is lost in obscurity, that this extensive and ancient structure appears. In it long genealogies of words can be traced back to their basic sounds, and these still have meaning in their simplicity. Each analogy can be demonstrated by numerous examples, and each sound feature that shares a sphere or class with others is found alongside several or all of them. Strict analogy thus guides the mind through a comprehensive system of word sounds. Beyond the influence of such abundance and regularity on usage, this structure also acts in its own right upon the nation, both in clear consciousness and in obscure feeling. The production of language among its people becomes more fruitful for everything it comes into contact with when alongside the world of ideas there stands a world of sounds that orders itself with ease and in a wondrous way. The structure of such a language connects each individual with far more distant times, with a far more original state of humankind, and with a seemingly infinite mass of thoughts and feelings that once existed and yet continually, in one way or another, touch and call forth one another again.

**80** Herein lies the system of rules of word formation (§55), which must be distinguished from the sounds themselves even in a purely lexical comparison of languages. Its foundation is the sound system. For the finer and more intellectually attuned the ear and the sense of language prove to be in the sound system, the more carefully every alteration of tone arising, perhaps at first by chance, through the insertion of an aspiration, a vowel or a nasal sound is observed and carried through the corresponding cases. Other changes of the same kind are shaped to follow the pattern all the more closely. From kindred nations the main features of this system pass into kindred languages, and in many respects the sameness of kind between languages can be discerned more accurately from lexical analogy than from grammatical analogy. For lexical analogy attaches directly to sound, whereas grammatical analogy rests on general laws of thought. The more individual the sameness of kind, however, the more surely it points to a historical connection. We must pay close attention, however, to cases where words whose structure shows traces of analogies of this kind have passed into other languages without the analogies themselves. The receiving nation has no feeling for them and so does not develop the words further along the same lines. The words become part of a language where no such structure exists in that degree of completeness, or where a different system of structure exists.

**81** This internal connection among words, which extends to their sounds, has a clear consequence. To establish that sameness of kind, we must compare the words within each language before comparing them across languages. Setting the words of different languages side by side in alphabetical order tears each of those languages out of its natural context. Once the words of one language have been compared with one another, two objects of comparison with another language emerge, the rules of lexical analogy and the basic sounds. With the rules come the complex words that actually were formed according to them and passed from one language into the other. Only in this way is a comparison possible that carries its own warrant. Very often, however, one is thrown back on direct comparison of words, either for want of resources or because a language has no internal structure worked out with such clarity, and also because one never fully grasps even the structure that is there. One must still investigate the origin of each word to be compared as precisely as possible in its own language, lest one take genuinely different words to be of the same kind on the strength of a merely apparent resemblance in sound.[^coptic-sanskrit]

**82** In all the respects considered here, attention has been confined to the dependence of word formation on a nation’s treatment of sound, to the way it gives scope and variety to its stock of word sounds. Consideration of meaning also plays a principal role in word formation. Where forming *λαμβάνω* from *λάβω*, for example, involves a change of sound alone, forming *legen* ‘lay’ from *liegen* ‘lie’ brings with it a shade of difference in sense. Word formation by compounding depends entirely on meaning. Several concepts are always joined, either to form a new third concept or simply to determine the main concept in various ways, as in formation by adding syllables at the beginning or end. Sound relationships enter into this only incidentally and in a subordinate way.

**83** In choosing sounds for concepts, language is naturally guided by the clearer and more obscure relations that feeling and imagination, in keeping with the individuality of nations, find between them. Historically, however, this goes back into the lost ages in which language was invented, beyond the reach of any inquiry. As language gradually changes, now the sound diverges progressively from the basic tone, now the concept from the basic concept, and this relation between sound and meaning very often changes altogether. The feeling on which this relation rests, being by nature less determinate, also varies from one people to another. And precisely those nations with more original languages, that is, languages bearing fewer traces of great upheavals, tolerate fewer consonants coming together in their syllables and thus lack a further means of shaping the character of the word sound. It is therefore by no means unusual to see strong and even disagreeable concepts paired with gentle and soft sounds, and vice versa, and no weight whatever should be placed on this analogy in historical investigations of language. Fancy and acumen find ample but highly uncertain ground here, and for every ten examples that demonstrate such agreement between sound and meaning, one comes upon a far greater number in which it is absent. It would, however, be equally mistaken to disregard it altogether. For there is no denying that in languages of great antiquity and of extensive and varied word structure, the feeling of such unmistakable agreement forces itself upon us more often than in others. Where this agreement continues to act for centuries on the same nation in a mother language that has remained unmixed, it heightens and strengthens the influence of language. For language, everything depends on making the word, through the closest fusion of sound with concept, into a being in its own right, suspended between the human being and the world, at once dependent on and independent of both. This is especially apparent in German and in the languages that depend on it.

**84** Word sounds pass down with their meanings from generation to generation and from tribe to tribe. But the concept is more mobile than the sound perceptible to the senses and makes transitions more readily, depending on circumstances that lie sometimes in the concept itself, sometimes in the individual, and sometimes in external conditions. Meanings therefore shift more frequently than sound does. Word sounds may therefore be regarded as forms that have survived from hoary antiquity down to our own time, preserving within themselves a more or less variable content through which a thread always winds, though it often binds the parts together only loosely. On this ground alone, the study of the structure of language requires a separation of the word as sound from the word as concept, even though this separation does not exist in nature. For the continuous sequence of presentations, just like the continuous sequence of sounds, requires division into units of thought corresponding to the word sounds, by means of which ideal objects can be apprehended and connected.

**85** To each such unit of thought, and hence to every word, there corresponds an object, either one that can be pointed out in corporeal form in nature or one formed by the mind more or less independently of sense perception. For although in the latter case the word itself is this object, we must distinguish the word in general and in itself from the word thought by a particular individual at a particular moment, and make the same distinction for language as a whole. The word causes the soul to form a presentation of the object given in it. This presentation must be distinguished from the object. The presentation can and must vary from one individual to another because it is determinate in every respect. The object, considered as an object, can be grasped only in more general terms. Alongside the objective part, which relates to the object, the presentation has a subjective part that lies in the way it is apprehended, both in the intellectual view of it and in the feeling that accompanies that view. Again, it hardly needs saying that this separation rests solely on abstraction. The word can have no place outside thinking, and neither can its object if that object is incorporeal. The mind produces the word in its entirety on every occasion. The word truly has its completeness and individuality only in each act of thinking, and as a constituent of language and an object of linguistic inquiry it is merely a general form that can be individualized in various ways within the limits set by its nature. The same holds for objects of sense, since what becomes present to the soul is always the presentation that the word gives of them, never the objects themselves directly.

**86** Each word in a language allows a certain latitude for different presentations. This makes it possible for the modes of presentation of very different individuals and ages to come together in the same language, and for each to have a language of its own even while the common language remains the same (§50). In understanding one another, we do not meet at a single indivisible point where our modes of presentation coincide. Rather, our spheres of thought meet. Their more general parts overlap, and their more individual parts extend beyond that overlap. This makes possible the intellectual progress of humankind, in that every extension of thinking can pass into the possession of others without fettering the freedom they need to make it their own and extend it further. It is possible also because the forms of thinking, the languages, undergo changes prescribed in the course of their development for them, as for everything subject to the conditions of earthly existence. In every meaning of a word, then, we must attend to how it leaves the individual mode of presentation free and how it determines it. A history of words and their particular meanings can trace all the different ways in which they have been understood and used.

**87** A concept raised to the clarity and determinacy of a word is always the result of the whole power of the soul working together. Since it is characteristic of the word to evoke this concept through sound, as if by an electric shock, its effect radiates in every direction through the whole soul. The unity of a power cannot be split into parts. Yet three principal aspects stand out in the formation and effect of a word. We can examine each word in terms of its logical construction, its sensory import and the impression it makes on feeling. Each of these three aspects in turn appears in the word in two ways, since we must distinguish the presentation of the object itself (§85) from the presentation the word gives of it by virtue of its formation and origin. Neither presentation cancels the other, and the two enter into a definite relation with each other.

**88** The word gives shape to the concept and, through sound, gives it sensory import directly for the ear and indirectly for all the sensory powers of the soul. The word apprehends every concept as a general one and, strictly speaking, always designates classes of reality. This holds even for a proper name, since it gathers together all the states of what it designates that differ in time and space, presenting it as a class in which it is contained in all these states just as different individuals are contained in a generic concept. Yet the word makes itself, and hence also the concept it contains, an individual within language. It can therefore take up the concept only from a single standpoint, yet it must give up none of the concept’s content. The word achieves this by giving the soul a definite stimulus along those three principal lines, so that the concept is produced in full, but from a sharply defined side. This is how words with the same meaning in several languages give rise to different presentations of the same object. This property of the word is a principal reason why each language affords a view of the world of its own.

**89** Just as language, and the word within it, generalizes what is individual in the world, so it gives mental form to what has the character of material in outward and inward perception. Yet just as the word itself in turn becomes an individual in language, it also takes on corporeal form in language by assuming sound, and serves the mind as material for further activity. Both take place at once in an indivisible unity. Generality and individuality, mental character and material character, interpenetrate synthetically in the word. These qualities would otherwise cancel one another. They can coexist because the word, considered outside the soul as a constituent of language, is nothing more than a stimulus to thinking, while the word considered in the soul as a completed concept is the thinking itself. This unceasing development of the general and non-sensory out of the individual and sensory, and their return into them, is characteristic of human mental power. Returning now to the three principal directions mentioned above (§87) in which the word stimulates the soul, we see that the word can have import for sense and feeling only insofar as it can retain that import in the intellectual domain. This happens only when the content of sense and feeling is at once, and again synthetically, destroyed as material and preserved as form. This is the work of the imagination, which mediates between the opposed natures in humankind. Since the intellectual content of the word can unite with the sensory content only in the domain of the imagination, this domain is properly the sphere in which the word hovers, as the imagination gathers all the powers of the soul to produce or understand it.

**90** We may call the way the word brings objects of outward and inward perception before the soul, always along those three lines, what is objective in the word, its matter. We may call the way the faculties of the soul enter into relation with one another along those lines and fuse into a unity of action what is subjective in the word, its form.

**91** Both are undeniably present in language and in every one of its words. What was once the individuality of the speakers has attached itself to the sounds and through them returns to living individuality. For this marvellous way of embodying a begetting of the mind that brings forth a like begetting in another person is precisely what is truly distinctive about language, and it depends chiefly on its principal constituent, the word. Form, however, is even more important than matter. For form inheres in the whole language. Thus even when a person of very different individuality apprehends it as a dead or foreign language, that form compels them to adopt the language’s mode of intuition by surrounding them with it on every side. Matter has no such power. The particular mode of presenting the object does not even always remain discernible in the word, or at least is no longer felt as it was when the word arose. The individuality of the word is lost in the generality of the concept. I already pointed to this above (§87) in distinguishing the presentation of the object from the presentation the word gives of it. This distinction is admittedly possible only because the word, as an embodied concept, is gradually adapted by people of very different individualities to different modes of intuition, in which each new use presents the object quite differently from the way its first inventor did. Understood in this way, whole series of examples can be adduced. Expressions for corporeal objects have certainly arisen for the most part from properties that were especially striking in those objects. They are simply nouns that have become adjectives.[^noun-adjective-order] Non-sensory concepts are designated metaphorically in terms of corporeal ones. Grammatical concepts, which merely govern the linking of discourse, are designated after words for concepts of things. In all these cases the character of the original designation is very often, indeed usually, lost, and the concept of the object takes its place, understood in general terms or in some other way. It is unlikely that the Indian word *dwipa* ‘elephant’ has always called to mind the quality of drinking twice, through the trunk and through the mouth. Few people who use the German words *Vernunft* ‘reason’ and *zurück* ‘back’ think that the former comes from *vernehmen* ‘perceive’ and the latter from *Rücken* ‘back’. Many words that formerly had no such secondary meaning are later used only jocularly, ironically or contemptuously. This is how the matter of the word changes. Even between the same sound and the same object designated by it, the mind finds ample room to introduce differently nuanced modes of presentation. Strictly speaking, however, a different word is uttered each time under cover of the same audible sound. For the word proper is only the word actually thought or spoken. What is handed down in language as if dead is merely the shell, brought to life again and again, and always a little differently. In the word as thought or spoken, matter and form are always determined together by the character of the word and the language, and by that of the speaker.

**92** This accords with the observation above (§86) that the nature of the word allows individuals, even within the same language, to have a language of their own up to a point. The difference is that the earlier observation also applies where the matter and form of the word sound are fully felt, whereas here we are speaking of the opposite case. The sound form, as the shell of the concept, thus proves to allow latitude in two ways.

**93** When the original mode of intuition of objects gradually becomes blurred in the words of a language, a certain individuality and sensory vividness undeniably perish in it. The language may nevertheless gain in another respect by this very process. The particular wealth of images and concepts a language brings to the soul does not determine its merits. What determines them is its effect on thinking and feeling in general. For example, the designation of objects by their properties, mentioned in §90, affords a greater fullness of intuition as long as it remains alive in a language. Without going into its lesser disadvantages, it restricts freedom in forming a presentation of the object by favouring one side too much. When it forms nouns too visibly from adjectives, it disrupts a correspondence with nature. To follow nature in its very form, language must use words that have independence of their own to express independent beings in nature. The accord of concept and sound through an obscure feeling of kinship (§83) has a much more fitting effect, since it attunes and guides the soul while leaving its mode of presentation unrestricted. If linguistic inquiry attends to these relations, looks for them in words without reading them into the words, and in this way awakens the nation’s sense of language, which always has an affinity with its mother tongue even without conscious awareness, it brings about clearer insight and a finer feeling for language. The original individuality of words can then come to light again more distinctly and, without doing further harm, take its place under a mode of apprehending objects that has grown freer and more intellectual with time.

**94** Scarcely will any two languages maintain a perfect balance among the different ways their word forms stimulate the soul. One stimulus will predominate over another. The force with which these stimuli converge in the concept to form the presentation of the object will also vary, and on it the clarity and determinacy of thinking chiefly depend. Only highly cultivated languages permit inquiries of this kind, yet feeling alone can also perceive the differences. In one language a greater sensory clarity prevails, another does more to attune feeling, and a third rests more drily content with logical determination. As the nation’s direction changes, it can modify this character, but it can never leave it behind, since it is continually subject to its influences. This character also partly determines how a language is used and how poetic, philosophical or scientific its literature is. The character of that literature in turn determines the character of the language’s word forms.

**95** When we examine the matter of words, the nature of the objects also comes into play. We can ask three questions of every word. What does it bring together as its object? How does it designate what it brings together? What subsidiary concept does the accompanying feeling lead it to associate with this designation? If these questions are put in turn from two standpoints, first from that of a single language with respect to the variety of objects, then from that of the objects with respect to the words of several languages, the relation of the designation to what is designated will have been covered exhaustively, at least as far as the directions of inquiry are concerned. For a language proceeds uniformly in forming words, and it is this uniform procedure that must be investigated by examining a substantial number of words.

**96** The material content of words seems to be determined of itself by the objects of thought. A comparative study of several languages shows the contrary, and this must necessarily be so, since the word depends on the apprehension of the object rather than directly on the object itself, and many objects are formed in the first place by the soul. The first law that prevails here is that language must divide the material of thought correctly. In other words, the object of the word must be marked off by fixed boundaries and contain everything that goes to complete its concept and nothing that belongs to it merely incidentally and is inessential to it. Even the meanings of expressions for corporeal objects do not always correspond to the fixed boundaries in nature, but fluctuate between them.[^sanskrit-hips] This indeterminacy occurs still more frequently in intellectual designations. In the languages with which we are concerned here in the first instance, it is quite common to find determinations mixed in with the concept that impair its generality and that it must carry about with it to the detriment of clarity. Several languages, however, also have new and excellently formed concepts that must count as genuine extensions of thinking. A word for a concept never before thought within these boundaries is of immeasurable benefit when the concept concerns enough material of thought to help thinking reach broader views.[^sanskrit-concepts] This distribution of concepts among words reveals, first of all, the nation’s sense for a correct and sharply defined apprehension of nature and for logical precision. In higher respects, however, from it emerges the mind’s gift for creation. We should certainly very seldom picture this as the giving of a new word to a concept thought in its purity. Usually it is simply a matter of restamping a word hitherto used in a lower or less determinate sense.

**97** The second law concerning the content of words is that a word should not encompass meanings that are too many in number and linked to one another by ties of kinship that are too loose. This is found more in some languages than in others. It is a complaint made particularly about Chinese and Sanskrit. Yet we should remember that the complaint mostly applies to the shortcomings of dictionaries. Without order or reflection, they list one after another all the meanings in which a word occurs in particular passages, singling out from each passage precisely the most specific meaning into which the connection among the concepts forces the word’s natural and simple sense. If this were duly taken into account and distinctions were drawn between periods and kinds of usage, that multiplicity of meanings, which at present is indeed troublesome especially to beginners, would shrink considerably. Where it cannot be denied, however, it coincides on the one hand with the indeterminacy criticised above (§96), which admits more than one determination of the concept and hence more than one concept. On the other hand, it may also arise from spinning out a concept with excessive ingenuity into all the threads that issue from it, or from a fondness for overbold metaphors, and may thus be characteristic precisely of the languages of highly ingenious nations. The multiplicity of meanings of words must, however, be distinguished from the wide scope of their meaning. However wide its scope, the meaning remains single, and a language that genuinely fosters reflection must have found a way to give thought a foothold even at the highest stages of abstraction or the lowest stages of individualizing determination, since such words can arise at either. Only the coexistence of multiple concrete meanings and the arbitrary application of general meanings to one of the many concepts they encompass impair precision of expression and ease of understanding.

**98** In speaking here of the way the concept is designated in the word, I do not mean designation by sound, although the mode of designation under discussion determines the sound and sometimes coincides with it. Sound has already been discussed above, both generally (§§73–82) and with special reference to meaning (§83). Here we are concerned with the content of the word, that is, the concept. A concept cannot be given through its definition or, if it concerns a corporeal object, through the entire nature of that object. The word must give it shape as a unity (§88). What indicates the concept to the hearer’s mind is therefore its designation, and is itself a concept. This designation, which is properly a designation only in relation to the hearer and on the speaker’s side is an apprehension, is originally present in every word. In words modelled on natural sounds it lies in the sounds the objects make, in other words for corporeal things it lies in other properties of those things, and in words for intellectual concepts it lies in the metaphors chosen to indicate them. When the concept was clothed in a sound, something had to guide the tongue in choosing that sound. It could only have been this mode of intuition, which gathers the whole concept at a single point. We can conceive of this mode of intuition only as sensory, partly because it is to guide the choice of sound and partly because word formation as a whole hovers in the sphere of the imagination (§89). In most cases, however, this sensory mode of apprehension of the object, which preceded the sound or arose together with it but always determined it, can no longer be traced. It then survives only in the effect it had on the sound, and sometimes it is no longer recognizable even there. In the former case, the sound character of the word, its harshness or softness, its brightness or dullness and so on, is the designation of the word’s content. In the latter case, the designation is simply the audible sound of the articulated sounds themselves. The three German words *Fliege* ‘fly’, *Krähe* ‘crow’ and *Gans* ‘goose’ furnish examples. In the first, what designates the concept is the incessant flying that makes the animal particularly troublesome. In the second, it is the bird’s cry. In the third, it is just the word itself, which comes from the Indian language and defies explanation there too.[^gans-etymology] Words for intellectual concepts, with and without recognizable metaphors, furnish similar examples.

**99** We might call this way of designating a concept a word’s own motivating meaning. For it is indeed by this that they differ from other words devoted to the same object. It is important in several respects to investigate whether many words in a language have such a motivating meaning. We must also make the distinction already indicated (§91) between cases in which the people still generally feel it and cases in which only the investigator can discern it. Since the prevalence of motivating meanings must surely be taken as the more original condition in languages, we can draw conclusions from it about the changes that have taken place in a language. Insofar as they can be established reliably, motivating meanings themselves are worth investigating. They show the direction in which a people has apprehended nature and has appropriated what belongs to the mind in the sensory clothing of language. Since the inquiry here is meant to be historical, however, we must take care to give ingenuity and imagination no more scope than is warranted, and restrict ourselves to what reliable traces of word formation lead us to.

**100** The sensory material of the word that we are discussing here is the handle by which the mind apprehends intellectual concepts. If we wish to follow the course of nature, we must think of their designation in language as ascending to the concept, reversing the imagined descent from concept to designation. Throughout human life, phenomena that come into existence seemingly by chance, through the mere fertility of generative forces, build up into a whole from which the unity of an idea later shines forth to the observing mind. So it is with language too. In the colourful variety of sounds drawn forth by the perception of nature, the need to communicate and social talkativeness, the soul discovers intellectual relations and stamps existing words with corresponding meanings. It does so more often and with greater genius in proportion to its own intellectual power and the refinement of its sense of language. A rich and deep content of ideas arises in languages, in words ingeniously adapted to that content, when an active mind, ever directed towards higher things, fertilizes the natural mass of sounds, and not by a kind of dull brooding over the material of ideas, occupied with dividing and dissecting it.

**101** The word, as the designation of the concept, is akin to the sign and the symbol. This is the place to establish these three concepts precisely and to show that although the word shares properties with the other two, in its innermost nature it differs from both. Insofar as the word evokes the concept through its sound, it does indeed fulfil the purpose of a sign. Yet what takes it altogether out of the class of signs is that what a sign designates has an existence independent of its sign, whereas the concept itself attains completion only through the word, and the two cannot be separated. To fail to recognize this and to regard words as mere signs is the fundamental error that destroys all science of language and all proper appreciation of language. Insofar as the word transforms the concept into sensory material before the imagination, it resembles the symbol. For it puts a shape in place of the idea and, in the case of a corporeal object, abstracts from the entirety of its reality by holding it fast by a single feature and designating it in that feature by something foreign to it, a sound. The sound thus contains the concept within itself, like a hieroglyph.[^deleted-word-symbol] In the symbol, the sensory and the non-sensory interpenetrate and are regarded as one. The non-sensory reveals itself in the sensory and the sensory expands into the non-sensory. Idea and corporeal material coincide.[^creuzer][^deleted-images-signs] In a word, too, the concept cannot be cleanly separated from sound. It cannot be separated from sound in the abstract, because it cannot be thought without language, or from the particular sound it has in a given language, because the concept bears the individuality of that sound as well. Nevertheless, the word differs entirely from the symbol. The symbol requires a complete, self-subsisting natural form that can be considered quite apart from any idea dwelling within it, but from every part of which the idea shines forth once the form manifests itself as a symbol. The sound in the word, as its sensory material corresponding to that natural form, is in contrast nothing apart from its relation to the concept, since its sole function is to evoke the concept in a definite shape. The sensory and the non-sensory therefore coincide in quite different ways in the symbol and in the word. In the word, sound and concept are inseparable and each is incomplete on its own. They are one and constitute a single being, but the sound in a sense gives way to the concept, which it is meant only to evoke and shape. In the symbol, the natural form stands independently alongside the idea that permeates it and maintains its own primacy. The two are the same, since from every point of the one the other shines forth, but they are not one. Even when no idea is thought within it, the natural form remains in all the respects in which it had import in the symbol, like the body when the soul leaves it. Words and word sounds, however, like all other objects, may be treated as symbols with regard to their character and number and in connection with their meaning. This may be how we should understand the account that the Egyptian priests[^egyptian-vowels] celebrated the gods by sounding out the seven vowels in succession.

**102** The symbol expressly requires the fusion of the non-sensory with the sensory. The body is to be more than merely body, and the idea more than merely idea. Language, by contrast, is a purely mental striving that proceeds from thinking to thinking (§24). Only on this passage is it compelled to take on corporeal material, and it takes on as little as possible, merely the light sound that fades as soon as it is heard, and of that only the articulated sound, reduced to a merely audible relation by cutting away as much noise as possible. The understanding that symbolizes thus stands in natural opposition to the sense and genius for language. They also accustom the mind to lingering for very different lengths of time in the succession of presentations. This is of first importance in the formation of minds, whether of individuals or of nations. Symbolizing leads to absorption in the individual object, whose shell must, as it were, be penetrated to perceive the idea shining through it. The sense of language sweeps the mind along in an irrepressibly lively movement. For it follows the fleeting movement of thought and strives towards thought’s infinity. Since every word belongs to a series and none fully exhausts its concept, the sense of language passes from one to the next and runs through the gradually unfolding succession of thoughts, leaving behind only the slightest breaks, whose pauses disturb the continuity they interrupt as little as possible.

**103** Where an obscure feeling for the character of the object has been put into the sound of a word, there is indeed something symbolic in that sound. Thus in *Stärke* ‘strength’ there is firmness, and in *Wolke* ‘cloud’, *Wolle* ‘wool’ and *Wald* ‘forest’ there is the wavering indefiniteness of the boundaries and of how the parts come together to the eye. For in such cases this character shines forth from the sound, which acts upon feeling like an image of it. Yet this does not make the word sound itself a symbol. For its symbolic part merely contributes to its meaning, which is apprehended by the quite different route that language takes in general. The symbolic is present still more distinctly and frequently in the concepts of words, precisely in what we are discussing here, their sensory designation. This is the case with all metaphorical expressions. Yet this, too, leaves the nature of the word intact. This undeniable symbolizing activity of language shows that a gift for it benefits the sense of language. The relation between the two now also becomes completely clear. There must be both a gift and an impulse to seek the sensory and the non-sensory in one another and to wrap each in the other. But the bright and clear orientation towards what belongs to the mind must predominate and, by the living strength of the power of thought, suppress the inclination to linger precisely over the obscure and mysterious connection between them.

**104** We have been discussing the sensory presentation in the word and its motivating meaning. What, then, does the soul actually present to itself in sensory terms when it encounters a word? The object as a whole? The properties of the object apprehended in the word? Something indefinite corresponding to the feeling the word arouses? Or, among peoples who have long had an alphabet, the written characters? This would probably be difficult to ascertain by self-observation. The very intention to observe changes the object of observation, and the presentation itself surely varies across all those possibilities according to the individual and the circumstances. One point must be held to be both certain and decisive for recognizing the true nature of language. The word brings the object into presentation only in the way required by the particular connection of thoughts to which it subordinates the object and into which it weaves it as a component. The word allows the presentation to dwell on the object only as long as that connection requires. This follows from the nature of the word, which substitutes sounds for actual objects precisely to transform them into thoughts through that mediation. It follows also from the fact that the succession of thoughts expressed through several words is, strictly speaking, a unity, and that discourse is a whole whose divisions serve above all to make its structure as a whole apparent. It is therefore one of the most important properties of the word that it should evoke in presentation exactly what its use requires and no more. This is indeed achieved largely by combining the word appropriately with other words that determine it and by the overall direction given to the hearer’s soul. But it also depends in part on the formation of the word, which in this respect must have purity, sharpness and sobriety in the determination of the concept and be fully suited to a clearly conceived purpose. This also accounts for the need for several expressions for the same object, according to differences of use, differences in the indication of the aspects singled out in the object, and differences in the states of mind in which the expressions are used. For these states of mind must be considered here alongside the logical purpose of discourse, and they bear chiefly on the kind of sensory presentation contained in the word. What accompanies the same word in a poetic discourse plainly differs from what accompanies it in a scientific one. Here too, however, language creates more by chance than by design, and words akin in sense are often simply words adopted from other dialects together with a subsidiary concept acquired there.

**105** The accompanying feeling (§95) acts on words either as a particular emotion of the inner life or as an overall disposition of the intellectual and affective powers working together. In the latter case the intellectual powers predominate and take on the colouring of the affective powers. From the first arise endearing, honorific, solemn, scolding, contemptuous, mocking and jocular expressions. From the second arise poetic and philosophical expressions, expressions that move us by exhortation, and so on. Strictly speaking, we never utter a word without some such nuance of feeling, even if it passes unnoticed in a particular case, because the strings it sets resonating are always taut in the inner life. Here, however, we are concerned only with particular words into whose formation these determinations of feeling have entered so thoroughly that the words now always arouse the same feelings and cannot be used where those feelings are out of place. In individual languages these expressions exhibit a remarkable twofold phenomenon. The languages of uncultivated peoples contain a great many such expressions that betray particular emotions of the inner life. Language continually draws human beings towards the life of the mind. It first leads them up to pure intellectuality. Then, if no counterweight intervenes, it leads them beyond that summit to a dry, sophistical and playful intellectuality. It does so only gradually. All popular dialects use endearing, diminutive, augmentative and pejorative words more frequently than the higher, refined dialect does. Here, however, one should not look for specifically poetic words. Everything is still in a sense poetic and is received as such without awareness of the contrast. In the languages of some highly cultivated nations, on the other hand, this awareness is so refined and lively, and expression so chaste, that a poetic word is never tolerated in unfettered discourse. This arises only indirectly from the nation’s feeling for poetry. It arises directly from its feeling for prose as a distinct kind of discourse, which presupposes a sense of language developed to a much higher degree and refined with greater care. It is therefore found only in the noblest languages, those that have been cultivated with the greatest ingenuity. Among philosophical words we must distinguish those that are merely logical, constructed by the understanding and belonging solely to technical scientific vocabulary, from those that arise out of deeper reflection or higher revelation through the synthesizing power of reason. Where abstract philosophy has entered into a grand union with the highest poetry, as in Indian, these expressions are found in exceptional strength and beauty.

**106** So far I have tried to analyse the nature of words and the requirements they must meet, considering each word individually, in order to show how language designates the individual concept. We must now examine the entire vocabulary of a single language, both in the relations of the words’ meanings to one another and in relation to the range of objects and concepts, in order to show how language creates suitable expressions for every use in speech and designates the whole body of concepts that arise.

**107** Every language, whatever it may be, carries in its womb, at every moment of its existence, the expression of all the concepts that can ever develop in the nation. Moreover, at each moment of its life, every language exactly matches the range of the nation’s thought at that moment. Finally, in each of its states, every language forms a complete view of the world, since it contains expressions for all the presentations the nation forms of the world and all the feelings the world evokes in it.

**108** These propositions rest on the identity of thinking and speaking. Whatever a human being can think, they can also say. A nation’s thinking sometimes develops through mingling with another nation, in which case its language also receives additions from outside. But its thinking can also develop entirely from within and complete its whole course within itself, and where this succeeds, both nation and language flourish more and develop a more distinctive character. The thinking of the nation, made up of the thinking of each individual, must be a whole at every moment. For thinking is how a human being at every moment asserts their mental existence in relation to the world. Since this whole existence, which consists solely in this activity, necessarily comes together in each individual act of thinking, this cannot take place piecemeal or in a mutilated form.

**109** Those propositions are of the utmost importance for a proper appreciation of language. For they imply that at no moment of its life should one regard language as finished. It is always advancing, and everything in it is the seed of further development. Its progress is truly organic, the unfolding of one whole from another, rather than a mechanical addition of parts. What makes this character of language possible is what has already been discussed: articulated sounds can be rearranged and combined in all sorts of ways, so that entire families of word sounds (§79) arise from a single basic sound through small differences that leave the general resemblance intact. It is also made possible by shifting the same word towards related meanings, fixing the meanings of broadly synonymous words, and giving a new stamp to words previously used in other ways. Language grows from within through the creation of sounds and the creation of concepts. The creation of concepts goes on without limit, but the creation of sounds, as all experience shows, has a limit. Language reaches a point at which its wealth of sounds no longer increases from its own womb and can at most be increased by taking in foreign words. Its further elaboration then becomes, in a sense, more inward and is confined to fixing and modifying meanings. The complete lack of historical experience prevents us from saying anything more precise about this dividing point. We can see this much, however: it comes when the coming together and mingling of tribes ceases, either in itself or in its effect on language. For the only plausible way to conceive of any internal expansion of a language in its sounds is that different speakers of the same language introduce variations into it through their distinctiveness or separation. These then pass into the language as a whole, either already accompanied by differences of meaning or acquiring them only there. Everyone really wants to speak as others do in order to be understood, yet each person does in fact speak somewhat differently because each is physically and mentally different. This slight variation is still understood and taken up again. If we imagine this continuing for centuries among individuals, families and tribes, a great variety of word sounds can arise in a language. We often experience for ourselves how one dialect enriches another and the language at large.

**110** Insofar as the richness of a language means its ability to meet the need for expression, all languages may be called equally rich. For once one truly has command of a language, no concept or turn of thought can lack a suitable designation. Even so, examining the richness of a language is no idle inquiry, provided we are clear about what the term means. There are indeed different degrees and kinds of richness. What matters first is that every object or concept that stands out clearly as a distinct entity should find in the language a single word designating it without circumlocution. Second, objects should have the requisite variety of expression, according to the feeling with which one speaks and the mode of intuition them that one seeks to bring about. Third, there may also be several words for the same object without any perceptible difference in meaning or intention, and fourth, there may be several forms of the same word.

**111** It happens in all languages that what some express in a single word others express by a circumlocution of several words, and the designation may still be just as clear and precise. Yet it is by no means a matter of indifference if a language must often resort to circumlocution. The communication of thoughts, the immediate purpose of speech, certainly does not suffer thereby. The difference lies in the form of the language and its effect in turn on thinking as a whole. For we must never forget that the character of language as an instrument of thought has an immediate influence on thinking. It can heighten or diminish its clarity, precision, speed and sharpness, and turn it outwards towards the senses or inwards towards the mind. This is by far the most important aspect of language. Where every concept finds the word appointed for it, this richness stimulates the mind. Language appears like the world, where objects likewise crowd upon one another, and it is as a view of the world that language is meant to stand between nature and the human being. I shall return later to the point that it is of the utmost importance for language to leave behind the barren abstraction of thoughts and take up into its own form the form of external reality. This is borne out here as well. Even a language poor in words can express a host of concepts and sequences of ideas, and can do so clearly and precisely. An example is the impoverished use that some missionary writings make of non-European languages that are surely richer in themselves. The content of the thought may then have been adequately conveyed. But the impoverishment of the language drags down the mind, and thought so presented lacks fullness, freshness and vitality.

**112** For the same reasons, several words for the same concept, even if they offered scarcely perceptible differences, and several forms of the same word should not be regarded as a superfluous luxury. They increase the sensory fullness of the language and, as the nation’s mental life develops, make it possible to give concepts finer shades and determine them more precisely, at least through the use of the different expressions, even when the distinction cannot always be demonstrated in the dictionary. Even when languages such as English, formed by the convergence of several languages, have two expressions for certain concepts that differ only in origin, such as *freedom* and *liberty*, their use still makes a difference to discerning speakers who know the language well. In general, no kind of linguistic richness should be held in low esteem. Each kind shows that much was spoken in the language and in many different ways, and that many thoughts and feelings thus passed into it, whether this is due to the length of its existence, the number of tribes and individuals who spoke it, or the mental vigour of the nation. The more of the substance of human existence resounds in answer to the spirit of a language, the greater, livelier and more intellectual its power to stimulate the mind.[^resonance-recipient]

**113** To assess a language fully with respect to its vocabulary, one would have to compare that vocabulary with the whole body of possible concepts, comparing what is designated with what is to be designated. If what is meant is concepts in their purity, this is impossible, since the material of thought cannot be cleanly separated from the material of language. It is designation itself that completes the emergence before the mind of what is to be designated. This is true even of natural objects, for the word designates the view of them grounded in the language, rather than the objects simply as such. This difficulty, however, concerns only strict and fully precise analysis. Apart from that, one can certainly ask which natural objects have expressions originally peculiar to a given language. With non-sensory concepts, however, this separation of word and concept is much less feasible in some cases and cannot be attempted at all in others. We can, however, follow a comparable procedure that brings us at least somewhat closer to the aim. We can substitute the concepts actually designated in a number of languages for the concepts that would have to be thought in their purity, and gauge the body of what admits of and calls for designation by what tends to be designated in the various known languages. In this respect, the study of existing languages serves to examine the range of thinking and feeling that has actually existed among humankind. We can combine this method with an inquiry into the pure material of thought, considered apart from any particular language, by setting up general categories of objects and concepts and subdividing them into progressively narrower classes. If we constantly bear in mind that a particular language must come into play as soon as we descend to the individual concept, we can compare the material of thought to be designated with the vocabulary of a language without falling into error. Such a comparison is neither pure nor complete, but it is adequate for the study of language.

**114** Dictionaries arranged by category, such as Pollux’s and Amara’s *Cosha*, are useful aids here. Havestadt had the practical idea of arranging the words of the language of Chile in the same way.[^havestadt-title][^havestadt-reference] These works must, however, be made much more comprehensive. From as many languages as can be compared, one should be sure to collect the words that designate objects or give concepts a definite stamp, where those objects or concepts find no exhaustive expression in a word of their own in other languages. I must repeat here that, strictly and precisely speaking, no word in one language corresponds completely to a word in another. Here, however, we are concerned only with cases in which a word actually brings a new concept or an important new shade of a concept into being for those who were previously unfamiliar with the language. For the aim here is to discover the material of thought to be designated, and simply proceeding by categories and logical definitions cannot suffice for this unless we also take into account what has already actually been thought in language.

**115** We can and must therefore specify the objects and concepts for which a language has words. We must identify what it has designated in words on earth and in the heavens, among lifeless and living things, in animal and human bodies, and finally among concepts of qualities and relations and among non-sensory concepts. Where several languages have something in common, as the American languages do, even if only because the nations that speak them are similarly situated, it becomes important to see whether roughly the same domain encompasses the stock of concepts stamped into words in those languages. By comparing many languages, and always taking as our starting point that each offers a complete view of the world, we may well arrive at a domain of this kind that is, as it were, original and recurs in them all. Indeed, since on the whole the same world presents itself to all nations, this domain would have to be the same for them all, were it not that the amount of this world actually transformed into language depends on power and opportunity. It is easy here to follow the progress of discovering and naming particular objects. Of special importance, however, is tracing the modifications of intellectual concepts, which have by no means been followed precisely enough even in familiar languages.

**116** Within the vocabulary of a language a further question arises: which kinds of objects and concepts predominate in the richness of their expressions? Here too, then, it is important to distinguish them by class. For differences naturally arise in this respect from the general level of cultivation of nations, and also from habits, customs and ways of life, which give a greater variety of expressions to one branch of human activity or another, and thus to one class of objects and concepts or another. The general study of language must begin with the broadest classes and arrange its classification so as to show how language indicates the concept both to the understanding (§98) and to the ear. This may be called its technique of designation.

**117** Since this requires us to follow language’s own course, and every need for designation has its basis in thinking, all words fall in this respect into those that concern the matter of thinking, its object, and those that concern its form, its activity. For thinking needs words of its own to connect, separate and compare concepts, and to handle them according to its characteristic operations. These words may be called logical or dialectical in general. Their distinctive feature is that, viewed subjectively, they designate what is produced by an inner act. Viewed objectively, they designate only the connections and relations of concepts and things to one another and to the understanding that thinks them, rather than objects or properties of objects.

**118** The principal division in the matter of thought is naturally between the corporeal and the incorporeal. For the technique of designation, however, another division, more fundamental in this respect, must come first, namely the division into words for objects, qualities and actions. For objects always stimulate human beings through some salient feature of their quality or movement and thus prompt them to designate those objects chiefly by that feature. Hence words for qualities and movements hover above the others as the highest and most general words in the language. General words for relations and connections must also be counted here. Strictly speaking, since it is the understanding that relates concepts to one another, these words fall into the class of expressions that designate its activity (§117). Yet insofar as the relations and connections are considered to be present in the objects themselves, the words belong here too. One might object at this point that the distinction is merely grammatical. In fact, it is both lexical and grammatical in all languages. For in every language some words are, by origin and by virtue of their concepts, already nouns, words of the adjective or adverb class, or verbs. They belong to these classes lexically, even without grammatical marks. Grammar can then recast words that belong to one of these three classes into the other two. It is particularly important to see that in more than one language verbs that are not original are formed simply by combining an original verb with an equally original stem, purely lexically and without any help from grammar.[^verb-stem-ellipsis] All the more particular divisions follow from these main divisions. I pass over them here, since I am trying to give as brief an outline as possible of the general structure of language.

**119** The same classification of concepts also makes it easier to understand the technique of designation and furthers that understanding. Here I shall stay within the domain of thinking (§98) and discuss this technique in terms of how words are chosen for things and concepts on the basis of other concepts singled out in them or brought into relation with them, leaving sounds aside. These concepts are far from always discernible in the words. But it is exceedingly important to seek out those that can be discerned, since only then does the distinctive view of the world afforded by the language shine out clearly. Moreover, the whole way of designating through affinities among concepts, subjective and objective alike, is one of the most remarkable aspects of language. For the task is nothing less than to rise from the earliest and crudest needs of mutual communication to the full range of the highest ideas and their finest shades, while always linking a new word or a new meaning to something already there. Language is always subject to the general condition that mutual understanding must be possible, so speakers may go beyond what is already understood only as far as their hearers can follow.

**120** Language relates incorporeal concepts to corporeal ones, and the entities of the corporeal world to the general categories of quality and movement, though the second rule is less thoroughgoing than the first. It follows from both rules that language contains a number of general concepts of corporeal quality and movement that supply the principal material for designating all the others. These are therefore what one must particularly look for when investigating the vocabulary of a language. This inquiry can accordingly proceed from two sides, that of the designating concepts and that of the concepts to be designated. Comparing the results of these two procedures then shows the aptness of the designation and, in the selection and specification of the designating concepts, reveals the people’s prevailing inclination towards one mode of sensory intuition or another, as well as their mode of mental apprehension and its degree of clarity, precision, purity and depth.

**121** This gives us no reason to require a language to possess a set of root words that can be regarded as the source of all the rest. Even where such words exist, derived words are also used, and used most frequently, to designate new concepts. But the root words cannot be passed over here. For they plainly have a twofold significance, grammatical and lexical. In connected speech they can appear without grammatical alteration either not at all or only under certain conditions, and they serve as stem words for derivation and compounding. In some languages, such as Sanskrit and Welsh, it has become customary in lexicography to trace every word back to one of the posited roots. Yet there is probably no language of this structure in which striking examples could not be found to show how unfounded such a procedure is. Alongside the majority of words that lend themselves readily and easily to derivation from the roots, there are always many that can be brought under them only by forced and fanciful contrivance.

**122** Language has another means of increasing the richness of its vocabulary besides forming individual expressions. It marks features common to whole series of words and creates expressions by altering existing word sounds according to definite rules, without creating new word sounds. This often takes place through simple compounding, but also through derivation, whether by agglutination or by inflection. Examples include patronymics, gentilic names, abstract nouns, words of possession (a class found in almost all American languages), causative and volitive verbs, and so forth. For good practical reasons, it has become customary to include in the grammar whatever can be brought under rules, and to leave the dictionary to list individual cases alphabetically. That is why these methods of designation are now generally found in grammars. They clearly belong to the formation of vocabulary rather than to the combining of words in discourse. A closer and deeper examination of them is important, for it allows us to judge how logically precise and philosophically sound the language’s procedure is. For since we are dealing here with classes formed according to rules, what matters is how the basis of classification is conceived, whether all the kinds that follow from it are covered, and even whether chance or a rule governed the choice between designating these concepts by derivation, according to laws that must be learnt, and by compounding, in a way that is immediately intelligible. For it would serve the logical purpose better to form words according to laws only where the kinds to be designated can be derived, in their number and character, from concepts alone. The case is different where a particular factual relation determines the class of words, as with the concept of possession. There is another case in which the logical aptness and completeness of linguistic designation must be tested. Where a set of concepts together fills a sphere, the question is whether the language has a distinct expression for each and whether the expressions themselves mark the interrelation of the concepts. As examples I need only mention the dimensions of space and the points of the compass. In the dimensions of space, several languages use one expression for two distinct concepts, such as ‘narrow’ and ‘tight’ (*schmal* and *eng*), or ‘broad’ and ‘large’ (*breit* and *gross*). In the points of the compass, the expressions in many languages, such as German and Sanskrit, preserve the uniformity of the concepts. German does so by reference to the course of the sun that divides the day, and Sanskrit by reference to an assumed standpoint from which the sides are determined. In other languages, such as Latin, this uniformity is disrupted or altogether absent.

**123** The aim of this entire investigation of the vocabulary of languages, indicated here only in the barest outline, is to understand the system by which it is formed as precisely and completely as possible, so that one can see how much and what, out of the given range of possibilities, each language designates, and in what way. One must therefore survey the domain of what is to be designated in its various divisions (§§113–118), the stock of concepts brought into relation with others for purposes of designation (§§119–122), and finally the range of word sounds in both root words and derived and compound words, with attention to the character and treatment of the sounds (§§73–82).

**124** Once this is done, we have a conception of the material that language offers to discourse. We need only consider the feeling that accompanies words (§105), their effect in turn on the soul. So far this has been discussed only as part of the content of a word. Here we must consider together the distinctive way in which the language treats all its words. Just as the world appears in what is to be designated, so the human being appears here, and the two are related to one another as they present themselves in one and the same language. The greater or lesser readiness with which feeling is aroused, the lighter or darker colouring of fancy and the more prosaic or more poetic tendency emerge here as causes, just as they were recognized as effects in the objective formation of words.

**125** It would therefore serve no purpose to go into greater detail here. It is enough to observe that the vocabulary examined objectively up to this point must now be considered subjectively, to see whether a certain uniformity can be discerned in the feeling that prevailed in word formation and what kind of national distinctiveness is revealed in it. For the feeling that acts on the formation of a word is the same feeling that always accompanies it afterwards in use. I call it feeling partly to gather under the most general term everything in which language can bear traces of the human way of being, but chiefly because, at the moment it is active, what we are discussing really operates only as feeling, outside clear and distinct consciousness. For in other respects it encompasses, most notably, the purely intellectual tendency, as well as the whole subjective effect that the words of a language produce by virtue of belonging to it and that differs from the effect produced by the words of another language. It goes without saying that this effect in turn contributes to the language’s objective portrayal of the world. Yet many distinctions and divisions that linguistic inquiry requires lead less to genuinely different objects than to the consideration of different aspects of the same object. If, for example, the word formation of a language tends chiefly to keep the mind within the sphere of readily associating ideas for the purposes of life, it will above all be this intellectual disposition that ordinarily accompanies the language’s expressions for incorporeal things as well. This disposition will also have a further result. Even in designating their objects, these expressions will take them from a more superficial side. Nevertheless, to know the language fully in this respect, one must always consider together both this objective character and the subjective influence upon it. For the latter will then appear in the same way in other parts of the structure of the language, notably in the construction of discourse.

**126** The subjective disposition that plays a part in forming a word and is in turn produced by the word once formed, since cause and effect continually change places, adheres far less firmly to the dead word than does the designation of the object. If even the designation of the object is subject to change over time, as we have seen (§84), this holds far more strongly for the subjective impression. Elevated words sink to the level of common ones, poetic words become prosaic, and vice versa. The whole direction of a nation’s thinking and feeling changes imperceptibly. As this in turn acts upon designation (§125), sound and meaning seem to stay the same and do so on the whole, yet the altered apprehension of the meaning discloses a difference in it to closer scrutiny. This is how a very different sense gradually slips in under expressions for incorporeal things, such as *Seele* ‘soul’, *Geist* ‘mind’ or ‘spirit’, *Herz* ‘heart’ and *Gemüth* ‘inner life’, simply through the way they are apprehended. Conversely, when this happens in a few principal expressions, perhaps through the influence of individual writers, the nation’s cast of mind gradually changes, and this change then acts back on the entire language with increased force. Individual words taken in isolation rarely bear any discernible traces of these changes in the feeling that accompanies them, whether or not the changes affect their meanings. Only where literary records exist can these changes be traced. Moreover, since the causes of these changes lie solely in the nation’s mode of apprehension, and very sudden and abrupt transitions are possible in a person’s inner life, languages undergo these transformations, the very ones most decisive for their innermost nature, more rapidly, more frequently and more intensely than they undergo the transformations that come from shifts in meanings themselves as a whole and from the reshaping of sounds. The study of these changes belongs chiefly to the history of intellectual cultivation. Linguistic study often leaves them outside its scope and is justified in doing so if it confines itself to the material, in a sense coarser, part of language. If, as here, the aim is to show language in the full extent of its relation to human beings, the study must include these changes.[^closing-pronoun]

## Terminology guide

**Concept, object and presentation** (*Begriff*, *Gegenstand*, *Object*, *Vorstellung*, *Auffassung*). A *presentation* is how an object becomes present to someone’s mind. The object can be corporeal or formed by the mind. A word gives the concept a shape from a particular standpoint, and the presentation given by the word continues to act alongside the individual’s presentation of the object. *Apprehension* names the way of taking up the object.

**Word sound** (*Wortlaut*, *Laut*, *Ton*, *Schall*). The sound of a word can continue across generations while its content varies. The German terms for sound overlap. In §91 *Schall* draws attention to the audible sound that may stay the same even when the word as actually thought or spoken changes.

**Form, matter and material** (*Form*, *Materie*, *Stoff*, *Einbildungskraft*). In §90 a word’s *matter* is how it presents objects along the logical, sensory and affective lines set out in §87. Its *form* is how the faculties work together in those respects. In §89 sensory and affective content is transformed by imagination, which can preserve it as form. *Material* also names what the words provide for discourse in §72.

**Motivating meaning** (*Selbstbedeutung*, *Bezeichnung*, *Bedeutung*). The *motivating meaning* is what gives a word its particular way of designating a concept. It can lie in a perceptible feature of a thing or in a metaphor. Humboldt distinguishes cases where speakers still feel the motivation from those where a historical investigator can recover it.

**Word, sign and symbol** (*Wort*, *Zeichen*, *Symbol*). A sign in the strict sense designates something with an existence of its own. A concept takes its completed shape through a word. The natural form of a symbol can stand on its own, and its idea can be seen throughout that form. A word can have symbolic properties even though its sound and concept have the more tightly joined relation described in §101.

**View of the world** (*Weltansicht*). Each language makes a view of the world available through its ways of forming and designating concepts. There is room within that view for individual modes of presentation and for changes in language and thought over time.

**Stem word and root word** (*Stammwort*, *Wurzelwort*). A stem word is a starting point for an analysis of word formation. Root words can serve as stem words for derivation and composition and are constrained in connected discourse. The analysis traces relative starting points. Humboldt also rejects the forced assignment of every word to a proposed root.

**Feeling and the inner life** (*Gefühl*, *Empfindung*, *Gemüth*, *Stimmung*). The term *feeling* includes the intellectual orientation that operates without clear reflective awareness in forming and using words. §105 distinguishes particular emotions from a combined intellectual and affective disposition. *Inner life* renders *Gemüth* where its affective capacities matter.

**Words of possession** (*Eigenthumswörter*). Humboldt lists *words of possession* among families formed by a recurring procedure. He explains the basis of the class through a factual relation of possession. The passage gives no forms from which to identify a particular grammatical category.
