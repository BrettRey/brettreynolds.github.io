Countability notation

An asterisk marks a form that the book judges ungrammatical. These judgments are announced in the spoken text:

  • *There are two furnitures.
  • *two police were standing on the corner
  • *two pants
  • *I had cookie.
  • *two rices, when the intended meaning is “two grains of rice”

The parenthesized choice I had (a) tea becomes both I had tea and I had a tea in Why does this matter?. The acceptable comparison is I had a cookie. The transcript retains these spellings; pronunciation instructions never replace them.

The French example, from Countability across languages:

French J’ ai coupé mes cheveux.
Gloss I have cut my.PL hair.PL

Translation: “I cut my hair.” PL marks plural. The running French sentence is J’ai coupé mes cheveux. The line breaks and word alignment come from the source example labelled ex:cheveaux; the label’s spelling is retained as an identifier.

You can see from the list that some nouns can be counted and others can not. For example, if you want to tell your friend about the fruit on the tree, you could tell them your tree has plenty of plums on it. The noun ‘plum’ is countable so you have to make it plural by adding an ‘-s-’ (sic). Or you could tell them your tree has plenty of fruit on it. In this case, the noun ‘fruit’ is uncountable, so you can not count an ‘-s’.

— Rosemary Allen (2008: 1), as quoted in the chapter
