01. Title and learning objectives

· Section MP3

Chapter 10. Questions and interrogatives.

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to distinguish between questions, which are semantic, and interrogatives, which are syntactic, in form-function analysis. Identify basic interrogatives using subject-auxiliary inversion. Explain do-support rules for basic interrogatives lacking auxiliary verbs. And analyse focused interrogatives and their interrogative phrases, such as “who”, “what time”, “where”, and “how often”.

You should also be able to recognize when interrogative phrases function as subjects and when they function as non-subjects. Explain the inversion rules in focused interrogatives with non-subject focus. Apply diagnostic tests for identifying subjects and testing inversion patterns. And understand the semantic distinction between closed and open questions.

For teaching, you should be able to recognize common student errors in interrogative formation and their sources. And you should be able to draw phrase structure trees for basic and focused interrogatives, showing gaps and co-indexing.

02. Tests used in this chapter

· Section MP3

This chapter introduces several diagnostic tests for analysing interrogative structures.

First, the declarative-interrogative alternation: convert between declarative and interrogative forms to identify subjects.

Second, the subject identification test: use auxiliary placement to determine which NP functions as subject.

Third, inversion rule testing: test exceptions to subject-auxiliary inversion, for example interrogative phrase subjects.

Fourth, the plain form test: use “be” versus “is” in do-support constructions to verify verb forms.

And fifth, gap identification: analyse missing constituents and their syntactic relationships through co-indexing.

03. Introduction: a classroom exchange

· Section MP3

Questions feature prominently in education, no less so in language learning classrooms. Most of them are asked by teachers to students, and many are experienced as awkward by students because of a feeling that the answer is obvious. Consider the following interaction, reproduced from Nunn, nineteen ninety-nine.

The teacher says: “And then we know that Mister Archer came on his own before... without anybody. He left his family in England and he came on his own to the Gulf. So he had to look for a house to live in first before he starts anything else and as you know he has found a house. And this house... who can tell me something about this house? What does does this house consist of? Tell me about this house. Yes, Mohanned.”

The student says: “It's a villa with a large garden.”

And the teacher says: “Yes. It's a villa with a large garden. That's right.”

04. Introduction: what the exchange achieved

· Section MP3

I don't know about you, but I find this hard to read. The teacher was probably thinking something perfectly reasonable: “I've been talking a lot. Maybe I should give the students a chance,” at which point, the question just popped out. Good on Mohanned for helping everyone get on with things. But what was actually achieved?

If the goal was to offer the students an opportunity to practice speaking, this seems wholly insufficient. In a class of who knows how many students, one said an eight-word sentence, counting the contracted “is” as a word of its own, that took all of, perhaps, three seconds, before the speaking turn reverted to the teacher. Nor was it likely to clarify the meaning of the text; the teacher even says, “as you know,” so, was anyone even confused about the nature of the house? And, really, did it matter if they were? It could have been a bungalow or an apartment for all the difference it made to the educational value of the interaction.

05. Introduction: questions as speech acts

· Section MP3

As John Searle observed, we do things with language, and questioning is one kind of thing we do. But in this case, it's akin to asking a guest if they would like a seat when they are already comfortably seated. Such a question is not just redundant but also disrupts the flow of social interaction, highlighting a disconnect between the speaker's intention and the situation. Similarly, in the classroom, the teacher's question, though well-intentioned, ends up creating an awkward moment that does little to advance the educational or social dynamics of the setting.

Of course, it's not quite as cringe-worthy as the seat offer would be. There's a script playing out here, and both the students and the teacher know how it's supposed to go. But that only goes some way to reducing the awkwardness of this meaningless question, and it doesn't mean that the exchange achieved anything worth the class time.

A key insight about the language landscape is that, in focusing on language forms, we risk overlooking the crux of communication: its purpose. Different forms have different purposes; in Searle's words, they perform speech acts. Fostering a classroom environment where students partake in genuine and natural speech acts leads to a more positive and comfortable learning experience.

06. Questions versus interrogatives

· Section MP3

A question is something that asks for an answer, whether it gets one or not. But questions are not all linguistic. Sure, they're typically speech acts, or things we do with language, but they don't have to be. If you walk into your kitchen and find a gift on your table, this might pose a number of questions, questions about its source and contents, perhaps about the intended recipient or even about the method of delivery. These don't even have to be formulated into language, let alone uttered to be questions.

Of course we often do use language to ask questions. The most common way to do so is through the use of an interrogative clause, such as “What's that gift doing there?” and “Is it for me?” Later in the book we'll deal with subordinate clauses, those that appear inside larger clauses, but for now, we'll focus on main-clause interrogatives.

Recalling the discussion of the many-to-many relationships between categories and functions in Chapter 3, we find the same kind of relationship with interrogatives and questions. As the gift example illustrates, questions don't have to be in the form of interrogatives; they don't even have to be linguistic. But, even if they are expressed in language, they can take non-interrogative forms. “That's for me?” is a question, hence the question mark, but not an interrogative clause. You could even just ask “for me?” which isn't even a clause, let alone an interrogative.

Conversely, an interrogative doesn't have to be a question. It could be a threat, “do you want to try that again?” An offer, “how would you like some help?” An instruction, “why don't you try again?” An evaluation, “is that the best you can do?” Or any number of other speech acts.

07. Terminological hygiene: one word, several meanings

· Section MP3

Terminological hygiene.

Words almost never have a single correct meaning. Obviously, “cookie” does not mean “in an upwards direction”, but it does mean “a small file stored on a user's computer by a web browser for tracking or data retrieval purposes” as well as “a sweet baked treat, often containing ingredients such as chocolate, nuts, or raisins”. I've also taken pains to make clear that just because a term such as “phrase” has a technical meaning along with non-technical meanings, it's not true that the non-technical meaning is generally wrong. It just depends on what one means: In the appropriate technical environment, the technical meaning should take priority.

That said, careless use of terminology often leads to confusion. One common problem in discussions of language is re-using terms dealing with diverse aspects of language. For instance, in the field of semantics, “subject” means “the main topic or the primary entity that the statement is about”, but for the syntactician it means “the function of a constituent in a clause that normally precedes the verb and, in active clauses, normally denotes the actor, if there is one, but in passive clauses, normally denotes whatever is acted upon”. This can be a problem.

08. Terminological hygiene: keeping questions and interrogatives apart

· Section MP3

Technical terms are often drawn from existing words. Instead of making up a totally new one like “predicore” or “maintent” to denote the technical meaning that syntacticians and semanticists have in mind when they use “subject”, both fields draw on the same non-technical vocabulary. The result is that many people coming to linguistics or language teaching for the first time are confused.

Another example of such confusing terminological overlap is “predicate”, which, in syntax refers to “the VP that is the Head of the clause”, while in semantics, means “a relation or property that is ascribed to one or more entities”. It's the conceptual meaning or function that links the semantic subject to some assertion or description about it. In the example “the cat sleeps”, the semantic predicate is the concept of sleeping that is attributed to the entity cat.

I've avoided this kind of overlapping terminology here, and I encourage you to do so in your teaching too, within reason. This is just to avoid confusion, not because certain uses of terms are correct or incorrect. So, whenever I use the term “question”, for example, I mean it semantically, and whenever I use “interrogative”, I mean it syntactically.

09. Main-clause interrogatives: basic and focused

· Section MP3

Main-clause interrogatives can be broadly divided into those with an interrogative word, for example “What is it?”, and those without, for example “Could it be a platypus?” I'll use the term “focused” for those that include an interrogative word and “basic” for the others that start with an auxiliary verb.

A note on terminology. CGEL uses “open” and “closed” for these interrogative types, but since those terms describe semantic question types, applying them to syntactic forms can be misleading. This chapter therefore uses “basic” and “focused” to describe the form of the clauses. Subordinate interrogative clauses, like “I wonder who it could be”, will be dealt with in Chapter 18.

10. Basic interrogatives and inversion

· Section MP3

Basic interrogatives.

Basic interrogatives invert the typical subject-verb order. The declarative “Today is a lovely day” becomes the basic interrogative “Is today a lovely day?”

The first figure shows this. The declarative sits above the interrogative, and arrows show “is” and “today” switching places. The caption reads: the subject and the auxiliary verb “is” switch places in the basic interrogative.

11. Only auxiliary verbs invert

· Section MP3

Not just any verb can participate in this inversion; only auxiliary verbs. In contemporary English, you can ask “Are they going?” with the auxiliary verb “are” in front of the subject “they”, but not “Go they?” with the lexical verb “go” in front. The book marks “Go they?” as ungrammatical. A footnote adds that “Go they” was possible until the end of the eighteenth century, citing Lass, nineteen ninety-nine.

I introduced the auxiliary verbs in Chapter 1 as those that have the NICER properties, and this kind of inversion is the I in NICER.

12. The NICER properties again

· Section MP3

Here's the NICER table, with an auxiliary example and a counter-example for each property. The book marks the counter-examples as ungrammatical, and I'll say so.

Negation. “Lee will not eat” is fine. “Lee eats not” is ungrammatical.

Inversion. “Lee has eaten” becomes “Has Lee eaten?” But “Eats Lee?” is ungrammatical.

Contraction. “Didn't” and “shouldn't” are fine. “Eatn't” and “keptn't” are ungrammatical.

Ellipsis. “Kim was eating, and Lee was, too” works, with the eating understood and not pronounced. “Kim kept eating, and Lee kept, too” is ungrammatical.

Rebuttal. “I did too read it”, or “I did so read it”, with stress on “too” or “so”, can be answered with “Did not!” But “I tried too to read it”, or “I tried so to read it”, is ungrammatical, and so is “Tried not!”

13. Subject-auxiliary inversion and learners

· Section MP3

The other part of the inversion is the subject, which is introduced in Chapter 3. There, I mentioned that subjects typically precede the Head VP, but that in a basic interrogative, such as “Could they be right?”, the subject comes immediately after an auxiliary verb. Subject-auxiliary inversion is a key aspect of basic interrogatives, and that's actually kinda weird. Most languages don't make questions this way, and many language learners struggle with it.

Instead of asking “Is this right?” they'll ask “This is right?” And of course, that's perfectly clear in most contexts, though, by default, it has the pragmatic force of requesting a clarification or expressing a doubt, rather than that of questioning.

14. Identifying the subject

· Section MP3

Identifying the subject.

Sometimes, it's hard to find the subject. When you're stuck, alternating between a declarative and a basic interrogative can be helpful. Consider two pairs.

In the first pair, the declarative is “Tuesday could be a good day to fly to Paris.” The book marks “Tuesday” as the subject. The basic interrogative is “Could Tuesday be a good day to fly to Paris?”

In the second pair, the declarative is “Tuesday, if things work out, you could fly to Paris.” The book marks “you”. The basic interrogative is “Tuesday, if things work out, could you fly to Paris?”

In the first pair, the first verb is the past-tense modal auxiliary “could”. Modal auxiliaries don't have third-person singular forms, even in the present, and this is the past. As a result, agreement provides no clues about the subject. But in the basic interrogative, “could” has moved in front of “Tuesday”, which almost certainly means that “Tuesday” is the subject.

The second pair also lacks any agreement signals about the subject. But in the basic interrogative, “could” is in front of “you”, which means that “Tuesday” can't be the subject; it must be “you”.

15. More basic interrogatives

· Section MP3

Here are some more examples. In the book, the auxiliary is in small capitals and the subject is in italics; I'll name each one.

First, different forms of auxiliary “be”. “Is rain ever warm?” The auxiliary is “is” and the subject is “rain”. “Are the evenings sometimes too bright?” The auxiliary is “are” and the subject is “the evenings”. “Am I functioning normally?” The auxiliary is “am” and the subject is “I”. “Last night, was the moon, perhaps, broken?” The auxiliary is “was” and the subject is “the moon”.

Next, different modal auxiliary verbs. “Will mornings ever surprise you?” The auxiliary is “will” and the subject is “mornings”. “Could silence be too loud?” The auxiliary is “could” and the subject is “silence”.

And two forms of auxiliary “have”, as opposed to the lexical verb “have”. “In your experience, have dreams lost their way?” The auxiliary is “have” and the subject is “dreams”. “Had the silent night revealed its secrets before?” The auxiliary is “had” and the subject is “the silent night”.

16. What the examples show

· Section MP3

You can see from these examples that, even with inversion, the auxiliary verb still agrees with the person and number of the subject, when possible: “is” is the right form to agree with singular “rain”, “are” for plural “the evenings”, and “am” for first-person singular “I”.

I included “Last night, was the moon, perhaps, broken?” to show that the auxiliary verb doesn't necessarily appear as the first element of the clause; other elements, the NP “last night” in this case, may come before it. The auxiliary verb appears before the subject, but not always before everything. The same is true of “In your experience, have dreams lost their way?”: the auxiliary precedes the subject, but it still follows the PP “in your experience”.

17. Do-support

· Section MP3

Do-support.

In all the examples so far, both the interrogative clauses and their corresponding declaratives would have an auxiliary verb. For example, the declarative counterpart of “Will mornings ever surprise you?” is “Mornings will sometimes surprise you”, with “sometimes” in parentheses in the book, and with the modal auxiliary verb “will”. But lots of declarative clauses, perhaps most, don't have an auxiliary verb available for subject-auxiliary inversion. In such cases, we recruit “do”, in its appropriate form. This is called do-support.

If inverting the subject and the auxiliary to form a question is a bit odd, magically pulling some form of “do” out of thin air and having it appear in front of the subject seems even stranger. It's no wonder language learners have problems with this. Still, it is the way we do it in Standard English, as you can see in these examples.

“Do coconuts migrate?” “Does the Holy Grail actually exist?” “Did Sir Robin bravely run away?” “Do knights prefer saying ‘Ni’ to ‘Hello’?” “Does the Norwegian Blue pine for the fjords?”

18. Tense moves to do

· Section MP3

There's an extra step to watch out for here. The declarative counterpart of “Does the Holy Grail actually exist?” would be “The Holy Grail actually exists”, with that third-person singular “s” on the present-tense verb. But in the basic interrogative, it's present-tense “do” that agrees with the third-person singular, so “do” becomes “does”, and “exists” becomes “exist”. The next figure shows this change under do-support with third-person, singular, present tense: the “s” of “exists” ends up as the “es” of “does”.

The figure after that shows do-support with past tense. Again, the tense is marked on “do” and not on the lexical verb: the “ed” of “existed” ends up as the “d” of “did”.

19. A verb-form puzzle

· Section MP3

A verb-form puzzle.

In “Does the Holy Grail actually exist?”, isn't “exist” also present tense? So, shouldn't it also agree? In fact, “exist” is the plain form of the verb: there is only one tensed verb in this clause and that's “do”, in the form “does”. This is surprisingly difficult to show, and I can't think of a way to do it with a basic interrogative, but I'll return to the issue after discussing focused interrogatives. In the meantime, try to think it through.

20. Closed questions

· Section MP3

Semantics.

Basic interrogatives are usually used to ask closed questions. One kind of closed question is the yes-no question. If I ask you, “Is rain ever warm?” then I basically expect you to either confirm or deny that rain is at least sometimes warm. You could say, “Yes, it is”, or “Sometimes”, or “I think so”, but “Aardvark” is not an answer. “I don't know” may be a valid response, but it too doesn't qualify as an answer. So, such a question has a closed set of answers that, specific wording aside, boil down to yes or no.

A footnote adds that CGEL uses “closed interrogative” for the syntactic construction I'm calling the basic interrogative.

Another kind of closed question offers a set of alternatives. For example, “Would you like to eat at five thirty, seven o'clock, or seven thirty?” closes the set of answers to one of those three times. Pragmatically, you could respond with indifference, express a love for food, comment on other obligations you have, suggest another time, or decline the offer of dinner altogether. But strictly within the semantic framework, none of those responses qualify as direct answers. The only ones that count as answers are those that state or imply a preference for one of the three times.

22. Interrogatives that aren't questions

· Section MP3

And on the topic of semantics, I would remind you that, syntactically, basic interrogative clauses are not always semantically questions. They can also be exclamations, “Isn't this the best cake you've ever tasted?” Expressions of surprise, “Are you serious?” Insults, “Did an infant dress you?” Advice, “Wouldn't it be better to read the instructions?” And so on. So, while the structure may be interrogative, the intended meaning can diverge from straightforward questioning.

23. Intonation in closed questions

· Section MP3

Intonation.

In most dialects, yes-no questions typically have a rising intonation, while alternative questions fall on the final alternative after rising on each previous one. I'll describe these patterns instead of demonstrating them; the figure marks them with arrows. In the figure, “Is rain ever warm?” ends with an upward arrow. For “Would you like to eat at five thirty, seven o'clock, or seven thirty?” the figure puts an upward arrow after “five thirty” and after “seven o'clock”, and a downward arrow after the last alternative, “seven thirty”.

This can vary quite a bit though, depending on the attitude behind the question, such as mere curiosity, doubt, anger, and so on, whether it is said in isolation or as one in a series of questions, and various other factors. The book cites Bartels, nineteen ninety-nine.

24. Focused interrogative clauses

· Section MP3

Focused interrogative clauses.

Focused interrogatives typically start with an interrogative word: “who”, including “whose” and, with decreasing frequency, “whom”; “what”, “which”, “when”, “where”, “why”, or “how”. A footnote adds that there are also some older or archaic words that students don't need to learn, including “whence”, “whither”, and some compound prepositions such as “whereby” and “wherein”.

This is an important group of words, a group that has a central syntactic role in forming interrogative phrases and clauses and a central semantic role in asking questions. But “interrogative word” isn't a lexical category like pronoun or auxiliary verb; interrogative words belong to many different lexical categories, as shown in the table. 26. Interrogative phrases

· Section MP3

Interrogative phrases.

The interrogative words appear in interrogative phrases, such as the ones in these four examples. Sometimes they're the Head and the only word in the phrase, as in “Who's the kid?”, where “who” is the Head of an NP. Sometimes they're the Head along with one or more Dependents, as in “Precisely who called you?” And sometimes, they are a Dependent somewhere in a larger phrase, as in “What time did they arrive?” and “At what time did they arrive?”

In both of those, “what” functions as the Determiner in the NP “what time”, but in the last one, the interrogative phrase is the whole “at” PP, which includes the NP, which, in turn, includes “what”.

27. More interrogative phrases, and phrases on their own

· Section MP3

Common interrogative phrases, apart from the individual words already listed, include “how many”, “how often”, “how long”, “what day”, and “since when”. There are also a number with “how” that are semantically interrogative but function syntactically unlike the others. These include “How come you did that?”, “How about if we take a break?”, and “What about this one?”

Interrogative phrases work very well as questions all on their own, given the right context. If somebody says, “They're arriving tomorrow,” then “What time?” is a perfectly reasonable way to ask what time they are arriving.

28. Interrogative phrase subjects

· Section MP3

Interrogative phrase subjects.

Cases like this are the easiest. “Who” is a pronoun that is the Head of an NP, and that NP functions as the subject of the focused interrogative clause. Starting with “He's the kid”, we just replace “he” with “who”, and we're done: “Who's the kid?”

And, as long as the interrogative phrase is the subject, then focused interrogative clauses work like this. Here are various such examples.

29. Examples with a subject interrogative phrase

· Section MP3

In each pair, the declarative comes first, and the interrogative phrase replaces its subject.

“That smells” becomes “What smells?”

“This time works for me” becomes “Roughly what time works for you?”

“Nobody can come” becomes “Which of your friends can come?”

“One person was there” becomes “How many people were there?”

Notice that in the last pair, the question is asked in the plural.

30. Subject-auxiliary inversion and fronting

· Section MP3

Subject-auxiliary inversion and fronting.

But it's not always the case that the interrogative phrase is the subject. When it's not, we're back to subject-auxiliary inversion and sometimes do-support, as with the basic interrogatives. And again, this can cause difficulties for students, who often produce uninverted clauses. The inversion is shown in three steps. First, “It is a pen.” Second, “It is what?” Third, “What is it?” In the book, a gap marker follows “it” in the third, and the word “fronted” labels the move.

There are a few things to observe here. First, “it” functions as the subject in all three examples, even when it's the last word in the clause. Second, “a pen” functions as a complement in the VP in the first, as does “what” in the second. And then there's the fronted interrogative phrase in the third. I mentioned fronting and stranding back in Chapter 4, in the discussion of preposition stranding.

That phrase is now in what we'll call Pre function, which comes up again in the last section of this chapter. The auxiliary verb is before the subject, and there's just an empty space where the complement in the VP used to be. I'm going to claim that there really is a gap there, and its function really is the same as that of “a pen” in the first example: it's still a complement.

31. Examples with a fronted interrogative phrase

· Section MP3

Here are a few more examples. Each shows the declarative first, then the interrogative. In the book, a gap marker shows where the fronted phrase came from.

“They can't smell it” becomes “What can't they smell?”

“I'm free until five o'clock” becomes “Until when are you free?”

“I should see these then” becomes “Which should you see then?”

“It might be bad since I lost” becomes “Why might it be bad?”

“I will want you to do it” becomes “Who will you want to do it?”

“You can do it like this” becomes “How can I do it?”

And “I was speaking about that” becomes “About what was I speaking?”

32. Pronouns and PP fronting

· Section MP3

As the example with “How can I do it?” shows, when using singular “you” or “I”, the declarative and the interrogative typically have contrasting pronouns: I ask about you and you answer “I”; I ask about myself and you answer “you”. For this reason, unless you're making this specific point, I avoid “you” and “I” examples when explaining interrogatives. That said, as “About what was I speaking?” shows, sometimes we speak to ourselves.

That last example is also another example of PP fronting. Notice that all the other examples, apart from “Until when are you free?”, have interrogative phrases that start with, and are limited to, an interrogative word. Only the interrogative PPs “until when” and “about what” start with a non-interrogative word.

34. Testing the inversion rule

· Section MP3

Testing the inversion rule.

At this point, you might be forming a generalization: “All English questions require subject-auxiliary inversion.” This seems reasonable given basic interrogatives, such as “Is it raining?”, and most focused interrogatives, such as “What did you eat?” But recall the counterexample habit from Chapter 1: when a rule seems too tidy, that's when to hunt for exceptions.

Look back at the section on interrogative phrase subjects. The examples there, “Who's the kid?”, “What smells?”, and “Which of your friends can come?”, all lack inversion. Why? Because when the interrogative phrase itself functions as the subject, there's nothing to invert with. The interrogative phrase is already in subject position, doing subject things like controlling agreement: “How many people were there?”, not “How many people was there?”, which the book marks as ungrammatical.

35. Exceptions to inversion

· Section MP3

This pattern extends beyond simple cases. Consider three more. “What happened next?” The book marks “What did happen next?” as ungrammatical. “Who told you that?” The book marks “Who did tell you that?” as ungrammatical. “Which solution works best?” The book marks “Which solution does work best?” as ungrammatical. In each, that holds with ordinary stress on “did” and “does”.

Students who overgeneralize the inversion rule produce errors like “Who did come to the party?”, marked ungrammatical, when asking for new information, not emphasis. The revised rule? “English questions require subject-auxiliary inversion except when the interrogative phrase is the subject.” This exception isn't rare; subject-position questions are common in classroom discourse: “Who knows the answer?”, “What comes next?”, “Which group wants to present first?”

Now search for more patterns: Can you find other systematic exceptions to inversion? What about echo questions, such as “You said what?!”, with stress on “what”, or declarative questions with rising intonation, such as “You're coming?” Each exception refines our understanding of how English actually forms questions.

Pause the recording and see what you can find.

36. Do-support in focused interrogatives

· Section MP3

Do-support inversion.

Just as with basic interrogatives, subject-auxiliary inversion works fine with focused interrogatives when there is an auxiliary in the corresponding declarative. But when there isn't, then we once again lean on “do” for support. Here are a few more examples, with the declarative first and the interrogative second.

“They smelled it” becomes “What did they smell?”

“I get free after five o'clock” becomes “When do you get free?”

“I see them then” becomes “Who do you see then?”

“It seems bad since I lost” becomes “Why does it seem bad?”

“It looks good” becomes “How does it look?”

And, just like before, the tense moves from the lexical verb to “do”, and the lexical verb appears in the plain form. For example, in “What did they smell?”, past-tense “smelled” becomes plain-form “smell”, and past-tense “did” is used. Similarly, in “When do you get free?”, present-tense “get” becomes plain-form “get”, only this time, the forms share a shape, so the change is surreptitious.

37. A second verb-form puzzle

· Section MP3

A verb-form puzzle, again.

Earlier, I brought up the question of whether or not “exist” is present tense in “Does the Holy Grail actually exist?” Now, I can use an interrogative to show you that it's clearly the plain form.

Compare three sentences. “The cat is very loud.” That's grammatical. “Does it ever just is quiet?” The book marks that as ungrammatical. And “Does it ever just be quiet?” That's grammatical.

If we try to use the present-tense form of “be”, the result is the ungrammatical “Does it ever just is quiet?” It's the plain-form “be” in the last sentence that is correct. The reason this is so difficult to show is that “be” is the only verb that has a plain form that is distinct from the plain present-tense form.

38. Asking about an activity

· Section MP3

Finally, there's the special case of asking about an activity. In such cases, since the speaker, who may or may not know what the activity is or was, assumes that the listener knows, we use lexical, not auxiliary, “do” as a stand-in for the activity. The structure, though, is the same.

“What do you do?” and “What did you do?” exhibit do-support: auxiliary “do” or “did” together with lexical “do”. “What might you do?”, “What can you do?”, and “What will you do?” show subject-auxiliary inversion with modal auxiliaries. “What are you doing?” and “What were you doing?” do the same, but with the progressive aspect, as do “What have you done?” and “What had you done?”, with perfect aspect.

39. Summary: the three types

· Section MP3

Summary.

So in sum, there are three different types of interrogatives. The decision tree in the figure asks a series of questions.

First: is there an interrogative phrase? If not, the clause is basic. If there is, the clause is focused, and the next question is: is the interrogative phrase the subject? If so, the clause is non-inverting. If not, it's inverting, and the last question is: does the declarative have an auxiliary verb? If it does, use subject-auxiliary inversion. If it doesn't, use do-support.

40. Open questions

· Section MP3

Semantics.

It will be familiar by now that interrogatives can do more than ask questions, but, in general, the focused interrogatives are used to ask open questions. A footnote adds that CGEL uses the term “open interrogatives” for focused interrogatives, which typically ask open questions.

These are open in that, when you ask, “Who did you see?” for example, the answer is limited only by the requirement that it be a person or persons. Otherwise, the possibilities are open. The main exception to this is “which”, which expects answers from a closed set, sometimes explicit, for example “Which would you like, small, medium, or large?”, and sometimes inferrable from the context, for example “Which one looks best?”

41. Intonation in open questions

· Section MP3

Intonation.

In general, open questions, and therefore most focused interrogative clauses, end with falling intonation.

42. Questions in the classroom and classroom questions

· Section MP3

Questions in the classroom and classroom questions.

Even before students start learning the ins and outs of interrogative clauses, they're able to ask questions: simple interrogative phrases, like “how long?” for “How long will it take?” or declarative clauses with rising intonation, such as “We have a test?” for “Do we have a test?”

The first is perfectly natural and accurate, and should not be met with an injunction to “use complete sentences”. Usually, if the student has chosen an interrogative-phrase question, a complete sentence would contain awkward redundancy, making the asker appear stiff and foreign. In the second case though, the student's high-terminal declarative is inappropriate, unless the teacher has already said something about a test. That's because the speech act evoked from such a clause is not a straightforward closed question. Rather, it's a request for confirmation, clarification, or restatement. The distinction is subtle, but consider Person A's second turn in this exchange.

43. A request for clarification

· Section MP3

Person A says: “I saw a poster about that concert you mentioned last week.”

Person B says: “Oh, the one at the park? Yeah, the whole team's going.”

Person A says, with rising intonation: “You're going?”

And Person B says: “Yeah, do you want to come?”

“You're going?” is a request to clarify whether “the team” includes Person B. The question “Are you going?” might suggest A wasn't paying attention because, from Person B's viewpoint, they've just told A they're going. By framing the question as a declarative, A acknowledges this possibility while asking for clarification. In other words, high-terminal declaratives have their place, but it's not a general question-asking form, and students should understand the difference.

44. Subordinate interrogatives used as questions

· Section MP3

There's one more common questioning strategy that students use. The clauses covered in this chapter are main clauses, but students sometimes employ subordinate interrogative clauses, which come in Chapter 18, as questions. For example, a student may ask, pointing, “how to say this word?” This is a subordinate interrogative clause, and as such, it's perfectly fine inside another clause: “Could you tell me how to say this word?” or “I wonder how to say this word.” But it's not Standard to use such clauses on their own to ask questions. Instead we'd use a focused interrogative with do-support: “How do you say this word?”

45. Classroom questions to memorize

· Section MP3

Classroom questions.

A good way to start learning how to ask questions is to memorize some, and a good set to memorize is the set of questions that come up in the language classroom regularly. Here are some ideas, but I'm sure you can think of others. Where the book has a blank, I'll say “blank”.

One. “What does blank mean?”

Two. “How do you spell blank?”

Three. “How do you say this word?” or “How do you say that word?”, while pointing.

Four. “How do you say”, followed by a word in your first language, “in English?”

Five. “Can you give me an example?”

Six. “Is this okay?”

Seven. “Can I say blank?”

Eight. “Do you have a blank?”

Nine. “Can I borrow a blank?”

Ten. “Do we have any homework?”

Eleven. “When is it due?”

Twelve. “Did I spell this right?”

Thirteen. “Why do we use this tense here?”

Fourteen. “What's the past tense of blank?”

Fifteen. “What's your email address?”

Sixteen. “When do we use blank?”

Seventeen. “Could you repeat that, please?”

Eighteen. “What's the difference between blank and blank?”

Nineteen. “Where can I find out more about blank?”

And twenty. “What should we do next?”

46. Phrase structure: the tree for a basic interrogative

· Section MP3

Phrase structure. Basic interrogatives with subject-auxiliary inversion.

Trees may help you think about the structure of basic interrogatives. The tree for the basic interrogative clause “Could they be right?” is shown in the figure, on the left.

At the top is a Clause with two parts. The first part is a Prenucleus, which is the auxiliary verb “could”, marked with the subscript x. The second part is a Head, which is another Clause. That inner Clause has a Subject, the NP “they”, and a Head, a VP. The Head of the VP is a gap, also marked x, and its Complement is a VP, “be right”, drawn as a triangle.

47. Co-indexing

· Section MP3

The first thing to notice about the trees is the x subscripts after the auxiliary verb and the gap. Linguists call such a mark a co-index. It's the “co” because it's shared between two, or more, elements, and it's the “index” because, like an index in the back of the book, it points you to something you want to find: if you're wondering where “could” came from, you check the index, and it tells you it came from the position that now says gap.

48. Gaps

· Section MP3

The next notable element is the gap itself, which is a placeholder. The VP, like all phrases, needs a Head, but in this case, the Head is the gap that's co-indexed to “could”, rather than being a verb, as we would usually expect for the Head of a VP. Such gaps are more important for linguistic theory than for language teaching, but as we see more gaps in this chapter and others that follow, I expect you'll come to see them as important and useful tools for your own thinking about phrase structure.

49. The Pre function

· Section MP3

Having looked at these two crucial elements, let's go through the tree on the left as a review. Starting at the top, we see that it has an auxiliary verb, “could”, in Prenucleus function, which is the key element of the basic interrogative clause type. Since “prenucleus” is an ugly, ponderous word used by virtually nobody outside the CGEL framework, I will simply call it Pre from now on. There will be a Post later.

Be sure to understand that subject-auxiliary inversion does not mean that the subject somehow becomes the auxiliary and vice versa. As the tree shows, the subject remains the subject, and the auxiliary verb appears in the Pre function.

50. The Head clause

· Section MP3

Next, there is the Head clause: “they”, gap, “be right”. At first glance, it may seem strange to have a clause as the Head of another clause. But this is an essential aspect of understanding basic interrogatives. In our example, the main clause functions as the Head of the overall interrogative structure. This arrangement is due to the unique positioning of “could” in Pre function. It has to be pre-something, and this shows it as preceding the central clause.

In the tree, the Head clause includes a subject NP, “they”, and a Head VP, represented by the gap where “could” would normally be. The VP “be right” acts as a Complement, and its internal structure is simplified as a triangle here. I encourage you to sketch this tree yourself to grasp its structure fully. Compare your version with the figure, and don't be discouraged if you miss some elements initially. Practice will help you capture all the components accurately.

51. Basic interrogatives with do-support

· Section MP3

Basic interrogatives with do-support.

A tree with do-support looks, structurally, just like one with subject-auxiliary inversion, such as the tree for “Could they be right?” The tree for the basic interrogative “Do you like pizza?” is shown in the right-hand figure. Again, the auxiliary verb “do” is in Pre function, again the rest of the clause follows as the Head, and again, there's a gap in the VP.

52. Focused interrogatives with subject focus

· Section MP3

Focused interrogatives with subject focus.

Next is the focused interrogative. Again, we'll start with the easiest case, the one with the interrogative phrase as the subject. The example in the next figure is “Who plays the piano?” There's no Pre and no gap, just “who” functioning as the subject. It couldn't be easier, and language learners rarely make mistakes with this clause structure.

In the tree, the Clause has a Subject, the NP “who”, and a Head, a VP. The Head of the VP is the verb “plays”, and its Object is the NP “the piano”.

53. Focused interrogatives with non-subject focus

· Section MP3

Focused interrogatives with non-subject focus.

When the interrogative phrase, that is, the focus, is not the subject, then things get more complex and language learners are much more likely to form questions lacking inversion, like “What you can play?” In “What can you play?”, shown in the figure on the right, there are two constituents in Pre function, two gaps, and two different co-indices, x and y. Each co-index connects the Pre to its relevant gap.

In words: the outer Pre is the NP “what”, marked x. Inside the Head clause is a second Pre, the auxiliary “can”, marked y. After the subject “you” comes a VP whose Head is a gap marked y, and whose Complement is a VP with the verb “play” as its Head and an Object that is a gap marked x.

The functions of the gaps are clearer here. “Play” is a transitive verb, the kind that takes an Object, so there's an Object gap. While we might say informally that “what” is the Object of “play”, that would go against the idea that an Object is a Dependent in the VP, so we give “what” a different function, Pre, but connect it back to the Object through the x co-index.

54. Summary

· Section MP3

Summary.

Every student error in question formation traces to a specific point in the system: inversion applied where it shouldn't be, do-support omitted, subject focus mishandled. When a student says “Where you are going?”, which the book marks as ungrammatical, you can now locate the gap, inversion missing, and explain why the student's logic was sound even though the result wasn't.
