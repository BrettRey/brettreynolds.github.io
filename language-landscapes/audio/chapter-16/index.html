01. Title and learning objectives

· Section MP3

Chapter 16. Pragmatics.

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to distinguish between semantic meaning and pragmatic meaning in communication. Apply Grice's cooperative principle and conversational maxims to analyse implied meanings. And identify different types of speech acts and their performative functions.

You should also understand politeness strategies and face-saving mechanisms in cross-cultural communication. Recognize how context shapes the interpretation of utterances in real-world language use. And analyse conversational implicatures and how they arise from maxim violations.

For teaching, you should be able to apply pragmatic principles to improve language teaching and intercultural communication, and understand why pragmatic competence is essential for effective second language use.

02. Introduction: the door exchange

· Section MP3

Introduction.

Consider this exchange. A says, “I'm on my way.” B replies, “The door's open.”

Nothing in the literal meaning of B's response addresses A's statement, but the exchange makes perfect sense. We can imagine a context in which A is likely going to B's house, and B is saying A can come right in without knocking. The study of how we make such inferences, how we derive meaning beyond what is literally said, is pragmatics.

03. One utterance, three contexts

· Section MP3

Even a seemingly straightforward utterance like “It's cold” can mean radically different things depending on context. Imagine three situations.

At home, near an open window: “It's cold.” This is a request to close the window.

Tasting soup: “It's cold.” This is a complaint about the food.

At the bus stop: “It's cold.” Here the speaker is just making conversation.

The same three words can function as a request, a complaint, or an ice-breaker. The linguistic form remains constant, but the meaning shifts dramatically based on context. This is the domain of pragmatics: how context shapes meaning.

04. Meanings beyond the words

· Section MP3

But pragmatics isn't just about context. When I say “Some students arrived late”, you understand that not all students were late, even though “some” literally means “at least one, possibly all”. Similarly, if you ask “Do you have the time?”, I understand that you are asking an open question, “What time is it?”, not a closed question, “Do you or do you not know what time it is?” And when I respond “Three fifteen”, you know I mean it's currently three fifteen, not that the baseball score is three to fifteen or that the number of students in the school is three hundred and fifteen. These meanings arise not from the words themselves, but from shared assumptions about how we use language.

05. Shared assumptions and the Pat example

· Section MP3

The study of these shared assumptions forms another key aspect of pragmatics. We expect speakers to be informative but not overly so, truthful, relevant, and clear. When they appear to violate these expectations, we search for additional meaning. If someone asks “Is Pat a good teacher?” and receives the response “Pat has neat handwriting”, the apparent irrelevance triggers a search for implied meaning, in this case, that Pat is not a particularly good teacher.

06. Semantics and pragmatics

· Section MP3

So while semantics deals with the relationship between linguistic forms and their conventional meanings, pragmatics examines how context, shared knowledge, and principles of communication combine to create meaning in actual language use. For language teachers and learners, understanding pragmatics is crucial because so much of what we communicate goes beyond what we literally say.

07. Why pragmatics matters for language teaching

· Section MP3

Why pragmatics matters for language teaching.

Crucially for you, this may be the chapter where the learners' instincts are often better than the teachers'. After all, learners bring with them sophisticated pragmatic knowledge from their first language. They know that literal meanings aren't always intended meanings, that social relationships affect how we speak, and that context shapes interpretation. They've been making these kinds of pragmatic inferences their whole lives.

08. Subject dropping and complete sentences

· Section MP3

Recall the discussion of subject dropping in the section “Dropping subjects” in Chapter 12. When a student says “Got it” or “Need help?”, they're using perfectly standard English. But teachers, perhaps focused on an artificially formal register or misunderstanding what constitutes Standard English, may incorrectly mark these as errors. Similarly, the insistence on “complete sentences”, discussed in the box “Use complete sentences” in Chapter 15, often reflects teachers' misconceptions about standard usage rather than any real grammatical principle. A response like “On the table” to the question “Where's my phone?” is entirely standard. In fact, answering with a complete sentence like “Your phone is on the table” could seem oddly formal or even uncooperative.

09. Teachers' misconceptions

· Section MP3

The issue isn't always that learners are breaking rules of Standard English, but rather that teachers sometimes operate under various misconceptions: conflating formality with accuracy, idealizing the written language, and failing to appreciate that more isn't always better. This may stem from their own education, where formal academic language was prioritized, or from textbooks that present an artificially rigid version of the language. It might reflect an over-reaction against genuine student errors, such as dropping heads, determiners, and other elements required in a particular context.

10. The four strands

· Section MP3

This highlights the need for a balanced approach to language development, encompassing Paul Nation's four strands of language learning. Learners implicitly absorb pragmatic norms through meaning-focused input, that is, listening and reading authentic interactions, and test their understanding through meaning-focused output, speaking and writing in context. Language-focused learning, direct instruction on specific pragmatic features like politeness markers or speech acts, makes these often-subtle aspects explicit, while fluency development, practising known pragmatic routines until they become automatic, helps learners use them smoothly in real-time communication. Pragmatic competence, like vocabulary or grammar, develops most effectively when all four strands are nurtured rather than through exposure or isolated instruction alone.

11. What the research says about instruction

· Section MP3

Indeed, research has demonstrated that instruction in pragmatic features significantly improves learners' outcomes compared to no instruction. The research cited is a pair of meta-analyses, one by Jeon and Kaya, in 2006, and one by Ren, Li, and Lü, in 2023. For teachers, this means consciously incorporating pragmatic awareness into lesson planning rather than assuming students will simply absorb these conventions.

12. Keep this in mind

· Section MP3

Keep this in mind as we explore the various aspects of English pragmatics in this chapter. Ask yourself whether you're insisting on more elaborate forms because they're more appropriate or just because they contain the structure you want students to practice. There's nothing wrong with the latter: explicit practice of forms is often necessary. But don't confuse it with teaching appropriate language use. And don't let it lead you to mark pragmatically appropriate responses as errors just because they don't showcase the target structure.

13. Cooperation and implicature: the stock correction

· Section MP3

Cooperation and implicature.

Consider this exchange from the section “Questioning grammaticality” in Chapter 2. A child asks, “Can I go to the bathroom?” The adult replies, “I don't know, can you?”

This stock correction, which you've probably heard and may have used yourself, depends on affecting to misunderstand the child's intended meaning. The child is making a request for permission, but the adult pretends to interpret “can” as a question about ability. The adult is being uncooperative, violating the normal expectations we have about how conversation works.

14. Locating a room: two responses

· Section MP3

Here's a similarly uncooperative conversation. A asks, “Do you know where J-B one-ten is?” B replies, “Yes.”

Compare that to how most people would react. A asks, “Do you know where J-B one-ten is?” B replies, “That's building J, there. And B is for basement.”

B's response doesn't literally answer the yes-no question that was asked, but it's exactly what we expect. B understands that A is asking for directions, not conducting a survey of people's knowledge of campus buildings. This kind of inference, deriving unstated meaning from what is said, is called implicature.

15. Everyday implicatures

· Section MP3

Implicature generally works because we assume speakers are trying to be helpful. When someone says “I hear you're a good cook”, they're usually angling for an invitation. When a colleague mentions “I didn't receive your email”, they're asking you to send it again. When someone responds to “How are you?” with “I've had better days”, they're saying things aren't going well.

16. The cooperative principle and the four maxims

· Section MP3

The philosopher Paul Grice formalized these expectations into what he called the cooperative principle: speakers try to make their contributions appropriate to the conversation. This principle breaks down into four basic maxims that we unconsciously follow and expect others to follow.

One. Be appropriately informative: not too little, not too much.

Two. Be truthful.

Three. Be relevant.

Four. Be clear.

17. Implicature from apparent violations

· Section MP3

Much of what we call implicature arises when these maxims appear to be violated. If I ask “How was the movie?” and you respond “Well, the popcorn was good”, I understand you didn't enjoy the film. Your seemingly irrelevant response triggers a search for hidden meaning. Similarly, if I say “Some students passed the test”, you understand that not all students passed. If everyone had passed, saying “some” would violate the maxim of being appropriately informative.

19. The maxims describe, not prescribe

· Section MP3

Grice didn't see the maxims as prescriptive rules about how we ought to speak. Rather, he meant them as descriptions of what speakers typically do and what listeners typically expect. When we deviate from these expectations, he observed, we usually do so deliberately, with the intention of creating additional meaning.

20. Deliberate breaches

· Section MP3

Here are three cases, each labelled with the expectation it breaks.

Asked “How did you get here?”, someone answers: “I placed one foot in front of the other, repeatedly transferring my weight from the back foot to the front foot, while maintaining my balance and adjusting my trajectory as needed to avoid obstacles and ensure arrival at my intended destination.” The label is “too much information”.

Second, about a student's excuse: “Yes, and I'm the Queen of England.” The label is “not true”.

Third, asked if they liked the gift: “Well, it's Thursday today.” The label is “not relevant”.

In each case, the speaker is overtly breaching normal expectations about communication, but in a way that creates clear meaning. The absurdly detailed description of walking mocks the obvious question. The claim to be the Queen of England marks the student's excuse as equally incredible. And the complete non-sequitur about Thursday effectively communicates dislike of the gift without saying so directly.

21. Cooperation across languages

· Section MP3

Something like these principles of cooperation seems to operate in many languages, but the ways speakers flout them vary considerably. What counts as “too much information” in one culture might be “appropriately thorough” in another. What seems “admirably direct” in some contexts might seem “unnecessarily blunt” in others.

Even routine greetings may work differently. English speakers expect “How are you?” to receive only a brief, positive response. I recently heard of an interaction somebody had with a Russian in which the reply was “Fine, like everyone in Canada.”

22. An Indonesian greeting and the signalling of non-cooperation

· Section MP3

Indonesian speakers use “sudah mandi” (“have you showered yet?”) as a similar kind of greeting that requires no literal answer about bathing, something I didn't at first understand when I was in Ambon. The extent to which speakers mark their non-cooperation varies too. English speakers often use elaborate irony or sarcasm, “Oh, GREAT, that's EXACTLY what I needed right now”, while speakers of other languages might prefer more subtle signals.

23. Abduction as the engine of implicature

· Section MP3

Abduction as the engine of implicature.

The maxims help us recognize when additional meaning might be present, but they don't explain how we actually arrive at specific interpretations. When someone says “The popcorn was good” in response to a question about a movie, how do we leap from this apparent non-sequitur to “the movie was bad”? The answer lies in abduction, inference to the best explanation.

24. The popcorn example as abduction

· Section MP3

Consider again the exchange. A asks, “How was the movie?” B replies, “Well, the popcorn was good.”

The interpretive process isn't a matter of applying memorized rules. Instead, we unconsciously ask: what would make B's response sensible? We generate hypotheses. B misheard the question. B has an unusual obsession with cinema snacks. Or B is avoiding saying something negative about the film.

Given the context, the hesitation marker “well”, the assumption that B is being cooperative, and the social norm against unnecessary negativity, the third hypothesis best explains the utterance. This abductive leap from a surprising utterance to the best available explanation happens automatically and rapidly.

25. Bridging in reading and in conversation

· Section MP3

This same abductive capacity that helps us bridge gaps in written texts, in the section “Abductive bridging and coherence” in Chapter 15, operates in conversation, but with crucial differences. In reading, we typically bridge from given information to new information, inferring unstated connections between explicitly mentioned elements. In conversation, we bridge from literal meaning to speaker meaning, inferring unstated intentions behind utterances.

26. What this means for learners

· Section MP3

For language learners, this means implicature isn't fundamentally about learning new rules but about applying existing reasoning skills to new linguistic and cultural data. When a student hears “I'm on my way” followed by “The door's open”, they're not consulting a mental rulebook that says “mentions of open doors following travel announcements indicate permission to enter”. Instead, they're asking what would make this response relevant and helpful, and inferring that the speaker must be facilitating entry.

27. Transfer across languages

· Section MP3

This abductive process explains why pragmatic competence can transfer surprisingly well across languages despite surface differences. The basic cognitive machinery, the ability to reason from puzzling communicative behaviour to likely intentions, seems to be shared across languages. What varies is the cultural knowledge that constrains which explanations count as “best”. In some contexts, “Nice weather today” might be small talk; in others, it might be an indirect suggestion to go outside. The abductive process is the same; only the background assumptions differ.

28. Where explicit instruction helps

· Section MP3

Understanding implicature as abduction also clarifies where explicit instruction helps: when it provides learners with the language and cultural premises they need to generate appropriate hypotheses. Teaching that English speakers often use past-tense modals for politeness, “Could you” rather than “Can you”, isn't teaching a mechanical rule but enriching the hypothesis space learners can draw from when interpreting indirect speech.

29. Speech acts and performatives

· Section MP3

Speech acts and performatives.

Consider what happens when a judge says “I sentence you to five years”. These words don't just describe something; they do something. The act of sentencing is accomplished through the utterance itself. Or consider a priest saying “I now pronounce you husband and wife”. Again, the words themselves perform the action. These are clear examples of what J. L. Austin, writing in 1962, called performative utterances.

But speech accomplishes actions in less obvious ways too. When we say things like “I promise to return it tomorrow” or “I apologize for being late”, we're not just making statements; we're performing the acts of promising and apologizing. Even apparently simple statements like “it's cold in here” often perform actions, in this case, perhaps, requesting that someone close a window.

30. Austin's insight

· Section MP3

Austin's key insight was that all utterances perform actions, not just those with explicit performative verbs like “promise”, “apologize”, or “sentence”. When we say “the bus leaves at six”, we might be making a prediction, stating a fact, issuing a command, making a suggestion, or offering an excuse, depending on the context.

31. One speech act, three clause types

· Section MP3

The same speech act can be realized by different syntactic constructions. This becomes particularly clear when we look at requests, or directives. Consider three examples. “Please close the window” is an imperative directive. “Would you mind closing the window?” is an interrogative directive. “It's freezing in here” is a declarative directive.

Each accomplishes the same basic task, trying to get the window closed, but does so through different clause types.

33. Locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts

· Section MP3

When I say “It's cold”, I perform three kinds of act. A locutionary act: producing words with a certain meaning. An illocutionary act: making a statement, a request, and so on. And a perlocutionary act: having some effect on the hearer.

The locutionary act is simply saying that the temperature is low. The illocutionary act might be requesting that someone close a window. And the perlocutionary act would be actually getting them to close it. Of course, perlocutionary acts can fail. My hint about the temperature might be ignored. But the illocutionary act of requesting is still performed whether or not it achieves its intended effect.

34. The contrast between “I know” and “I see”

· Section MP3

These three levels of acts help explain many pragmatic misunderstandings, particularly across cultures. Consider the contrast between the English responses “I know” and “I see”. A speaker says, “The meeting is at three p.m. tomorrow.” The listener replies either “I know” or “I see”.

Both responses concern what the listener knows about the meeting time, but “I know” claims it was already known, while “I see” takes it in as new. They perform dramatically different illocutionary acts. “I know” often functions as a conversation-stopper that signals impatience or can even be perceived as rude, while “I see” acknowledges receiving valuable new information. It's not uncommon for language learners to use “I know” when they mean “I see”, creating perlocutionary effects, annoying or confusing the speaker, that they never intended. This pragmatic distinction, rarely taught explicitly, causes frequent cross-cultural misunderstandings and deserves attention.

36. Politeness and face: three ways of asking someone to move

· Section MP3

Politeness and face.

Consider these ways of asking someone to move. “Move.” That's an imperative. “Could you move, please?” That's a modal interrogative. “I wonder if you'd mind moving a bit?” That's a subordinate interrogative.

All convey basically the same message, but they differ dramatically in politeness. The imperative is direct to the point of rudeness in most contexts, while the subordinate interrogative is almost apologetic in its indirectness. Understanding these differences requires understanding the concept of face.

37. Face and face-threatening acts

· Section MP3

Face and face-threatening acts.

Face, in pragmatics, refers to our public self-image, how we want others to see us and how we see ourselves. It has two aspects. Positive face: our desire to be liked, appreciated, and approved of. Negative face: our desire for autonomy and freedom from imposition.

Many speech acts inherently threaten either positive or negative face. Requests threaten negative face by imposing on the hearer. Criticisms threaten positive face by expressing disapproval. Refusals threaten face on both sides: they deny a request, threatening the asker's positive face, while also creating social discomfort, threatening the refuser's positive face.

38. Three refusals

· Section MP3

Consider these refusals. “No.” That's a bare negative. “I'm afraid I can't.” That's an expression of regret. “I'd love to, but I'm away.” That's a positive preface plus an excuse.

The bare negative, the first example, does nothing to mitigate the face threat. Each subsequent example adds more face-saving elements: expressions of regret, positive feelings, and explanations. These structures work to preserve both the speaker's and hearer's face.

39. Politeness strategies

· Section MP3

Politeness strategies.

English speakers manage face threats in two main ways. Sometimes we try to minimize the imposition on others, which is called negative politeness, and sometimes we try to emphasize social bonds and shared goals, which is called positive politeness.

40. Negative and positive politeness between colleagues

· Section MP3

Consider this exchange between colleagues. A says, “I was wondering if you might possibly have time to look at this report?” B replies, “Let's go through it together. We can figure out what needs changing.”

A's request shows classic negative politeness: the past progressive “was wondering”, the past-tense modal “might”, the hedge about possibly having time. Each element works to minimize the imposition. It's as if A is saying, “I know this threatens your autonomy, and I'm doing everything I can to show I respect that.”

B's response, in contrast, exemplifies positive politeness. Instead of maintaining distance, B emphasizes connection and shared purpose through “let's” and “we”. By framing it as a collaborative activity rather than a favour, B reduces the face threat while strengthening social bonds.

41. Politeness across cultures

· Section MP3

Different cultures and contexts favour different mixes of these strategies. Some Asian cultures, for example, tend to use more negative politeness in professional contexts, maintaining greater distance even between close colleagues. American English speakers often use more positive politeness in professional settings, actively working to reduce distance. Neither approach is inherently more polite; they're just different ways of managing face threats.

42. Power, distance, and imposition

· Section MP3

Power, distance, and imposition.

The choice of politeness strategy depends on three main factors. Power: relative social status of speaker and hearer. Distance: degree of familiarity between participants. Imposition: how big a request or face threat is involved.

As a rough tendency, as power difference and social distance increase, so does the complexity of the request structure, even though the basic imposition remains the same.

43. Modal verbs and distance

· Section MP3

As a rough guide, the relationship between these factors can be seen in the interaction patterns of modal verbs. “Can” and “will” go with low distance and equal power. “Could” and “would” go with medium distance or a power difference. And “might”, or “wonder if you could”, go with high distance or a power difference.

44. Cross-cultural pragmatic failure

· Section MP3

Cross-cultural pragmatic failure.

When learners transfer first-language politeness strategies directly to English, it can lead to pragmatic failure. Four examples. Being too direct, as in “Give me pen”. Being too indirect, as in “If it's not too much trouble, I'm hoping that maybe”, trailing off. Using inappropriate register, as in “Yo, Professor!” And missing expected markers, as in “I want coffee” instead of “I'd like a coffee, please”.

45. Helping students with politeness

· Section MP3

Help students understand four things. Politeness norms vary by culture. Direct translation often doesn't work. Context determines appropriate forms. And practice with authentic situations helps.

46. AI and politeness practice

· Section MP3

AI conversation tools can provide safe environments for students to practice politeness strategies across different contexts without the anxiety of real-world consequences. These tools can simulate various power dynamics and social distances, giving students valuable practice with feedback.

47. Addressing the teacher

· Section MP3

Addressing the teacher.

The choice of address terms in educational settings provides a clear example of how power, distance, and imposition shape linguistic interactions. Using an inappropriate term, like “Teacher” by an adult learner, can threaten both the learner's face, by appearing childish, and potentially the teacher's face, by not using a preferred or role-appropriate term.

Consider the context of adult English language instruction in Ontario, such as LINC programs. What term should an instructor encourage adult learners to use when addressing them? Several options exist, each carrying different social meanings and potential face implications.

48. First name and “teacher”

· Section MP3

First name. In many contemporary Canadian educational contexts, particularly adult learning and increasingly in post-secondary institutions, using the instructor's first name is encouraged or is the norm. This reduces social distance, fosters a more egalitarian atmosphere, and minimizes the power differential. But this informality might conflict with the politeness norms of learners from cultures where formal address is required to show respect, potentially causing discomfort for the student.

“Teacher”. It's common for learners transferring patterns from other languages to use “teacher” as a direct address form, as in “Excuse me, teacher!”, or as an honorific with either first or last names, as in “teacher Brett”. The first is strongly associated with children's usage, making its use by adults potentially infantilizing, while the second is rarely used in English.

49. Ms. or Mr., Professor, and the best choice

· Section MP3

“Ms.” or “Mr.” plus a last name. This remains a standard, respectful form of address that clearly marks the institutional roles. It maintains a greater social distance than first names. While polite, in some adult learning environments aiming for reduced hierarchy, it might be perceived as somewhat formal or even old-fashioned compared to first-name usage, though it remains a safe default for students unsure of the instructor's preference.

“Professor”. This title is generally reserved for instructors in colleges and universities and marks a specific academic rank and role. While appropriate as a title or vocative in that context, unless the instructor prefers first names, it would typically not be used in community-based adult ESL programs like LINC.

The “best” choice is highly dependent on the specific classroom culture, the instructor's preference, which should ideally be communicated clearly, and the learners' own cultural backgrounds and comfort levels. Discussing these options helps learners understand that terms of address are not just labels but active choices that negotiate relationships and reflect social norms. Misunderstanding or misapplying these norms can lead to pragmatic failure, reinforcing the importance of context in determining appropriate language use, as in the box on cross-cultural pragmatic failure earlier in this chapter. Instructors can facilitate this by explicitly discussing address terms and establishing clear expectations suitable for an adult learning environment.

50. Context and meaning: the open door

· Section MP3

Context and meaning.

Consider what happens when someone says “The door's open”. Without more information, we have no way to know whether this is a warning about security, an invitation to enter, a complaint about a draft, an explanation for noise, or a suggestion to close it.

And yet, in actual conversation, we rarely have trouble determining which meaning is intended. When A says “I'm on my way” and B responds “The door's open”, likely, B is telling A they can walk right in. When someone gets up to investigate a noise and says “Oh, the door's open”, we know they've found the source.

51. The email exchange

· Section MP3

What's interesting isn't just that context determines meaning, which is obvious, but how quickly and reliably speakers and hearers converge on the same interpretation. Consider a longer example.

A asks, “Did you get my email?” B replies, “My laptop died.” A asks, “So you need me to send it again?” B replies, “No, I saw it on my phone. I'll respond tonight.”

B's first response isn't a simple “no”, which would be true but misleading. Nor is it a full explanation like “yes, I got it on my phone, but I haven't responded because my laptop died and I prefer to write longer emails on a proper keyboard.” Instead, B offers just one piece of relevant context, the laptop problem, trusting A to work out its implications. A proposes one interpretation, which B then corrects while filling in the rest of the story.

52. The coffee exchange

· Section MP3

This kind of collaborative meaning-making is the norm in conversation. Consider another example. A asks, “Coffee?” B replies, “I've had five cups already.” A asks, “Tea then?” B replies, “Water's fine.”

A doesn't ask, “Are you declining my offer of coffee because you've already had too much caffeine, or is there some other reason you're mentioning your coffee consumption?” The meaning is clear from context. Similarly, B doesn't spell out, “No thank you, I don't want tea either because I've already had too much caffeine today, but I would accept a non-caffeinated beverage.” Each turn provides just enough information for the other person to infer the relevant meaning.

53. Teaching pragmatic competence: context and common ground

· Section MP3

Teaching pragmatic competence.

When we interpret language, we draw on several types of context: linguistic, meaning the surrounding text; physical, the immediate environment; social, relationships and roles; and cognitive, shared knowledge. This context-sensitivity is crucial for pragmatic competence.

Successful communication depends on what we call common ground: the knowledge, beliefs, and assumptions shared by speaker and hearer. When pragmatic expectations aren't met, communication can break down through pragmalinguistic failure, using language that doesn't convey the intended meaning, or sociopragmatic failure, violating social norms.

54. Why pragmatic failure is serious

· Section MP3

For language learners, pragmatic failure can be more serious than grammatical errors, for four reasons. Grammar errors are usually recognized as learning errors. Pragmatic errors may be interpreted as rudeness. Feedback on pragmatic errors is often indirect or absent. And patterns from the first language can be deeply ingrained.

55. Explicit and implicit instruction

· Section MP3

While earlier findings, such as those of Jeon and Kaya, often suggested a distinct advantage for explicit instruction, more recent large-scale analysis, by Ren, Li, and Lü, indicates that the difference compared to well-designed implicit instruction may not always be statistically significant, highlighting the effectiveness of various focused pedagogical techniques. Whether you lean more on explicit or implicit pragmatic instruction, you should approach the topic intentionally. The ideas presented in the chapter should provide you with a solid base from which to build.

56. Duration of instruction

· Section MP3

Beyond the type of instruction, research syntheses indicate other factors also influence pragmatic learning. Notably, the duration of instruction appears crucial; a recent meta-analysis suggests that longer periods of focused instruction tend to yield greater gains in pragmatic competence compared to shorter interventions, reinforcing the benefit of sustained attention rather than brief, isolated lessons. As always, the opportunity costs of time spent should be considered, as discussed in the section on the frequency blindness problem in Chapter 8.

57. Learner characteristics

· Section MP3

Learner characteristics also play a role. The biggest gains show up in foreign language settings, where classroom input is paramount.

58. Measurement and the limits of the research

· Section MP3

How learning is measured can affect the observed outcomes; for instance, gains might appear larger on written tasks compared to more complex oral production tasks. It's also important to recognize that these meta-analyses have limitations, often constrained by the number and methodological rigour of available primary studies, and frequently focusing on specific pragmatic features like speech acts. So, while research clearly supports intentional pragmatics teaching, applying these findings requires considering the specific learners, context, duration, and assessment methods involved.

59. Using AI to make pragmatics visible

· Section MP3

Using AI to make pragmatics visible.

One of the challenges in teaching pragmatics has always been making implicit norms explicit. Conversational AI tools offer unique advantages in this regard.

60. Controlled variation

· Section MP3

First, controlled variation: AI can demonstrate how the same basic message changes under different conditions of power, distance, and imposition. For example, showing students how a request transforms across contexts. To a close friend: “Close the window?” That's direct, with minimal politeness. To a classmate: “Could you close the window, please?” That's conventional politeness. To a professor: “I'm sorry to trouble you, but could I ask you to close the window?” That shows high deference.

61. Simulated consequences and analytical explanation

· Section MP3

Second, simulated consequences: AI can respond differently to pragmatically appropriate versus inappropriate utterances, providing immediate feedback without real-world social costs.

Third, analytical explanation: unlike human interlocutors who may simply follow pragmatic norms unconsciously, AI can often explicitly identify the implicit rules being followed or broken and explain them to learners.

62. AI as a supplement

· Section MP3

For teachers, AI tools can help illuminate pragmatic patterns that might otherwise remain invisible. By analysing language samples and highlighting pragmatic features, such as hedges, politeness markers, and indirectness strategies, AI can make these patterns more salient for both teachers and students.

The most effective approach integrates AI as a supplement to, not a replacement for, human interaction. After all, the ultimate goal is successful communication with other people, not with machines. But AI's ability to make explicit what is usually implicit offers a valuable window into the normally hidden workings of pragmatic norms.

63. Summary

· Section MP3

Summary.

Pragmatics is where grammar meets the world. A student who can produce a grammatically perfect request may still offend, and a student who says almost nothing may communicate exactly what's needed. The Cooperative Principle, speech acts, and face give you a vocabulary for talking about these successes and failures, and for recognizing that what counts as polite, direct, or appropriate shifts with every classroom, culture, and conversation.
