§11.3.1, last paragraph. The claim that “most of the negative modal auxiliary verbs are like can’t” isn’t supported. Shouldn’t patterns with mustn’t: in You shouldn’t do it, the advice is not to do it, so the modal sits outside the negation. Others, like can’t, have their modal meaning within the scope of negation. The pattern varies from modal to modal.

See Huddleston & Pullum (2002), The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language, Ch. 3, §9.10, p. 204: “There is a general tendency for strong or medium modals to take internal negation, and for weak modals to take external negation”, with He mustn’t/shouldn’t go with them as internal and He can’t go with them as external.

§17.2.1.1, last paragraph before the exercise. Replace “Wilson & Wilson (2005) found that” with a description of their proposal. §17.2.2, first paragraph. Qualify “just one cultural model of how talk should be organized”. Stivers et al. (2009) compared the timing of responses to polar (yes-no) questions in informal conversation in ten languages from five continents. Speakers of every language avoided overlapping talk and kept silence between turns short, and the languages’ mean response times fell within about a quarter of a second of the cross-language mean. Tempo differs across languages, but the avoidance of overlap and of long gaps was found in all ten.

See Stivers et al. (2009), “Universals and cultural variation in turn-taking in conversation”, PNAS 106(26): 10587–10592, abstract (p. 10587) and Results (p. 10588).
