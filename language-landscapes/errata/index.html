§4.3.2, Test 1, example (3b). Remove the asterisk from just carefully: the phrase is possible when just has a limiting or focusing meaning. The modification test must hold the modifier's meaning constant; it doesn't show that adverbs reject every use of the listed modifiers.
82

§4.3.2, Test 3, example (10b). Come before lunch or quickly shouldn't be marked unconditionally ungrammatical. Its acceptability depends on context. Phrases of different categories can coordinate when their functions and meanings permit it, so coordination alone doesn't establish category identity.
83

§4.3.2, Test 4, example (12d). Remove the asterisk from the people locally, which can be acceptable in context. AdvPs have a more restricted distribution as noun modifiers than PPs, but this example doesn't establish a categorical prohibition. See also the changes globally in footnote 2.
91

§4.3.4, “In in English and Italian”. The generalization that Italian uses su for small vehicles and in for large ones is incorrect. Italian in macchina and in treno correspond to English by car and by train. For being on a particular train, Italian can use sul treno or nel treno. The choice depends on the construction and whether a particular vehicle is specified.

See Treccani's explanation of in treno, sul treno, and nel treno.

§4.3.6, final sentence. “Such trees are the focus of the next chapter” should read “Such trees are the focus of Chapter 6”.

Exercises, Part B4 and its answer. Specify that likely is being considered in the certainty use listed under adverbs in §4.2 (p. 75). The intended answer is adv, but the isolated word also has adjective uses. Rejection of the intended right modifier alone doesn't distinguish an adverb from an adjective.

Answer key, Part A5. The question asks for all adverbs and prepositions in Is he in yet, or still out? The complete answer is: in prep; yet adv; still adv; out prep.

Answer key, Sample Part C3. Replace here, now, upstairs with again, never, thus. The question asks for three adverbs that can't be modified by very; the printed answers are prepositions under the chapter's analysis.
105

§5.4, paragraph before (21) and example (21a). The intended distinction is lexical versus auxiliary, so “not modal” should read “lexical rather than auxiliary”. However, Don’t be late doesn’t establish that be is lexical: negative imperatives allow do with auxiliary be. Use Why don’t you be more tolerant? to illustrate lexical be, and retain the imperative as an exception to the usual diagnostic.

See Huddleston & Pullum (2002), The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language, §2.3, p. 105, and §2.5.7, pp. 113–114.
107

§5.5, introduction to (28). Replace the claim of “seven distinct tenses” with a description of the eight listed tense–aspect examples. The list includes habitual wa-wat ‘usually go’, and footnote 5 already notes that the forms combine tense and aspect. The number of examples isn’t a count of distinct tenses.
108

Exercise, first paragraph. Replace the claim that French speakers don’t care about the distinction between I do it and I’m doing it with a statement about conventional marking: French often uses je le fais where English distinguishes those forms; Je suis en train de le faire makes the ongoing interpretation explicit. The grammatical contrast doesn’t establish what speakers care about.
108

Exercise, second paragraph. Identify the variety as Cuzco Quechua and remove the claim that every assertion must mark its information source. Assertions without an evidential are possible, so an unmarked assertion isn’t necessarily incomplete or misleading.

See Martina T. Faller (2002), Semantics and pragmatics of evidentials in Cuzco Quechua, pp. 23–24 and 145.
109

§5.6, example (29b). Replace I would walk to school yesterday with I would walk to school, introduced as a past routine. The original is an awkward standalone illustration of habitual would. The form would remains past tense in the chapter’s analysis.
111–112

§5.7.1, discussion of (36). Remove the restriction of I’m living in Mississauga to someone such as an international student. Other speakers can use the progressive to present their residence as temporary. The choice concerns how the speaker portrays the situation.
112–113

§5.7.1.1, examples (37)–(38). Qualify the grammaticality judgments by variety and intended interpretation. Footnote 7 already records the use of I’m knowing that feeling in South Asian English. The progressive examples in (38) are compared with ordinary general statements; specially supplied temporary readings can change acceptability.
113

§5.7.1.1–2, discussion of (39) and repeated events. Present the relevance of time-limitedness as the chapter’s account, without requiring an impending end to matter in every use. We’re having dinner, He’s being grumpy, and It’s tasting sweeter can describe an activity, temporary behaviour, and a developing or contextually limited experience. The gym-membership and commitment scenarios are illustrations, rather than necessary conditions for the progressive.
114

§5.7.1.2, restaurant example. I’m eating at that new restaurant can describe either a meal in progress or a current pattern of visits. Being inside or outside the restaurant can provide a contextual clue but doesn’t determine the interpretation.
114

§5.7.1.3, discussion of (40). Remove the categorical requirement that the dinner must already have been arranged and the Smiths invited. The progressive can present an event as part of a current plan; it can also announce a plan. The amount of prior arrangement required depends on context.
118

“No progressive perfect” box. Present the explanation in terms of the relevance of an endpoint as this chapter’s analysis. Keep that explanation distinct from the grammatical observation: I’ve been trying is possible, while I’m having tried is ungrammatical.
119

§5.8, introduction to (43). It’ll work and It should work compare different modal verbs, rather than the present and past forms of one verb. Table 5.5 pairs should with shall. Keep (43) as a comparison of likelihood and use the will/would pair in (44) to illustrate tense-related remoteness.
119–120

§5.8, example (45) and its introduction. Treat the “intimate” and “public” labels as contextual illustrations, rather than fixed divisions between interlocutors. Would can make a request more tentative or polite; the effect depends on context and intonation.
121

§5.8.1, examples (49)–(50). I am reading a book doesn’t entail reaching the end: replace “until the end” with “progressive”. The book was read describes a passive construction and can report an event: replace “resultant/state” with “passive”. These meanings belong to constructions in context, rather than following from participle forms alone. In noun modification, participle-shaped words such as sleeping and broken can also be adjectives; their shape and meaning don’t settle the category.
121

Exercise, Part 1. In (a), extend the bold target from have been to have been trying, so the target includes the full perfect-progressive construction. In (b), retain have completed and allow “no tense” as an answer: this infinitival target is non-tensed, and should is outside it. The instructions should ask for each target’s tense, if any, and aspect.

Exercise, Part 3(c). Replace The broken vase lay on the floor with The book was read, already used in (49c). The exercise asks for non-tensed verb forms, but broken in the original allows an adjective analysis. The replacement supplies a clear verbal past participle.
